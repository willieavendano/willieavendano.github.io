# Method development plan (advisor draft, 2026-11-02)

Prepared from the student's proposal with research-agent support. Everything here is a starting point for the student to adopt, adapt, or argue against. Citations should be verified against the originals before they enter the paper.

## 1. The research question as written

> How does David Lynch's use of recurring visual motifs and symbolism to portray female agency evolve across his feature films?

Problems, in order of severity:

- **"Evolve" presumes directional change.** QCA can show *difference* between films; it cannot show *evolution* unless a trajectory is defined in advance (which invites a progress narrative). Use "differ" or "change," and let "evolution" be a possible finding, not a premise.
- **"Female agency" is undefined and is not the same as "feminist themes."** Agency is a property of a character (capacity to act, choose, resist within the story); feminist themes are a property of the film's politics. Pick agency, define it, and treat the feminist reading as the literature you argue with, not as a code.
- **"Motif" vs. "symbolism."** A motif is a *repeated observable element* (red curtain, mirror, flickering lamp): codable. A symbol is an *interpretation* of what the element means: the analysis, not the data. Code motifs; argue symbolism. Conflating them makes the coding frame unfalsifiable.
- **"His feature films"** is ten films; the study uses four. Say so in the question.

Rewordings:

1. *How do recurring visual motifs associated with female characters differ in their relationship to those characters' agency across four David Lynch films (1986–2001)?* **(recommended)**
2. *In what ways does the framing, lighting, and mirror/doubling imagery around central female characters construct or constrain their agency in Blue Velvet, Fire Walk with Me, Lost Highway, and Mulholland Drive?* (sharper, but pre-commits to three motifs before the pilot)
3. *Does the visual coding of female agency in Lynch's films change between 1986 and 2001, and if so, how?* (answerable but reads as closed)

**Corpus logic.** The four films share a defensible feature: each centers a woman in peril whose identity is doubled or split (Dorothy/Sandy; Laura/Donna, Laura/Maddy by extension; Renee/Alice; Betty/Diane and Rita/Camilla). State that inclusion criterion explicitly, with a sentence on each exclusion: *Eraserhead*, *The Elephant Man*, *Dune*, *The Straight Story* (no central female protagonist); *Wild at Heart* (Lula is central, but the structure is a road romance without the doubling; this is the weakest exclusion and a reader will ask, so name it as a limitation); *Inland Empire* (fits the criterion; excluded for feasibility: three hours, digital video changes lighting/color coding, and it pushes the corpus past what one coder can handle). Decide now: **Inland Empire is out.** "If I feel like I need one more" is scope creep in writing.

## 2. Method identity

What the proposal describes (a priori categories from literature, applied systematically to segmented material, with a pilot and consistency checks, interpretation preserved) is **qualitative content analysis in the Schreier/Mayring tradition**, not Braun & Clarke thematic analysis. Film scholars would call the data-gathering layer **formal analysis** (Bordwell & Thompson's mise-en-scène and cinematography vocabulary).

Precise label: **"Qualitative content analysis (Schreier, 2012) of a purposive film corpus, using a deductive-inductive coding frame whose observable indicators are drawn from formal film analysis (Bordwell & Thompson)."**

Required citations:

- Schreier, M. (2012). *Qualitative Content Analysis in Practice.* SAGE. (coding frame, segmentation, pilot, trial coding, consistency)
- Mayring, P. (2014). *Qualitative Content Analysis: Theoretical Foundation, Basic Procedures and Software Solution.* Open access via SSOAR. (deductive category application; free PDF)
- Bordwell, D., & Thompson, K. *Film Art: An Introduction* (10th–13th ed.). (vocabulary for indicators: framing, shot scale, lighting key, color, blocking, point of view)
- Optional, for the "why not thematic analysis" sentence: Vaismoradi, Turunen & Bondas (2013), *Nursing & Health Sciences* 15(3), 398–405.

**Coaching note for the advisor.** Thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) is inductive and interpretive: codes emerge from reading, themes are constructed by the researcher, and the reflexive version explicitly rejects reliability coding. QCA is rule-bound: build a coding frame with defined categories and mutually exclusive subcategories, segment the material first, code every unit, pilot the frame on a subsample, revise, then do the main coding and check stability. The student's instinct ("consistent coding framework," "systematic comparison") is QCA. Formal film analysis is what she does *inside* each unit to decide which code applies: the measurement instrument, not the method. The paper should say this in one sentence.

## 3. Step-by-step plan, Nov 2026 – Apr 2027

| Phase | Weeks | Work | Deliverable |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Define & read** | Nov 2 – Nov 29 (4 wk) | Lit review on (a) Lynch & gender (Nochimson 1997; McGowan 2007; Mulvey 1975), (b) QCA method, (c) formal film vocabulary. Draft operational definitions table. | Annotated bibliography (12–15 sources); v0 definitions table; final RQ |
| **B. Coding frame v1** | Nov 30 – Dec 19 (3 wk) | Deductive codes from literature. Build spreadsheet. Pin editions. Segment one film (Blue Velvet) into scenes using a written segmentation rule. | Codebook v1; scene list for BV |
| **C. Pilot** | Jan 4 – Jan 22 (3 wk) | Code all BV scenes meeting the sampling rule. Add inductive codes. Keep a revision log (date, change, reason). Second coder codes 10 BV scenes. | Codebook v2; revision log; pilot agreement figure |
| **D. Main coding** | Jan 25 – Mar 5 (6 wk) | FWWM, LH, MD at ~2 weeks each: segment, code, within-film memo. No codebook changes after this point except logged clarifications. | Four coded datasets; four within-film memos |
| **E. Reliability + cross-film** | Mar 8 – Mar 19 (2 wk) | Second coder on 10–15% subsample across all four films. Build film × motif × agency matrix. Negative-case pass. | Agreement table; matrix; negative-case memo |
| **F. Write** | Mar 22 – Apr 23 (5 wk) | Draft, two advisor passes, one film/English-teacher read, format. | Paper submitted ~Apr 26, buffer to Apr 30 |

**Operational definitions table** (columns): *Concept → Observable indicator → Include if → Exclude if → Example (film, timestamp)*. E.g., "Mirror/doubling motif → a reflective surface or second figure visually paired with the female character in the same frame or by match cut → include: character sees or is seen in reflection, split-screen doubling, physical double → exclude: incidental background reflections not in focus → *Lost Highway* 00:41:12 (verify)." Do the same for each agency dimension: *autonomy* (character initiates action the plot follows), *resistance* (acts against another's stated will), *vulnerability* (framed as object of threat; a representation code, not a deficit), *control* (another character directs her movement or speech). Each definition must be decidable by a second person from the frame plus immediate narrative context.

**Deductive vs. inductive.** Start with codes the literature already names (gaze and point-of-view structure from Mulvey; doubling and the "two women" from Nochimson/McGowan; red/blue color, curtain, lamp flicker, close-up on the face from Lynch criticism). Expect the pilot to add 3–6 codes (e.g., "singing/performance as voice," "phone call as mediated agency"). Mark each code D or I in the codebook.

**Unit of analysis: the scene**, defined as continuous action in one location and time, using a written rule (new scene when location *or* time changes, confirmed by a cut). Shots are too many (Blue Velvet alone is 1,000+) and too atomistic for "agency," which is an action-level concept; sequences are too interpretive to segment reliably. Scene is also what a second coder can check.

**Sampling rule (prevents cherry-picking):** segment the *entire* film into numbered scenes; code **every scene in which a named female character is onscreen for ≥15 seconds or speaks**. Record excluded scenes with the reason. No "relevant scenes" language: relevance is decided by the rule, not by the coder.

**Pilot:** Blue Velvet only, full pass. Revision log is a dated table. The paper's method section quotes two or three log entries as evidence of refinement.

**Reliability:** a second coder (a peer in the class or the advisor) independently codes 10 scenes in the pilot and 10–15% of scenes across the corpus later, using only the codebook. Report **percent agreement per code** and a half-page discussing every disagreement and how it was resolved (codebook edit vs. judgment). Cohen's kappa is defensible (cite McHugh 2012, *Biochemia Medica* 22(3), 276–282, for interpretation), but with 30–40 scenes and many codes it will be unstable and hard to defend orally. **Recommend percent agreement + discussion**; kappa optional on the two highest-frequency codes only.

**Audit trail:** one folder, versioned: codebook v1/v2/v3, revision log, scene lists, coded sheets, memos, agreement sheets. Cite Lincoln & Guba (1985) *Naturalistic Inquiry* for the term.

**Negative-case analysis:** after the matrix is built, deliberately search for scenes that contradict the emerging pattern (high-autonomy scenes in the earliest film, high-control scenes in the latest). Write them up. This is the best defense against the progress-narrative charge.

**Systematic cross-film comparison:** (1) within-film memo per film: which motifs co-occur with which agency dimensions, with counts *and* exemplar scenes; (2) a **film × motif × agency-dimension matrix** (counts of scenes, plus proportion of coded scenes); (3) cross-film reading of the matrix, written in terms of *differences*, not *development*; (4) only then, a discussion section that asks whether a trajectory exists. The order matters: the matrix comes before the story.

## 4. Tools

- **Spreadsheet (Google Sheets/Excel) is sufficient and recommended.** Columns: `film | edition | scene_id | start_tc | end_tc | duration_s | female_chars_present | meets_sampling_rule (Y/N + reason) | motif codes (one column per motif, 0/1) | agency codes (one column per dimension, 0/1) | pov_holder | brief_description | frame_grab_filename | memo | coder | date`. Tab 2: codebook. Tab 3: revision log. Tab 4: agreement check. One row per scene keeps the matrix a pivot table away.
- **QDA software:** only for coding text (transcripts, memos). Taguette (free) and QualCoder (free, handles video with timestamps) are fine; MAXQDA/ATLAS.ti student licenses are overkill. NVivo no. For four films with 150–250 coded scenes total, the spreadsheet wins on auditability and in the oral defense ("show me your data").
- **Timestamps/frames:** pin one edition per film and name it in the paper (Criterion for all four). Timestamps are relative to that edition's main feature. VLC: Video → Take Snapshot (set folder and filename pattern in Preferences); name files `BV_0412_mirror.png` (film, scene, motif).
- **Zotero** with the Zotero Connector; one collection per film plus "method."

## 5. Legal and access

Legitimate access: purchased Criterion discs (library may own them; check interlibrary loan), Criterion Channel subscription, or library streaming (Kanopy carries several Lynch titles through many public libraries). Frame grabs used in a criticism paper with attribution are a standard fair-use case (purpose: criticism/scholarship; amount: single stills; effect on market: none); keep them small, caption with film, year, timestamp, edition. Do **not** copy or store film files, rip discs, or share clips; grabs only. Nothing goes into a shared or public repository except the spreadsheet and the paper.

## 6. AP Research rubric alignment (Academic Paper rubric)

- **Understand and Analyze Context:** the gap statement (prior Lynch-and-gender criticism is interpretive and film-by-film; no systematic cross-film coding exists). Corpus justification lives here too.
- **Understand and Analyze Argument / Evaluate Sources and Evidence:** engaging Nochimson vs. the misogyny critique vs. McGowan as competing positions, not a list.
- **Research Design:** the codebook, sampling rule, unit of analysis, pilot, reliability check, audit trail. This row is where a STEM advisor's strengths earn points; aim for "replicable by a reader with the codebook."
- **Establish Argument:** the matrix → claim chain; negative cases addressed; conclusion scoped to the four films.
- **Select and Use Evidence:** frame grabs plus counts, each exemplar tied to a scene_id.
- **Engage Audience / Apply Conventions:** consistent citation, labeled figures, defined terms.
- **Limitations** (scored within Design/Argument): single primary coder, four films, interpretive dependence on narrative context, edition-specific timestamps.

Five things the reader will look for: (1) Can I tell exactly which scenes were coded and why those? (2) Could I apply the codebook to a scene myself? (3) Is there evidence the frame was tested and revised? (4) Does the conclusion claim change *within* the corpus rather than "Lynch evolved"? (5) Are disconfirming scenes discussed?

## 7. Top risks and mitigations

1. **Inland Empire creep** → corpus fixed in writing by Nov 29; exclusion sentence drafted now.
2. **Interpretation drift across films** (coding gets looser by film four) → codebook frozen after pilot; re-code 5 BV scenes blind in March and compare to January.
3. **Progress-narrative confirmation bias** → RQ says "differ"; negative-case pass is mandatory; matrix precedes narrative.
4. **Agency definitions too abstract to code** → every definition needs an exclude rule and a timestamped example before pilot; the second coder's confusion is the test.
5. **Advisor is not a film scholar** → recruit an English/film teacher as reader for the lit review and one draft; advisor owns the design rows.
6. **Time: ~9 hours × 3 viewings ≈ 27 hours plus coding** → first viewing is segmentation only; second is coding; third is targeted re-check of flagged scenes. Budget 10–12 hours per film all-in.
7. **Second coder unavailable or untrained** → identify by January; a 30-minute codebook walkthrough and two practice scenes before independent coding.
8. **Data loss / untraceable claims** → one cloud folder, dated versions, every exemplar in the paper carries scene_id + timestamp + edition.
