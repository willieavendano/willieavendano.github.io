This week

The question

As proposedHow does David Lynch's use of recurring visual motifs and symbolism to portray female agency evolve across his feature films?

Four things in that sentence will cost points if they stay:

  • "Evolve" presumes directional change. Content analysis can show difference between films. "Evolution" is a possible finding, not a premise; building it in invites a progress narrative.
  • "Female agency" is undefined, and it is not "feminist themes." Agency is a property of a character (the capacity to act, choose, or resist inside the story). Feminist themes are a property of the film's politics. Pick agency, define it, and treat the feminist readings as the literature you argue with.
  • Motif is not symbol. A motif is a repeated observable element (mirror, red curtain, flickering lamp): codable. A symbol is an interpretation of what it means: the analysis. Code motifs; argue symbolism.
  • "His feature films" is ten films. The study uses four. Say so.

RecommendedHow do recurring visual motifs associated with female characters differ in their relationship to those characters' agency across four David Lynch films (1986–2001)?

Alternative (sharper, but pre-commits to three motifs before the pilot)In what ways do framing, lighting, and mirror/doubling imagery around central female characters construct or constrain their agency in Blue Velvet, Fire Walk with Me, Lost Highway, and Mulholland Drive?

Our thoughts

  • KeepThe design. A coding frame applied consistently across a fixed corpus is more rigorous than most AP Research humanities proposals, and it fits a real gap: Lynch-and-women is heavily written, but almost entirely as single-film psychoanalytic readings. Only Schaffner (2009) and Braziel (2004) compare across films, and neither uses an explicit coding frame or tracks motifs like mirrors, lighting, and color against defined agency categories.
  • Name itCall the method what it is. "Qualitative content analysis (Schreier, 2012) of a purposive film corpus, using a deductive-inductive coding frame whose observable indicators come from formal film analysis (Bordwell & Thompson)." Not thematic analysis: Braun & Clarke's version is inductive and rejects reliability coding; yours is rule-bound, piloted, and checked. Formal film analysis is the measuring instrument inside each scene, not the method.
  • FixThe real risk is not film theory. It is cherry-picking and drift. Four things close it: a written sampling rule that decides which scenes get coded (not "relevant scenes"), a codebook frozen after the pilot, a second coder on a 10–15% subsample, and a deliberate hunt for scenes that contradict the pattern.
  • OrderThe matrix comes before the story. Build the film × motif × agency table first, write each within-film memo, then read across films in terms of differences. Only after that ask whether a trajectory exists. Doing it in the other order is how progress narratives get written.
  • DropSentiment analysis of "feminism." Right instinct, wrong tool. Polarity is not agency (Laura screaming "no" is maximally negative and maximally resistant), it reads text when the question is about images, Lynch's deadpan irony breaks lexicon and transformer models alike, and four films of thin dialogue is too small a corpus. Full reasoning in the computational strand.
  • InsteadQuantitize your own coding. Once the scene × code spreadsheet exists, per-film code frequencies and a four-panel motif × agency heatmap are the cross-film comparison in one figure. It is methodologically coherent (Sandelowski et al. 2009), cheap, and scores well on Research Design. One optional stretch strand beyond that, never two.
  • RecruitGet a film or English teacher as second reader. Willie owns the design rows (sampling, codebook, reliability, audit trail). The literature review and the interpretive argument need a reader who knows Mulvey from Nochimson.

Method kit

Unit of analysis: the scene

Continuous action in one place and time. Written rule: a new scene begins when location or time changes, confirmed by a cut. Shots are too many (Blue Velvet alone has 1,000+) and too small to carry "agency," which is an action-level idea. Sequences are too interpretive to segment reliably. A scene is also what a second coder can check.

Sampling rule (no cherry-picking)

Segment the entire film into numbered scenes. Code every scene in which a named female character is onscreen for 15 seconds or more, or speaks. Log excluded scenes with the reason. Relevance is decided by the rule, not by you.

Pilot

Blue Velvet, full pass. Keep a dated revision log (date, change, reason). The method section of the paper quotes two or three log entries as evidence the frame was tested and revised.

Reliability

A second coder (a classmate or Willie) codes 10 scenes during the pilot and 10–15% of scenes across all four films later, using only the codebook. Report percent agreement per code plus a half-page on every disagreement and how it was resolved (codebook edit vs. judgment call). Cohen's kappa is optional on the two most frequent codes only (McHugh 2012 for interpretation); with 30–40 scenes it is unstable and hard to defend orally.

Audit trail and negative cases

One versioned folder: codebook v1/v2/v3, revision log, scene lists, coded sheets, memos, agreement sheets (Lincoln & Guba 1985 for the term). After the matrix exists, go looking for scenes that break the pattern: high-autonomy scenes in the earliest film, high-control scenes in the latest. Write them up. This is the best defense against the progress-narrative charge.

Operational definitions (starter rows; the pilot fills the examples)

Each definition must be decidable by a second person from the frame plus immediate story context. Mark each code D (deductive, from literature) or I (inductive, added in the pilot). Expect the pilot to add three to six codes (singing or performance as voice, the telephone as mediated agency).

Computational strand

Why "sentiment analysis of feminism" fails as stated

  • Wrong construct. Sentiment tools output positive/negative polarity. Polarity is not feminism and is not agency. Laura screaming "no" is maximally negative and maximally resistant; Dorothy's quiet compliance scores neutral and codes near zero on autonomy.
  • Wrong modality. The question is about images. Sentiment reads text. A reader will ask why dialogue polarity is evidence about mirrors, and the rubric row on method alignment has no good answer.
  • Lynch breaks the tools. Sparse, deadpan, ironic dialogue defeats lexicon methods (VADER, NRC) by design, and transformer classifiers remain unreliable on narrative irony.
  • Tiny corpus. Four films of thin dialogue is a few thousand lines. No per-film average would be defensible.

Do this: quantitize the coding

The scene × code spreadsheet is already the best dataset in the project. Counting, cross-tabulating, and visualizing your own codes is coherent because the numbers inherit the qualitative definitions rather than importing a foreign construct (Sandelowski, Voils & Knafl 2009, "On Quantitizing"). Normalize within film, since scene counts differ. No-code route: a Google Sheets pivot (rows = motif, columns = agency code, filter = film) with conditional formatting as a heatmap. Code route:

import pandas as pd, seaborn as sns, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
df = pd.read_csv("coding.csv")                     # one row per scene x motif x agency code
co = df.groupby(["film", "motif", "agency_dim"]).size().unstack(fill_value=0)
films = ["Blue Velvet", "Fire Walk with Me", "Lost Highway", "Mulholland Drive"]
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(16, 4), sharey=True)
for ax, f in zip(axes, films):
    sns.heatmap(co.loc[f].div(co.loc[f].values.sum()), ax=ax, cmap="mako",
                vmin=0, vmax=0.25, annot=True, fmt=".2f", cbar=(f == films[-1]))
    ax.set_title(f)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig("motif_x_agency.png", dpi=200)

Optional stretch: pick one, or none

Text: agency verbs in screenplay action lines. Sap et al., "Connotation Frames of Power and Agency in Modern Films" (EMNLP 2017), labels ~2,000 verbs for whether the subject holds agency and power; the lexicon ships as agency_power.csv inside the Riveter package on GitHub. Run it on action lines ("Dorothy grabs the knife," "Laura is dragged"), which describe the visual track and are already attributed to characters, not on dialogue. Caveats: the Lost Highway screenplay (Faber 1997) is the only published text; Blue Velvet and FWWM circulate as drafts; the Mulholland Drive script online is the 1999 TV pilot, not the feature. Disclose the draft-vs-film gap.

Image: luminance and palette. Sample one frame per second with ffmpeg, compute mean luminance and a five-color k-means palette per scene (OpenCV, scikit-learn), join to scene_id. PySceneDetect gives shot boundaries and shot-length statistics (expect false cuts on dissolves and strobe). It can show that scenes coded low-key are measurably darker, which triangulates the lighting code. It cannot say anything about agency. Luminance is not meaning. About a day of Willie's time to set up.

Limitations paragraph, whichever strand is used. Name the construct gap (verb agency or luminance is a proxy, not the thing), the source gap (drafts differ from films; subtitles lack speakers), the tool gap (a lexicon built on mainstream screenplays; a cut detector that misses dissolves), and the sample (n = 4 films, so descriptive, not inferential). The computational strand triangulates the qualitative reading. It never replaces it.

Tools, all free, all fine on a MacBook: Python 3.11+, pandas, matplotlib, seaborn, spaCy, PySceneDetect, ffmpeg, OpenCV, scikit-learn; Voyant Tools and Orange Data Mining for no-code exploration. Full write-up in 03-methods/computational-strand.md.
