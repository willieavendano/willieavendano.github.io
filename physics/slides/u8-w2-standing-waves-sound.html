Where We Are

Last week, waves traveled. This week, patterns lock in.

Unit 8 began with wavelength, frequency, wave speed, superposition, and interference. Now we use those ideas to explain a wave pattern that stays in place:

  • Standing waves — fixed nodes and antinodes
  • Harmonics — allowed patterns on a string
  • Sound — pressure waves in air
  • Resonance — large response at a natural frequency

Same wave model. New boundary conditions.

Make the continuity explicit. Nothing about standing waves replaces the Week 1 model: the pattern comes from superposition of waves. The new ingredient is boundary conditions — what the ends of the string or tube are allowed to do. Students often treat harmonics as a new list to memorize; keep returning to "which pattern fits the boundary?"

Act I

Standing Waves

Interference that holds a shape in place

Use this divider as the conceptual reset. A standing wave is not a wave that stopped carrying energy; it is a stable interference pattern made by two waves of the same frequency and amplitude traveling in opposite directions. Keep the distinction between the pattern and the individual traveling waves alive.

Standing Waves · 1 of 3

Nodes stay still. Antinodes move the most.

A standing wave has locations where destructive interference is permanent and locations where constructive interference is permanent.

N
Node
A point with zero displacement at all times. The string does not move there.
A
Antinode
A point with maximum displacement. The string oscillates most there.
Read the pattern

Adjacent nodes are separated by λ/2 m. A node to the nearest antinode is separated by λ/4 m.

Ask students to identify nodes before naming any harmonic number. The most common failure is counting loops instead of locating the zero-motion points. Physically mark a rope with tape if available: nodes are the points that never leave the tape line; antinodes are the widest excursions.

Standing Waves · 2 of 3

A string fixed at both ends must have nodes at both ends.

The endpoint condition controls which wavelengths can fit. For a string of length L m fixed at both ends:

n = 1
fundamental
One half-wavelength fits: L m = λ/2 m.
n = 2
second harmonic
One full wavelength fits: L m = λ m.
n = 3
third harmonic
Three half-wavelengths fit: L m = 3λ/2 m.

Every allowed pattern begins and ends on a node.

Standing Waves · 3 of 3

Harmonic number counts the allowed pattern.

For a string fixed at both ends

L m = nλn m / 2

fn Hz = n f1 Hz

  • n = 1 is the fundamental frequency
  • n = 2, 3, 4, … are higher harmonics
  • Higher n means shorter wavelength
  • Higher n means higher frequency
Keep the two equations conceptually separate. The length equation selects the wavelength pattern; the frequency equation tells how the allowed frequencies are spaced. Students often think "third harmonic" means three times the wavelength — it means three times the fundamental frequency and three half-waves across the string.

Quick Check · Whiteboard · 4 minutes

Which pattern belongs on the string?

Sketch, then justify
  1. Draw the second harmonic for a string fixed at both ends. Mark every node and antinode.
  2. A pattern has four antinodes. What harmonic number is it?
  3. For a fixed string, does the fourth harmonic have a longer or shorter wavelength than the fundamental?

Do not calculate first. Count loops, then connect the pattern to n.

This is a visual formative check, not an answer-key slide. Listen for students saying "four nodes" when they mean four loops. The endpoint nodes are included in the count of nodes but not in the count of antinodes. Ask students to explain their answer with boundary conditions, not just a memorized picture.

KDEA in Action

From a string to a frequency

Same protocol. New wave model.

This is the bridge into the worked example. Emphasize that the equation is not the solution; the model selection is part of E. Students need to identify the boundary condition, choose the string relation, and then solve algebraically with units traveling through every line.

Worked Example · Setup

Find the third-harmonic frequency

A guitar string is fixed at both ends. Its length is 0.80 m, and waves travel along it at 120 m/s. What is the frequency of the third harmonic?

Before the table
  1. Boundary condition: fixed end → fixed end.
  2. Pattern requested: n = 3.
  3. Start with the fundamental, then scale to the third harmonic.

Target unit: Hz, not just a number.

Worked Example · KDEA

L = 0.80 m, v = 120 m/s → f3 = ? Hz

StepWhat you write
Kstring length L = 0.80 m; wave speed v = 120 m/s; harmonic number n = 3
Dthird-harmonic frequency f3, target unit Hz
Ef1 Hz = v m/s / (2L m); fn Hz = n f1 Hz
Asymbols first: f1 Hz = (120 m/s) / (2 · 0.80 m) = 75 Hz
f3 Hz = 3 · 75 Hz = 225 Hz

The meter cancels in (m/s) / m, leaving 1/s = Hz.

This is the fully worked KDEA example students should model. Pause at the E row and ask why the factor of 2 appears: the fundamental has one half-wavelength across the string. In A, point out the unit check explicitly: (m/s) divided by m leaves 1/s, which is hertz. The common failure is using f = v/L for the fundamental and getting 150 Hz — that is the second-harmonic relationship, not the fundamental.

Act II

Sound & Resonance

Air responds when the frequency matches a natural mode

Transition from strings to air columns. The medium changes, but the logic does not: a system has allowed patterns, and a driver produces a large response when its frequency matches one of those patterns.

Sound · 1 of 3

Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave.

Air particles oscillate back and forth parallel to the direction the sound travels. The pattern is alternating compression and rarefaction.

C
Compression
Particles are closer together; pressure is higher than average.
R
Rarefaction
Particles are farther apart; pressure is lower than average.

Pitch tracks frequency. Loudness tracks amplitude.

Avoid saying that air travels from the speaker to the listener. The disturbance travels; the particles oscillate locally. This distinction matters later when students compare sound to string waves. The pitch and loudness sentence is a useful connection to everyday sound without turning the slide into a vocabulary list.

Sound · 2 of 3

Resonance is a frequency match.

The idea

A system has a natural frequency — a frequency at which it prefers to oscillate. Drive it at that frequency and energy transfers efficiently, producing a large amplitude.

  • Off resonance — small response
  • Near resonance — response grows
  • At resonance — maximum response
  • Energy arrives in phase with the motion
Students often describe resonance as "the object vibrates because the sound is loud." Reverse the cause and effect: the driver frequency matches the system's natural frequency, so each cycle adds energy in the right phase. Use the playground swing analogy only if you can keep the phase idea attached to it.

Sound · 3 of 3

Tubes have boundary conditions too.

Open – open
both ends open
Pressure nodes at the openings. Fundamental: L m = λ/2 m; allowed harmonics are all integer multiples.
Closed – open
one end closed
Pressure antinode at the closed end and pressure node at the open end. Fundamental: L m = λ/4 m; only odd harmonics appear.

Ask: what must happen at each endpoint?

Be precise about displacement versus pressure. For an air column, the open/closed condition is easiest to state in terms of pressure: open ends are pressure nodes, closed ends are pressure antinodes. Students may remember the pattern reversed from string displacement; name the measured quantity before counting.

Resonance Demo · 8 minutes

See the frequency match.

1
String driver
Change driving frequency slowly. Watch the string jump into a stable mode.
2
Air column
Sweep a tone near a tube's natural frequency. Listen for the amplitude peak.
3
Coupled sound
Use one vibrating source to excite a second object with the same natural frequency.
Record

What changed: frequency, amplitude, or both? What evidence says the system is resonating?

The demo is about evidence, not spectacle. Require students to name the controlled quantity and the observed response. If the equipment is unavailable, use a phone tone generator and a bottle or tube. Keep the question "what changed?" in view: resonance is a large amplitude response caused by a frequency match.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 10 minutes

Predict the resonant pattern.

With your partner
  1. A tube is open at both ends. Sketch its fundamental pressure pattern and label nodes and antinodes.
  2. A tube is closed at one end and open at the other. Sketch its fundamental pressure pattern.
  3. Which tube can support a second harmonic? Explain using the endpoint conditions.

Start at the endpoints. The boundary condition decides the pattern.

Do not supply the sketches from the slide. Circulate for the endpoint reasoning. The likely failure is importing the string picture without checking whether the diagram represents air displacement or pressure. Have students label the quantity first, then mark what must be zero or maximum at each boundary.

The Big Picture

Boundary conditions choose the notes.

Standing waves, harmonics, sound, resonance: one pattern story

This is the synthesis slide. The through-line is not "strings versus tubes"; it is a system, its boundaries, its allowed modes, and its response to a driver. Students should be able to move from a physical setup to a sketch, then from the sketch to a wavelength or frequency relation.
