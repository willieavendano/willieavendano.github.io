Where Are You?

Position & Displacement

Vectors with a sign, measured from a reference point

This divider opens the first real kinematics content. Frame it as "the simplest quantity" — position is where something IS. Everything else (velocity, acceleration) is about how position changes. The Δ callback is deliberate: students saw Δx on the very first Greek symbol tour in Workshop I.

Position · 1 of 2

Position is a vector. The sign tells you where.

Every position is measured from a reference point — a zero you pick before you start. The number tells you how far. The sign tells you which side.

One dimension is enough (for now)

Pick a straight line. Call one direction positive and the other negative. That's it — a single number now encodes both distance and direction.

Example

A student stands at x = +3 m: 3 meters to the right of the origin. Another at x = −2 m: 2 meters to the left. Both are positions. Both are vectors.

In kinematics, position is x (horizontal) or y (vertical). The symbol carries the axis.

"Reference point" and "sign encodes direction" are the two non-negotiables here. Students who don't internalize that negative positions are real — not an error — get lost the first time a graph dips below the axis. Keep the axis convention explicit: we pick positive direction first, every time.

Position · 2 of 2

Δx: the change in position

The Δ you met in Unit 0 now has a job: displacement is how far your position changed — and in which direction.

Definition

Δx = xf − xi

  • xf — where you end up (final position)
  • xi — where you started (initial position)
  • Δx itself is a vector: the sign tells you which way you moved

A runner goes from xi = 10 m to xf = 4 m. Displacement: Δx = 4 m − 10 m = −6 m — 6 meters to the left, even though she never crossed the origin.

This slide IS the Δ callback from Unit 0. The subscripts i/f are also a Unit 0 callback (Workshop I's variable-notation aside). Hammer the distinction: distance traveled is always positive (a scalar), but displacement can be negative (a vector). The runner example proves it — 6m of motion, negative displacement. This is the first place sign-as-direction pays off.

Quick Check

On your whiteboard

Two displacements, one reference point
  1. A cyclist rides from xi = −5 m to xf = +7 m. What is Δx? Which direction did she travel?
  2. A ball rolls from xi = 3 m to xf = −1 m. What is Δx? Did it cross the origin?
Answers: (1) Δx = 7 − (−5) = +12 m — moved to the right (positive). (2) Δx = −1 − 3 = −4 m — moved left (negative), and yes, it crossed x = 0. Watch for students who subtract the wrong way (xi − xf) — that's the single most common sign error for the rest of the unit.

How Fast?

Velocity

The slope of a position-vs-time graph

Transition from "where" to "how fast." The slope-of-graph definition is the conceptual anchor — save the equation for the next slide. Students who think of velocity as "the formula v = d/t" instead of "the slope of the x-t curve" can't read a graph when the line isn't perfectly straight.

Velocity · 1 of 3

Velocity = rate of change of position

Velocity tells you how fast position is changing — and in which direction. It is always the slope of a position-vs-time graph.

Definition

v = Δx / Δt

  • Units: m/s — meters of position change per second
  • Velocity is a vector: the sign tells you the direction of motion
  • Speed is the magnitude only — it's velocity with the sign stripped off

Same Δ pattern: final minus initial. Δx = xf − xi, Δt = tf − ti.

The speed-vs-velocity distinction is not an advanced footnote — it's the definition. A car moving at 20 m/s west has velocity = −20 m/s (if east is positive) and speed = 20 m/s. Every graph-reading slide from here depends on students knowing that a negative slope means negative velocity, not "slowing down."

Velocity · 2 of 3

Reading an x–t graph: flat, straight, curved

The shape of the x–t graph tells you everything about the motion — before you calculate a single number:

Flat
horizontal line
Position isn't changing → velocity = 0. The object is stopped.
Straight ramp
constant slope
Position changes at a steady rate → constant velocity. Slope up = positive; slope down = negative.
Curved
changing slope
The slope (velocity) itself is changing → the object is accelerating. Steepening curve = speeding up; flattening curve = slowing down.

The slope at any single point on a curve is the instantaneous velocity at that moment.

The three tiles are the entire graph-reading vocabulary students need. Don't rush past "curved" — that's where the slope-is-velocity idea gets tested hardest. The instantaneous-velocity chip previews what they'll need for non-constant motion, but the main job today is getting the three shape categories locked in.

Velocity · 3 of 3

Turn and talk: describe the motion

With your partner — 3 minutes

Sketch this on your whiteboard: an x–t graph where an object:

  1. Starts at x = 0 and moves forward at a constant speed for 4 seconds, reaching x = 8 m
  2. Stops and stays at x = 8 m for 3 seconds
  3. Turns around and walks back at a slower constant speed, reaching x = 2 m after 5 more seconds

Label each segment: where is velocity positive? Zero? Negative? Which segment is steepest (fastest)?

This is a formative check disguised as pair work. Circulate and look for: (a) are the constant-speed segments straight lines? (b) does the return segment slope downward? (c) is its slope less steep than the first segment? The "slower = less steep" connection is the core transfer — if they get that, the rest of the week is downhill.

Changing Speed

Acceleration

The slope of a velocity-vs-time graph

Same framing as velocity: the graph definition is the concept, the equation is just the math. Students instinctively think acceleration = "speeding up," which is wrong — slowing down is also acceleration (negative). Nail this distinction here or it haunts every free-fall problem.

Acceleration · 1 of 3

Acceleration = rate of change of velocity

Acceleration tells you how fast velocity itself is changing — and in which direction. It is always the slope of a velocity-vs-time graph.

Definition

a = Δv / Δt

  • Units: m/s2 — “meters per second, per second”
  • Acceleration is a vector: sign tells you which way the velocity is changing
  • Negative acceleration does not automatically mean slowing down — it means the velocity change points negative

If velocity is in m/s and time is in seconds, acceleration is always in m/s2.

"m/s2" is mouthful — read it out as "meters per second per second" and explain: every second that passes, the velocity changes by that many m/s. The negative-acceleration pitfall is the #1 kinematics error: a car moving backward (−10 m/s) that speeds up to −20 m/s has negative acceleration AND is speeding up. The sign of acceleration alone doesn't tell you "speeding up" vs "slowing down" — you need to compare the signs of v and a.

Acceleration · 2 of 3

Reading a v–t graph: same three shapes

The same flat/straight/curved logic applies — except now the y-axis is velocity, not position:

Flat
horizontal line
Velocity isn't changing → acceleration = 0. The object moves at constant velocity (which could be zero).
Straight ramp
constant slope
Velocity changes at a steady rate → constant acceleration. Slope up = velocity increasing; slope down = velocity decreasing.
Curved
changing slope
The acceleration itself is changing → jerk (non-constant acceleration). We'll stay away from this in Unit 1.

Flat v–t ≠ stopped. Flat v–t = constant velocity. Stopped is a special case where that constant velocity happens to be 0.

The "flat ≠ stopped" chip is the critical correction. In an x-t graph, flat means stopped. In a v-t graph, flat means cruise control — the car is moving at a steady speed. This is the most common graph-misreading in the entire unit, and it's worth saying out loud at least three times this week.

Acceleration · 3 of 3

On your whiteboard

Read the graph, don't calculate (yet)

A v–t graph shows a car that:

  1. Starts at v = 0 and accelerates uniformly, reaching v = 20 m/s after 5 seconds
  2. Maintains v = 20 m/s for 8 seconds
  3. Brakes, coming to a stop over the next 4 seconds

Sketch the v–t graph. Label each segment: where is acceleration positive? Zero? Negative?

Answer sketch: segment 1 = straight line sloping up from (0,0) to (5,20) — positive acceleration. Segment 2 = flat horizontal line at 20 m/s — zero acceleration (constant velocity, NOT stopped). Segment 3 = straight line sloping down to (17,0) — negative acceleration. Cold-call on the segment-2 trap: "is the car stopped during segment 2?" If anyone says yes, loop back to slide 13's chip.

The Big Picture

The Slope Chain

Three graphs, one story

This is the conceptual centerpiece of the week: the three graphs aren't separate — they're chained together by slope. Every slide so far has built toward this moment. The video (next slide) animates it; today's job is just to name the chain and recognize it.

The Slope Chain · 1 of 2

Slope connects all three graphs

You don't need three separate measurements. From a single position-vs-time graph, you can extract velocity. From the velocity graph, you get acceleration. The math is always the same operation: slope.

x–t → v–t
slope of position gives velocity
v = Δx / Δt at every point on the x–t graph. A steep x–t slope = large velocity. A flat x–t slope = zero velocity.
v–t → a–t
slope of velocity gives acceleration
a = Δv / Δt at every point on the v–t graph. A steep v–t slope = large acceleration. A flat v–t slope = zero acceleration.

One motion. Three graphs. Slope is the thread that ties them together.

The single-column tile-grid makes each link read as a distinct step. Walk it left to right on the board if possible: draw an x-t curve, then underneath it sketch what the v-t must look like based on the slope at each point, then under that sketch the a-t. The chain is spatial, not algebraic — let it be visual.

KDEA in Action

Extract Velocity & Acceleration

Same four steps you learned in Unit 0. New context, same protocol.

This is the first KDEA table of Unit 1 — make it explicit that nothing about the protocol changed. The equations are new, but the four-step move is identical to Workshop II. Students who think KDEA was "a Unit 0 thing" need to hear that it's THE method for every problem all year.

Worked Example · 1 of 2

Given an x–t graph, find the velocity

A student walks away from a motion sensor. The x–t graph shows a straight line from (t = 0 s, x = 0.5 m) to (t = 4.0 s, x = 2.5 m). What is her velocity?

StepWhat you write
Kxi = 0.5 m, xf = 2.5 m, ti = 0 s, tf = 4.0 s
Dvelocity v, target unit m/s
Ev = Δx / Δt = (xf − xi) / (tf − ti)
Asymbols first: Δx = 2.5 m − 0.5 m = 2.0 m, Δt = 4.0 s − 0 s = 4.0 s
v = 2.0 m / 4.0 s = 0.50 m/s

Straight x–t line = constant velocity. The slope IS the velocity: 2.0 m ÷ 4.0 s = 0.50 m/s.

Walk this exact KDEA table format — it's identical to the Workshop II tables from three weeks ago. Point out that v is positive (slope is up), so the student walks away from the sensor. The "symbols first" line in A is non-negotiable: Δx and Δt get computed before the division, the way it would appear on a test.

Worked Example · 2 of 2

Now find the acceleration from v–t

A car's v–t graph shows a straight ramp from (t = 0 s, v = 5.0 m/s) to (t = 3.0 s, v = 20.0 m/s). What is the car's acceleration?

StepWhat you write
Kvi = 5.0 m/s, vf = 20.0 m/s, ti = 0 s, tf = 3.0 s
Dacceleration a, target unit m/s2
Ea = Δv / Δt = (vf − vi) / (tf − ti)
Asymbols first: Δv = 20.0 m/s − 5.0 m/s = 15.0 m/s, Δt = 3.0 s − 0 s = 3.0 s
a = 15.0 m/s / 3.0 s = 5.0 m/s2

The units check out: m/s divided by s gives m/s2 — exactly what acceleration demands.

The unit check at the bottom is deliberate: m/s ÷ s = m/s2. Students who write 5.0 m/s as the final answer here have lost the unit — flag it. This is also the first Unit 1 KDEA with acceleration in the D row; the target unit m/s2 is a Unit 0 callback (derived units, Workshop I slide 3b).
