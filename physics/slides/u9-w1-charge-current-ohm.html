Act I

Electric Charge & Coulomb's Law

The force that runs every circuit you'll build this unit

Frame charge as the electrical analog of mass in Unit 2 — it's the "how much stuff" quantity that a force law (Coulomb's law) acts on, exactly the way mass was the quantity Newton's second law acted on. That parallel will make Coulomb's law feel far less foreign.

Charge · 1 of 4

What is electric charge?

  • Two types: positive and negative. Like charges repel; opposite charges attract.
  • Charge is conserved — it's never created or destroyed, only transferred from one object to another.
  • Charge is quantized — it always comes in whole-number multiples of the elementary charge, e ≈ 1.6 × 10−19 C, the charge on one proton (or one electron, with a minus sign).

The unit of charge is the coulomb (C) — a huge amount of charge; everyday objects carry micro- or nanocoulombs.

The "quantized" point matters more than it looks — it's the same logic as the SI base units from Unit 0 (build everything from a smallest piece). Every charge a student will ever compute is secretly n × e for some integer n, even though we'll almost never work at that scale directly. Coulombs are enormous compared to real static-charge amounts — that's why practice problems use μC, not C.

Charge · 2 of 4

Three ways an object gets charged

⚡
Friction
Rubbing transfers electrons from one material to another — balloon on hair, socks on carpet.
🔗
Conduction
Direct contact between a charged and neutral object shares the charge across both.
📡
Induction
A charged object nearby (no contact) pushes electrons around inside a neutral one, separating charge without touching it.

In all three, no charge is ever created — electrons just move from where they were to where they end up.

Keep this fast — it's context for why Coulomb's law matters, not a deep dive. The through-line to hammer: every method is electrons relocating, never charge appearing from nothing. That's the conservation rule from the last slide, made concrete.

Charge · 3 of 4

Coulomb's Law: the force between two charges

F = k |q1q2| / r2

  • q1, q2 — the two charges, in coulombs (C)
  • r — the distance between their centers, in meters (m)
  • k — Coulomb's constant, k ≈ 8.99 × 109 N·m2/C2

Same shape as universal gravitation from Unit 3: two source quantities on top, distance squared on the bottom.

This is the payoff of the Unit 3 callback — F = Gm₁m₂/r² and F = kq₁q₂/r² are structurally identical. If a student can already picture how gravity gets weaker with the square of distance, Coulomb's law is the same picture with charge standing in for mass. The absolute-value bars matter: the formula gives magnitude only — sign (attract/repel) comes from checking whether the charges match or differ, done separately.

Charge · 4 of 4

Worked example: force between two charges

Two point charges, q1 = 3.0 μC and q2 = −5.0 μC, sit 0.20 m apart. Find the force between them.

StepWhat you write
Kq1 = 3.0 × 10−6 C, q2 = −5.0 × 10−6 C, r = 0.20 m
Dforce magnitude F (target unit: N)
EF = k|q1q2| / r2
AF = (8.99 × 109)(3.0 × 10−6)(5.0 × 10−6) / (0.20)2 ≈ 3.4 N

Opposite signs → the force is attractive. F ≈ 3.4 N pulling the two charges together.

Walk the unit check: N·m²/C² times C times C over m² leaves N — confirm it out loud before trusting the number, exactly like every other KDEA problem this year. The sign discussion is a separate, final step after the magnitude — students who try to carry the minus sign through the formula get confused; the rule is "compute magnitude, then reason about direction from the charge types."

Practice · Turn & Talk · 6 minutes

Your turn: two positive charges

With your partner

Two charges, q1 = 2.0 μC and q2 = 6.0 μC, are separated by 0.30 m. Run full KDEA to find the force between them, and state whether it's attractive or repulsive.

Answer for the teacher: F = (8.99×10⁹)(2.0×10⁻⁶)(6.0×10⁻⁶)/(0.30)² ≈ 1.2 N, repulsive (both positive). Circulate and check that students square r before dividing — the most common slip is squaring the whole denominator expression incorrectly or forgetting the square entirely.

Act II

Current, Voltage & Resistance

The three quantities every circuit is built from

Transition from static charge (charges sitting still) to charge in motion (current). This is the conceptual hinge of the whole unit — everything from here through the capstone build is about charge that flows, not charge that sits.

Current, Voltage, Resistance · 1 of 3

Current: charge in motion

I = Q / t

  • Current (I) is the rate charge flows past a point — unit: ampere, A = C/s
  • By convention, current flows from + to − — even though the actual moving particles (electrons) go the opposite way
  • A circuit must be a closed loop for current to flow at all — break the loop anywhere and I = 0 everywhere

1 amp = 1 coulomb of charge passing a point every second — that's roughly 6 × 1018 electrons per second.

Conventional current direction (+ to −) versus actual electron flow (− to +) trips students up every year — name it explicitly and move on; we use conventional current for every calculation this unit, full stop. The "closed loop" point previews next week's whole lesson on series/parallel topology — a single break anywhere in a series loop kills current everywhere.

Current, Voltage, Resistance · 2 of 3

Voltage: the electrical "push"

V = W / Q

  • Voltage (V), or potential difference, is energy delivered per unit charge — unit: volt, V = J/C
  • A battery's voltage rating is the energy it gives every coulomb of charge that passes through it
  • Voltage is always measured between two points — it's a difference, not a property of one spot

No voltage, no push, no current — a battery is what keeps charge moving around the loop.

"Between two points" is worth repeating — students who say "the voltage of the wire" instead of "the voltage across the resistor" are missing this. It sets up next week's voltmeter-placement rule (measured in parallel, across a component) before they ever touch a meter.

Current, Voltage, Resistance · 3 of 3

A useful picture: water in a pipe

I
Current ↔ flow rate
How much water passes a point per second (liters/s, here: coulombs/s)
V
Voltage ↔ pressure
The push behind the water — a pump (here: a battery) that creates the pressure difference
R
Resistance ↔ pipe narrowness
A narrower pipe resists flow more — you need more pressure to push the same flow through it

This analogy breaks down eventually (all analogies do) — but it's a fast, reliable way to check whether a circuit answer is in the right ballpark.

Use this analogy sparingly but deliberately — it's genuinely useful for a quick sanity check ("more resistance with the same voltage means less current — does my answer show current going down?") but shouldn't replace the actual equation. Flag explicitly where it breaks down if a curious student pushes on it: it doesn't handle AC, capacitance, or the fact that real current is near-instantaneous everywhere in the loop, not a slow flow like water.

Resistance

What determines a wire's resistance?

L
Length
Longer wire → more resistance. Twice the length, twice the resistance.
A
Cross-sectional area
Thicker wire → less resistance. It's easier to push charge through a wider path.
ρ
Resistivity (material)
A material property — copper resists far less than rubber. This is why wires are copper and insulation isn't.
T
Temperature
Most conductors resist more as they heat up — a hot wire has higher resistance than a cool one.

Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω) — unit: Ω = V/A.

Full resistivity formula (R = ρL/A) is beyond this course's scope — the goal here is qualitative fluency: which direction does each factor push resistance, not computing it from ρ. That said, μ from Unit 0's Greek tour makes a cameo — remind students ρ (rho) is a different Greek letter, not to be confused with μ (friction) from Unit 2.

Act III

Ohm's Law

V = IR — the equation that ties current, voltage, and resistance together

This is the single most-used equation for the rest of the unit — students will run it dozens of times between now and the capstone build. Make sure the triangle mnemonic (V on top, I and R on the bottom) lands before moving to the worked example.

Ohm's Law · 1 of 3

V = IR

Voltage across a resistor equals the current through it times its resistance. Rearrange for whichever variable is missing:

V = IR
solve for voltage
given current and resistance
I = V/R
solve for current
given voltage and resistance
R = V/I
solve for resistance
given voltage and current

Same move as every kinematics equation this year: identify what's missing, rearrange symbols first, then plug in numbers with units.

This is the E step of KDEA, presented as a menu — exactly like the four kinematics equations in Unit 1. Point out explicitly: this is one equation in three algebraic disguises, not three separate formulas to memorize. That framing prevents the "which formula do I use" panic later.

Ohm's Law · 2 of 3

Worked example: finding current

A resistor rated at 25 Ω has 12 V across it. Find the current through it.

StepWhat you write
KV = 12 V, R = 25 Ω
Dcurrent I (target unit: A)
EV = IR
Asymbols first: I = V / R  →  I = 12 V / 25 Ω = 0.48 A

V/Ω leaves A — the unit confirms the algebra before you trust the number.

Same discipline as every KDEA table this year: rearrange before substituting. Point out that 0.48 A is a perfectly normal small-electronics current — this is the scale students will actually measure with a multimeter next week, so the number should feel plausible, not abstract.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 6 minutes

Your turn: finding voltage

With your partner

A circuit carries a current of 1.5 A through a resistor of 8.0 Ω. Run full KDEA to find the voltage across the resistor.

Answer for the teacher: V = IR = (1.5 A)(8.0 Ω) = 12 V. This one is a straight substitution with no rearranging needed — a confidence-builder before next week's harder multi-resistor problems. Watch for students who still write "I = V/R" out of habit instead of solving for V directly.

Bonus · Electrical Power

Ohm's law has a power family too

Electrical power (P, in watts, W = J/s — the same watt from Unit 0 and Unit 4) has three equivalent forms, depending on what you know:

P = IV
know I and V
the general definition — rate of energy delivered
P = I2R
know I and R
substitute V = IR into P = IV
P = V2/R
know V and R
substitute I = V/R into P = IV

All three come from the same two equations (P = IV and V = IR) — nothing new to memorize, just algebra you already know.

This slide exists to prevent "three new formulas" panic — walk the derivation live on the board: start from P = IV, substitute Ohm's law in each direction, and both other forms fall out. This is the exact same "menu of one idea" framing as the Ohm's law slide. Power will matter directly for the capstone — an LED or motor has a power rating that constrains circuit design.
