How Hard to Stop?

Momentum

Mass in motion — a vector quantity every moving object carries

Open with the intuition before the symbol: a bowling ball rolling slowly and a baseball thrown hard can be equally difficult to stop with your bare hands. Momentum is the single quantity that captures "mass in motion" — heavier and/or faster both increase it.

Momentum · 1 of 1

p = mv — mass times velocity

Momentum measures how much "quantity of motion" an object has. It combines how heavy something is with how fast it's moving.

Definition

p = mv

  • Units: kg·m/s — mass (kg) times velocity (m/s)
  • Momentum is a vector: it points in the same direction as velocity
  • A heavy, slow object and a light, fast object can have the same momentum

No new units to learn — kg·m/s is just mass and velocity's units multiplied together.

Stress that momentum inherits its direction from velocity, exactly like every other vector this year — sign conventions from Unit 1/2 (pick positive, stick with it) apply immediately. There is no special "momentum unit" the way there was a joule for energy; kg·m/s is literally mass times velocity's units, which is worth pointing out since students expect every new quantity to get a named unit.

Quick Check

On your whiteboard

Two objects, one question
  1. A 0.50 kg ball moves at 8.0 m/s. Find its momentum.
  2. A 1000 kg car crawls through a parking lot at 5.0 m/s. Find its momentum. Which object — the ball or the car — is harder to stop?
Answers: (1) p = (0.50 kg)(8.0 m/s) = 4.0 kg·m/s. (2) p = (1000 kg)(5.0 m/s) = 5000 kg·m/s — the slow-moving car has over 1000 times the momentum of the fast ball, even though it "looks" gentle at walking pace. This is the entire point of the definition: mass dominates just as much as velocity does. Watch for students defaulting to "fast = more momentum" without checking mass.

Changing Motion

Impulse

What a force does to momentum, over time

Transition from "how much motion" (momentum) to "how motion changes" (impulse). This mirrors the position→velocity→acceleration progression from Unit 1 — each new quantity describes how the previous one changes.

Impulse · 1 of 1

J = FΔt — force applied over time

A force doesn't change an object's motion instantly — it acts over some duration. Impulse measures the combined effect of how hard and how long a force pushes.

Definition

J = FΔt

  • Units: N·s — force (N) times time (s)
  • Impulse is a vector: it points in the same direction as the force
  • Assumes a constant (or average) force over the interval Δt

Same force, longer time → bigger impulse. Same force, shorter time → smaller impulse.

"Average force" is doing real work in that third bullet — most real collisions have a force that spikes and fades (bat hitting ball, airbag deploying), and F here means the constant force that would produce the same total effect. This detail matters for the worked examples later, where we compute F_avg, not some instantaneous peak force.

Quick Check

On your whiteboard

Force over time
  1. A golf club exerts an average force of 4000 N on a ball for 0.0005 s. Find the impulse delivered to the ball.
  2. A tugboat pushes a barge with 1500 N of force for 20 s. Find the impulse. Which situation — the golf swing or the tugboat — delivers more impulse?
Answers: (1) J = (4000 N)(0.0005 s) = 2.0 N·s. (2) J = (1500 N)(20 s) = 30,000 N·s = 3.0×10⁴ N·s — the slow, sustained push from the tugboat delivers far more impulse than the violent, brief golf swing. This pairs directly with slide 5's momentum result: a small force over a long time can outdo a huge force over a short time. Same lesson, different quantity.

The Connection · 1 of 2

Impulse comes straight from Newton's second law

Unit 2 gave you F = ma. Acceleration is itself Δv / Δt — so a little substitution turns Newton's second law into impulse.

The derivation

F = ma

F = m (Δv / Δt)

FΔt = mΔv

The left side, FΔt, is impulse. The right side, mΔv, is the change in momentum. They're the same thing.

This is the pivotal slide of the week — everything after it is a consequence of this one algebra move. Walk it on the board exactly as written: start from F = ma (Unit 2), substitute a = Δv/Δt (Unit 1), multiply both sides by Δt. Students who see this derivation once rarely need to memorize the impulse–momentum theorem as a separate fact — they can rebuild it.

The Connection · 2 of 2

The units agree, too

If impulse and change in momentum are really the same quantity, their units had better match. They do.

Impulse

N·s

A newton is kg·m/s2, so N·s = kg·m/s2 · s = kg·m/s.

Change in momentum

kg·m/s

Straight from p = mv — mass times velocity, no substitution needed.

N·s and kg·m/s are the same unit wearing two different outfits.

This is a dimensional-analysis check in the KDEA spirit — units confirming an algebraic identity before students trust it. Worth writing both unit chains on the board side by side. Some textbooks report impulse in N·s and momentum in kg·m/s without ever pointing out they're identical; closing that gap here prevents confusion later when a problem mixes the two labels.

Putting It Together

The Impulse–Momentum Theorem

J = Δp — the force applied equals the momentum it changes

Name the theorem explicitly — this is the headline result of the week and the thing that shows up on the Unit 5 Test. Everything from here is applying J = Δp to real numbers.

The Theorem · 1 of 1

J = Δp = m(vf − vi)

The impulse delivered to an object equals the change in that object's momentum. This is one equation with two equally useful readings.

Two ways to use it
  • Know the force and time → find how much the object's motion changed.
  • Know how much the motion changed → find the average force (or the time) that must have caused it.

Same Δ pattern as every quantity this year: final minus initial. Δp = mvf − mvi.

Frame the "two readings" bullet as the whole reason this theorem is useful — most problems this week go in the second direction (known Δv, unknown F), which is the harder direction for students used to plugging F into F=ma directly. Watch for the classic sign error: v_i and v_f must use a consistent positive direction, especially when an object bounces back (reverses direction).

Worked Example · 1 of 2

The average force of a bat on a ball

A 0.145 kg baseball travels toward the batter at 20.0 m/s. The bat sends it back the other way at 35.0 m/s. Contact lasts 1.5 × 10−3 s. Find the average force the bat exerts on the ball.

StepWhat you write
Km = 0.145 kg, vi = −20.0 m/s, vf = +35.0 m/s, Δt = 1.5 × 10−3 s
Daverage force F (target unit: N)
EFΔt = m(vf − vi)
Asymbols first: F = m(vf − vi) / Δt
F = (0.145 kg)(35.0 − (−20.0)) m/s / (1.5 × 10−3 s)
F = (0.145)(55.0) / (1.5 × 10−3) ≈ 5.3 × 103 N

The ball reversed direction, so vi and vf have opposite signs — that's why they add, not subtract, in magnitude.

This is the sign-convention trap of the week: choosing "toward the outfield" as positive makes the incoming pitch negative and the outgoing hit positive, so v_f − v_i = 35.0 − (−20.0) = 55.0 m/s, not 15.0 m/s. Students who forget the sign flip will compute a 3× too-small force. The final answer, ≈5.3×10³ N, is a genuinely large but physically real number — worth a beat to say "yes, a bat really does exert several thousand newtons, for a millisecond."

Worked Example · 2 of 2

Same crash, two different stopping times

A 1200 kg car traveling at 22 m/s comes to a complete stop in a collision. Compare the average force if the stop takes 0.080 s (hitting a wall directly) versus 0.80 s (a crumple zone and airbag absorbing the impact).

StepWhat you write
Km = 1200 kg, vi = 22 m/s, vf = 0 m/s
Daverage force F for Δt = 0.080 s and Δt = 0.80 s (target unit: N)
EFΔt = m(vf − vi)
AΔp = (1200 kg)(0 − 22 m/s) = −26,400 kg·m/s
0.080 s: F = −26,400 / 0.080 ≈ −3.3 × 105 N
0.80 s: F = −26,400 / 0.80 ≈ −3.3 × 104 N

Ten times the stopping time gives one-tenth the force — same impulse, completely different outcome for the passenger.

The impulse (Δp = −26,400 kg·m/s) is identical in both cases — it's fixed by the mass and the velocity change, full stop. What changes is how that impulse gets delivered: instantly (huge force, catastrophic) or stretched out (survivable force). This example is the direct setup for the safety section next — don't over-explain the "why" yet, let the numbers speak, then land it on the next divider slide.

The Payoff

Why Δt Matters

Same impulse, same Δp — but a longer Δt means a smaller force

This divider names the physics principle behind every safety application that follows: for a fixed impulse, force and time are inversely related. Airbags, crumple zones, and padded gloves are all the same idea wearing different clothes — none of them change how much momentum has to be absorbed; they all just stretch out the time it takes.

Applications · 1 of 1

Stretch the time, shrink the force

Airbags
Inflate in milliseconds to give a passenger's head a longer, softer stop than the dashboard would — same Δp, much smaller F.
Crumple zones
The front of a car is designed to fold, not stay rigid — folding takes time, and that extra Δt protects the passenger cabin.
Catching an egg
Pulling your hands back as you catch extends the contact time — the same egg, caught rigidly, would break.
Follow-through in sports
A tennis or golf swing that follows through applies force over a longer stroke, increasing impulse delivered to the ball on purpose — the opposite goal from safety gear.

Same equation, opposite goals: safety gear stretches Δt to shrink F; a good swing stretches Δt to grow J.

The follow-through tile is the deliberate twist — three tiles are about reducing force by increasing time (protecting something), and the fourth flips the goal (maximizing the impulse delivered to a ball by keeping the racket/club in contact longer). Students sometimes think "longer Δt = always safer," so this tile corrects that: it's always about what you're trying to accomplish with J = FΔt, not a fixed rule of "long time good, short time bad."

Turn & Talk · 8 minutes

Design a safer landing

With your partner

A 0.50 kg raw egg is dropped and hits the floor at 4.0 m/s, stopping in 0.0020 s — it breaks. Your job: describe a landing surface or catching method that would let the egg survive, and estimate what stopping time you'd need for the average force to drop to one-tenth of the original.

Hint: you don't need new physics — the impulse (Δp) doesn't change. Only Δt does.

There's no single correct answer here — the point is applying F ∝ 1/Δt qualitatively and quantitatively. Mathematically: since Δp is fixed, F and Δt are inversely proportional, so a stopping time 10× longer (0.020 s instead of 0.0020 s) gives exactly 1/10 the force. Listen for groups proposing pillows, towels, or a "catch and pull back" motion — all valid, all working through the same Δt-stretching mechanism as the airbag example.
