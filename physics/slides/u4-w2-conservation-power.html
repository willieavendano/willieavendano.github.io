The Big Idea

Energy Is Never Created or Destroyed

Only transformed — from one form into another

State this claim as big as KDEA's "the way to solve ANY problem." It is one of the deepest laws in all of physics, and algebra-based mechanics only needs the mechanical-energy special case of it. Don't undersell the size of the idea just because the math today is simple.

Conservation · 1 of 4

Total mechanical energy: KE and PE, added together

Definition

E = KE + PE

One number that tracks all the mechanical energy an object has at a given instant — moving energy plus stored energy.

Same unit as both pieces: joules. Adding KE + PE is legal because the units already match.

Worth a quick KDEA-style unit check: you can only add two quantities if their units match, exactly the Unit 0 "3 km + 500 m" trap. KE and PE are both joules, so the addition is legitimate — unlike, say, adding a velocity to an acceleration.

Conservation · 2 of 4

If nothing removes energy, total E stays constant

The law (no friction, no air resistance)

KEi + PEi = KEf + PEf

Whatever the total was at the start, it's still the total at the end — even though the split between KE and PE can change completely.

A roller coaster at the top of the first hill: mostly PE. At the bottom: mostly KE. Same total, the whole way down.

The roller coaster image is the one to lean on all week — tall hills are PE reservoirs, valleys are where it converts to KE, and the coaster never goes higher than its starting hill (without a motor) because it can't manufacture energy from nothing. Explicitly connect back to Unit 3: gravity is doing the same "always perpendicular to circular motion, zero work" trick at the bottom of a loop.

Conservation · 3 of 4

Same law, three familiar scenes

↓
falling object
PE converts to KE as height drops. All PE at release, all KE just before impact.
↑
thrown straight up
KE converts to PE on the way up. Zero KE at the peak — all of it is PE there.
↔
pendulum swing
Trades KE and PE back and forth every swing. Max KE at the bottom, max PE at each end.

Three different motions, one identical bookkeeping rule underneath.

The pendulum tile previews the Energy Lab track setup two weeks from now — a cart on a track behaves like one arc of a pendulum swing. Point that out explicitly so the lab doesn't feel like new content when it arrives.

Worked Example · 1 of 3

A cart released from rest on a frictionless track

A 2.0 kg cart starts from rest at the top of a frictionless track, 1.5 m above the bottom. How fast is it moving when it reaches the bottom?

StepWhat you write
Km = 2.0 kg, hi = 1.5 m, hf = 0 m, vi = 0 m/s
Dfinal speed vf (target unit: m/s)
EKEi + PEi = KEf + PEf  →  0 + mghi = ½mvf2 + 0
Asymbols first: \( v_f = \sqrt{2gh_i} \) — m cancels!
vf = √(2 · 9.8 · 1.5) = √29.4 ≈ 5.4 m/s

Mass canceled out of the algebra — a heavier cart would arrive at the same speed. That's not a coincidence.

The mass cancellation is worth dwelling on — it's the energy-method version of "all objects fall at the same rate" from Unit 1 free fall. Ask the room: does a bowling ball reach the bottom of a frictionless ramp faster than a marble released from the same height? (No — same v_f, this equation proves it.) √29.4 is irrational, so ≈ is required.

When It Isn't Free

Energy and Friction

Real tracks aren't frictionless — where does the missing energy go?

This divider reopens Unit 2's friction (μ) content in a new context. Mechanical energy isn't truly destroyed by friction — it converts to heat and sound, forms of energy this course doesn't track numerically. What we CAN track is how much mechanical energy friction removes, via the work it does.

Friction · 1 of 3

Friction removes mechanical energy — it doesn't destroy it

The adjusted law

KEi + PEi + Wfriction = KEf + PEf

Wfriction is negative — friction always opposes motion (Unit 4, slide 7's θ = 180° case), so it always subtracts mechanical energy from the total.

The energy isn't gone from the universe — it left the mechanical system as heat.

This is the direct callback to Week 1's positive/negative/zero work tiles — friction is the canonical example of W < 0 because it points opposite to motion by definition. Students who internalized that slide will find this equation almost obvious; students who didn't will need it re-derived here.

Worked Example · 2 of 3

A block on a real (not frictionless) ramp

A 3.0 kg block starts at rest at the top of a ramp 2.0 m high. Friction does −15 J of work on it as it slides to the bottom. Find its speed at the bottom.

StepWhat you write
Km = 3.0 kg, h = 2.0 m, vi = 0 m/s, Wfriction = −15 J
Dfinal speed vf (target unit: m/s)
EKEi + PEi + Wfriction = KEf + PEf
A0 + mgh + Wfriction = ½mvf2 + 0
(3.0)(9.8)(2.0) + (−15) = ½(3.0)vf2
58.8 − 15 = 43.8 J = ½(3.0)vf2  →  vf ≈ 5.4 m/s

Without friction this block would hit 6.3 m/s (check it) — friction cost it about 0.9 m/s of speed.

The chip's comparison number (6.3 m/s frictionless, from √(2×9.8×2.0)) is a good live-board addition if time allows — it makes the "cost" of friction concrete rather than abstract. Watch for students who add W_friction as positive instead of subtracting — the negative sign has to be carried in, not applied as a separate subtraction step.

Quick Check

On your whiteboard

Same setup, different question

A 4.0 kg sled starts at rest at the top of a 3.0 m hill. It reaches the bottom at 6.5 m/s. How much work did friction do on it along the way?

Same equation, solved for a different unknown — the KDEA table just rearranges which row is D.

Answer: KE_f = ½(4.0)(6.5)² = 84.5 J. Frictionless prediction: mgh = (4.0)(9.8)(3.0) = 117.6 J. So W_friction = KE_f − mgh = 84.5 − 117.6 ≈ −33.1 J. This is the reverse-engineering skill they'll need for the Energy Lab in two weeks — solving for W_friction from measured data instead of solving for v_f from a given W_friction.

How Fast Energy Moves

Power

The same work, done quickly or slowly

Power is a smaller, more mechanical topic than conservation — treat it as its own short arc rather than trying to connect it deeply to the conservation law. The core idea: two processes can do identical work but at wildly different rates, and rate is what "power" measures.

Power · 1 of 3

Power = work done, per unit time

Definition

P = W / t

  • W — work done, in J
  • t — time it took, in s
  • Units: J/s = W (watt) — met in Workshop I, entrance is today

Carrying a box up the stairs slowly and carrying it up quickly do the exact same work — different power.

Direct callback to Workshop I's units-you'll-earn tile grid (W · watt · power · J/s · Unit 4, Energy). The stairs example is deliberately identical work / different time to isolate what power actually measures — students conflate "more work" with "more power" constantly, and this distinguishes them.

Power · 2 of 3

What a watt actually looks like

≈100 W
a person, walking
sustained human power output over a normal pace
≈750 W
a microwave oven
household appliance, mid-range
≈75,000 W
a car engine
a typical car engine at highway power, ≈100 horsepower

Same unit (J/s) across four orders of magnitude — this is exactly the estimation instinct from Workshop I.

This is a Fermi-flavored slide — the point is building intuition for scale, not memorizing the numbers. If time allows, ask students to guess which is bigger before revealing: most underestimate how much more powerful a car engine is than a person, which is the whole point of building the intuition.

Worked Example · 3 of 3

Climbing a flight of stairs

A 70 kg person climbs a staircase, gaining 3.0 m of height, in 4.0 s. Find their average power output.

StepWhat you write
Km = 70 kg, h = 3.0 m, t = 4.0 s
Dpower P (target unit: W)
EW = mgh (work against gravity), then P = W / t
AW = (70)(9.8)(3.0) = 2058 J
P = 2058 J / 4.0 s ≈ 510 W

That's over 5× the average walking power from the tile grid — climbing stairs is genuinely demanding.

The comparison back to slide 15's ~100 W walking figure gives this abstract number a felt scale. Two- step KDEA (find W, then find P) is the shape students will need on next week's problems — flag that explicitly since it's a slightly different pattern than a single-equation A row.

Quick Check

On your whiteboard

Two motors, one question

Motor A does 3,600 J of work in 6.0 s. Motor B does 3,600 J of work in 2.0 s. Which motor is more powerful, and by what factor?

Same work total — power is entirely about the clock.

Answer: P_A = 3600/6.0 = 600 W. P_B = 3600/2.0 = 1800 W. Motor B is 3× more powerful despite doing identical work — this locks in the "power is about the rate, not the total" distinction one more time before the recap.
