Torque & Rotational Equilibrium

Week 1

Lever arms · balance conditions · hands-on stations

Unit 6 opens with the turning effect of forces, not with angular kinematics. Students already know force diagrams and right-triangle trig; this week asks them to apply those tools to a pivot. Three meetings: Mon 3/1 (44m), Tue 3/2 (55m), Thu 3/4 (80m). Keep the physical stations central: the goal is not memorizing a new formula, but seeing why distance from the pivot and force direction both matter.

Why This Matters

A force can move an object without translating it.

Push a door near its hinge and it barely turns. Push the handle and it swings. The force can be the same; the turning effect changes because the force acts at a different distance and direction.

  • Translation asks: what happens to the center of mass?
  • Rotation asks: what happens about a chosen pivot?
  • A longer wrench gives the same force more leverage.
  • A perpendicular push turns better than a glancing push.

This week: measure the turning effect, then make it balance.

Students often treat torque as a completely new kind of force. It is not another force; it is the rotational effect of a force about a chosen point. Keep asking "about which pivot?" because torque is always relative to that choice. The door and wrench examples make the lever-arm idea concrete before any symbols appear.

The Turning Effect

Torque

How strongly a force tries to rotate an object about a pivot

Pause here and have students mime a door push with one finger. Ask what would make the door turn more: a bigger force, a farther push, or a more perpendicular push. Collect all three before introducing the equation. This keeps the formula attached to a physical cause rather than as an isolated memorization.

Torque · 1 of 3

Torque has three ingredients.

r
lever arm · m
Distance from the pivot to where the force acts. Farther from the pivot usually means more turning effect.
F
force · N
A larger force produces a larger torque when the lever arm and angle stay the same.
θ
force angle · °
Only the component of force perpendicular to the lever arm produces the turn.

Torque is about a pivot. Name the pivot before calculating.

The three tiles are the conceptual checklist. Make students point to the pivot, draw the lever arm, and identify the angle between r and F in every station sketch. Most later errors are really setup errors: the wrong distance or the wrong angle, not difficult algebra.

Torque · 2 of 3

Use the perpendicular part of the force.

Definition

τ = rF·sinθ

  • τ — torque, in N·m
  • r — pivot-to-force distance, in m
  • F — applied force, in N
  • θ — angle between r and F, in °

Right triangle only: F⊥ = F·sinθ, so τ = rF⊥.

Units: m × N = N·m. Torque is not measured in joules.

This course uses right-triangle trig only. Do not introduce cross products or radians here. Draw the force vector, the perpendicular component, and the small right triangle. Students must know that the sine belongs to the component perpendicular to r. At 90 degrees, sin 90 degrees = 1; at 0 degrees, the force points along the lever arm and produces no torque.

Torque · 3 of 3

Same force. Three different turning effects.

θ = 90°
maximum torque
sin 90° = 1, so τ = rF. The force is fully perpendicular.
0° < θ < 90°
partial torque
Only F·sinθ turns the object. The rest points along the lever arm.
θ = 0°
zero torque
sin 0° = 0. A force through the pivot has no lever arm effect.

If the line of action passes through the pivot, τ = 0 N·m.

Rotational Equilibrium · 1 of 3

Balance means two conditions, not one.

Translation balance

ΣF = 0 N

Forces cancel. The center of mass has no linear acceleration.

Rotation balance

Στ = 0 N·m

Clockwise and counterclockwise torques cancel about the chosen pivot.

Static equilibrium: ΣF = 0 N and Στ = 0 N·m.

This is the central condition for the week. A meter stick can have zero net force and still rotate if the torques do not balance. Conversely, torques can balance about one point while the object translates if the net force is not zero. Use the word "and" deliberately. Students should write both conditions at the top of every equilibrium problem.

Rotational Equilibrium · 2 of 3

Choose a sign convention and keep it.

+
counterclockwise
A force that tends to lift the left side or push the right side down can be positive if you choose CCW positive.
−
clockwise
A force that tends to lift the right side or push the left side down is negative under the same convention.
The equation

Στ = τCCW + τCW = 0 N·m

The choice is arbitrary. Consistency is not.

Worked Example · Setup

A meter stick balances a hanging mass.

A 1.00 m meter stick is supported at its center. A downward force of 4.0 N acts at 0.20 m to the left of the pivot. What downward force at 0.40 m to the right balances the stick?

Before the algebra
  1. Choose the pivot and a positive rotation direction.
  2. Draw the two lever arms and identify the torque signs.
  3. Write both equilibrium conditions; the vertical support force handles the net force.

Target: the unknown force in N.

Worked Example · KDEA

Left torque must equal right torque.

StepWhat you write
KrL = 0.20 m, FL = 4.0 N, rR = 0.40 m; both forces are perpendicular, so sin 90° = 1
Dright-side force FR, target unit N
EΣτ = 0 N·m: rLFL = rRFR
Asymbols first: FR = rLFL / rR
FR = (0.20 m)(4.0 N) / 0.40 m = 2.0 N

The farther force needs less magnitude: FR = 2.0 N.

This is the house KDEA pattern students should reproduce. The support force is not needed to solve the torque equation because the pivot was chosen at the center and the support acts through that pivot, giving zero torque. Do not call the answer 2.0 joules; torque units are N·m and force units are N. The result is a proportional-reasoning check: doubling the lever arm halves the balancing force.

Quick Check · Whiteboard · 5 minutes

Which setup balances?

Do not calculate yet
  1. A 6.0 N downward force acts 0.30 m left of a pivot. A second downward force acts 0.15 m right of the pivot. Which side must the second force be on to balance?
  2. For the same first force, is the balancing force larger or smaller than 6.0 N? Explain using the lever arms.
  3. What changes if the second force is at 45° instead of 90°?

Talk in torque language: direction, lever arm, perpendicular component.

Do not reveal numerical answers on the student slide. Listen for the proportional explanation: the 0.15 m side is closer, so it needs a larger force; at 45 degrees, only F·sin45 degrees contributes, so the force must increase again for the same torque. Students who compare raw forces without comparing r are missing the central idea.

Hands-On Stations

Make the balance visible.

See-saw · meter stick · wrench

This divider is the transition from formal model to physical evidence. Tell students that each station changes one part of the torque relationship: the see-saw emphasizes distance and balance, the meter stick makes both equilibrium conditions visible, and the wrench emphasizes angle and perpendicular force. The written claim at every station should include a sketch, a measurement, and units.

Station A · See-Saw

Balance the turning effects.

Your station card
  1. Choose a pivot and place one mass at a measured distance r1.
  2. Predict where a second mass must go using r1F1 = r2F2.
  3. Test. Record both lever arms in m and both weights in N.
  4. Explain any mismatch: measurement, friction, or the stick's own weight?

Claim frame: “The see-saw balanced because Στ ≈ 0 N·m about the pivot.”

Station B · Meter Stick

Equilibrium requires force balance too.

Torque question

Do the clockwise and counterclockwise moments cancel about the support?

Στ = 0 N·m

Force question

Do the upward support and downward weights cancel?

ΣFy = 0 N

Move the support point until the stick balances. Then check whether your measured center of mass and the torque model agree.

A stick that does not rotate is not automatically in full equilibrium.

The station is designed to expose the distinction between net force and net torque. The support force can make the vertical forces sum to zero, but the support location determines whether the torques sum to zero. Ask students to name which condition each observation tests: no linear acceleration tests force; no angular acceleration tests torque.

Station C · Wrench

Angle changes the usable force.

90°
perpendicular pull
All of the force contributes: τ = rF·sin 90° = rF, in N·m.
30°
angled pull
Only the perpendicular component contributes: τ = rF·sin 30°, in N·m.
Measure and compare

Use the same wrench length r and the same force F. Pull at two angles. Which trial produces the larger torque? Make a prediction before testing.

Worked Example · Wrench KDEA

Why a glancing pull needs more force

A wrench has a 0.25 m lever arm. A pull of 40 N acts at 30° to the lever arm. Find the torque.

StepWhat you write
Kr = 0.25 m, F = 40 N, θ = 30°
Dtorque τ, target unit N·m
Eτ = rF·sinθ
Aτ = (0.25 m)(40 N)·sin 30°
τ = (0.25 m)(40 N)(0.50) ≈ 5.0 N·m

At 90°, the same r and F would produce 10 N·m.

This is the only trig calculation students need this week: a right triangle and sin 30 degrees. Make the comparison explicit: the angled pull produces half the torque of the perpendicular pull. Do not let students use cosine by pattern matching; ask which component is perpendicular to the lever arm, then draw the triangle that makes the sine relationship visible.

Common Traps

Four ways torque problems go wrong.

1
wrong distance
Using the full stick length instead of the pivot-to-force distance r.
2
wrong angle
Using the angle to the table instead of the angle between r and F.
3
lost sign
Adding torque magnitudes without labeling clockwise and counterclockwise.
4
one condition
Claiming equilibrium from Στ = 0 N·m while ignoring ΣF = 0 N.

Sketch first. Label the pivot, the lever arm, the force angle, and the sign.

These are the failure modes to listen for while circulating. The most common is using the wrong r: it must be the perpendicular distance or the pivot-to-force geometry specified by the equation, not an arbitrary object length. The second is silently treating all torques as positive. Require arrows and signs on station cards before students calculate.

Exit Ticket · 4 minutes

One claim, one equation, one unit.

Answer on your own
  1. A student pushes a door with the same 20 N force first near the hinge and then at the handle. Which push produces greater torque? Explain using r.
  2. Write the rotational equilibrium condition with units.
  3. Write the second condition required for static equilibrium with units.

Turn in the reasoning, not just the choice.

Collect this as a fast diagnostic, not a graded quiz. Look for three phrases: farther from the pivot, net torque equals zero N·m, and net force equals zero N. If a student writes only "the handle" without naming r, the concept is still descriptive rather than quantitative. Use responses to group students for Thursday's longer practice.
