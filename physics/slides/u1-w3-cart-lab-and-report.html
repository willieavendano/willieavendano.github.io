The Lab

Constant-Acceleration Cart

How does position change with time on an accelerating cart?

This lab is the physical version of everything they've done on paper for two weeks. The cart on the ramp is the exact setup previewed in Unit 0's KDEA Example 3 — except now it accelerates instead of rolling at constant speed. Students should feel the callback.

The Lab · 1 of 3

The question we're answering today

A cart rolls down a ramp under constant acceleration. What is the relationship between its position and time?

What we measure
  • Position (x, in m) — where the cart is at each moment
  • Time (t, in s) — the instants when we record those positions

From those two measurements, you can extract velocity (slope of x–t) and acceleration (slope of v–t). The four kinematics equations predict what that data should look like — your job is to see if they're right.

You are not "proving" the equations. You are testing whether a model (constant a) fits real data.

Frame the lab as model-testing, not proof. The phrase "you are not proving the equations" is deliberate — physics doesn't prove models, it tests their predictions against measurement. This mental model matters for every lab this year.

The Lab · 2 of 3

How we run it

Procedure — each group needs
  1. Set up the track at a shallow angle. Mark position intervals (every 20 cm or so) with tape.
  2. Release the cart from rest at the top. Start the timer at the moment of release.
  3. Record the time the cart reaches each tape mark. Run three trials per mark — average them.
  4. Enter your data directly into a table: position (m) vs average time (s).

One person releases. One person times. One person records. Rotate roles between trials — everyone touches every job.

Three trials, not one. No measurement is reliable with a single pass.

Emphasize the three-trial rule — it's the lab version of "no measurement is exact" and the basis for the uncertainty conversation later. Rotating roles is non-negotiable: every student should have timed, released, and recorded by the end of the period.

The Lab · 3 of 3

Run the lab

In your group — 25 minutes

Set up the track, run your three trials, and fill in your data table. You need at least five position points — more is better. The last five minutes are for a quick rough graph: just plot your points on graph paper, don't draw any lines yet.

Before you pack up: does your x–t plot look curved? It should — constant acceleration makes a parabola. If it looks like a straight line, your ramp may be too flat.

Circulate and check the rough plots before cleanup. A straight-line x-t means the cart isn't accelerating enough — the track angle may need to be steeper, or friction is dominating. Catching this now prevents a whole group from building a report on bad data.

The Write-up

Lab Report I

Your first formal report — and we build it together

This is the pivot from running the lab to writing about it. "We build it together" is the scaffolding promise: five lab reports this year, fading from fully guided (this one) to fully independent (Report V in Unit 7). Every section that follows is a piece of that scaffold.

Lab Report I · 1 of 5

The anatomy of a lab report

Every report you write this year follows the same seven-part structure. This one we fill in together.

Question → Hypothesis → Materials → Procedure → Data → Analysis → Conclusion
seven sections, one story
The Question is what you're trying to find out. The Hypothesis is your prediction — before you touch the track. The rest is how you tested it and what you found.

Today we focus on the three sections where most points live and most mistakes hide: Data, Analysis, and Conclusion.

Don't belabor the Materials and Procedure sections — they exist but are the least-weighted parts of the rubric for Report I. The money is in Data (tables done right), Analysis (graphs that mean something), and Conclusion (connecting the result back to the hypothesis). Students who spend 20 minutes writing a beautiful procedure paragraph and 5 minutes on their data table have the priorities backward — flag it now.

Lab Report I · 2 of 5

Question through Procedure: the setup

Q
Question
One sentence. What are you trying to find out? "What is the relationship between position and time for a cart accelerating from rest down a ramp?"
H
Hypothesis
Your prediction before the experiment. "If acceleration is constant, then the x–t graph should be a parabola, and x should be proportional to t2."
M
Materials
A bulleted list. Cart, track, ramp support, meter stick, stopwatch, tape. Be specific — "meter stick" not "ruler."
P
Procedure
Numbered steps. Someone who wasn't there should be able to replicate your experiment from this section alone.

Four sections, one rule: someone who wasn't in the room should be able to read your report and redo the experiment.

Data tables done right

A data table is not a scratch pad. Every cell, every header, every digit matters.

Four non-negotiables
  • Units live in the header, not in every cell. The header says Position x (m) — cells are just numbers.
  • Consistent significant figures. If your meter stick reads to 0.01 m, every position gets two decimal places — even 1.00, not 1.
  • One measurement per cell. Raw data and averages get separate columns. No slashes, no “0.68 / 0.71 / 0.65” in one box.
  • Units-travel-with-the-math applies here too. The header carries the unit so the column makes sense on its own.
This is the most-graded slide in the deck. Every single one of these rules shows up on the rubric. The sig-figs rule in particular — students lose points for "0.2" instead of "0.20" when the instrument reads to 0.01. Show them the example table and ask them to spot the difference between a cell that says "1" and a cell that says "1.00" — the zeros aren't decoration, they're precision claims.

The Analysis

Graphs That Earn Their Place

Axis labels, best-fit lines, and what the slope means

This divider opens the Analysis section — the highest-weighted part of the Report I rubric. Every graph they make this year will be graded on the same four criteria: labeled axes with units, data points visible, a best-fit line (not connect-the-dots), and a sentence saying what the slope means physically.

Graphs · 1 of 2

Four things every graph in this course must have

1
Axes labeled with units
Not "x" and "t" — write Position x (m) and Time t (s). The unit lives in parentheses, right next to the variable name, exactly like the data table headers.
2
Data points you can see
Plot each point as a visible dot or small circle. Don't hide them under a thick line. The reader needs to see your raw measurements before they see your interpretation.
3
A best-fit line — not connect-the-dots
A straight line (or smooth curve) that passes near the points, capturing the trend. It does not have to touch every dot — real data scatters. Connect-the-dots hides the uncertainty.
4
A sentence: what the slope means
"The slope of this x–t2 graph is 0.51 m/s2, which is half the acceleration — matching a ≈ 1.02 m/s2." A number without an interpretation is just a number.

A graph without labeled axes is not a graph. It's a doodle.

Tile 3 is the conceptual leap — students want to connect the dots with a ruler because it "looks right." Best-fit line is a harder idea (you're drawing a line that misses some points on purpose) but it's the one that earns full credit. Tie it to the uncertainty conversation coming up: the scatter IS the uncertainty made visible.

Graphs · 2 of 2

Which graph answers your question?

Constant acceleration predicts x ∝ t2. So plotting x vs t should give a parabola. Plotting x vs t2 should give a straight line — and the slope of that line tells you ½a.

x vs t

Curved (parabola). Hard to extract a slope from a curve by hand. Good for seeing the shape of the relationship — shows acceleration is happening.

x vs t2

Straight line — if acceleration is constant. Add a column to your data table for t2. The slope = ½a, so a = 2 × slope.

Your report needs both: the x–t graph (to show the shape) and the x–t2 graph (to extract the acceleration from the slope). Two graphs, one conclusion.

Linearizing the data — turning a curve into a straight line so you can read the slope — is a skill you will use in every lab this year.

Linearization is the hidden star of this slide. For Report I, we tell them which graph to plot (x vs t²). For later reports, they'll have to figure out the linearization themselves. Planting the concept now — "add a column for t² and replot" — builds the instinct. The "two graphs, one conclusion" line prevents students from stopping after the parabola.

Activity

Sketch the graph you expect

With your group — 5 minutes

Before you plot your real data, sketch what the x vs t2 graph should look like if acceleration is constant:

  1. Draw and label your axes with units.
  2. Sketch the shape you predict (straight line through the origin? Straight line with a non-zero intercept? Curved?).
  3. Write one sentence: "If my data matches this shape, it means ______."

Now plot your real data on the same axes in a different color. Does the prediction match?

This is a hypothesis-on-graph-paper exercise — the hypothesis section of the report, drawn instead of written. It forces students to commit to a prediction before they see whether their data agrees. If the data doesn't match, that's not a failure — it's the most interesting part of the lab, and exactly what the Conclusion section is for.

The Fine Print

Uncertainty

No measurement is exact — and your report needs to say so

At this level (algebra-based, not AP), uncertainty means three things: significant figures as precision claims, the ± notation for ranges, and the acknowledgment that no measurement is perfect. Error propagation (adding uncertainties in quadrature, partial derivatives) is NOT part of this course. Keep it to those three ideas.

Uncertainty · 1 of 2

Three honest things your report needs to say about uncertainty

1
Significant figures ARE your precision claim
Writing 0.68 s says "I'm confident to ±0.01 s." Writing 0.7 s says "I'm confident to ±0.1 s." The digits you keep or drop make a claim about your instrument. Don't claim more precision than your stopwatch earned.
2
Use ± to show the spread in your measurements
If your three trials for one position gave 0.68 s, 0.71 s, and 0.65 s, your average is 0.68 s ± 0.03 s. The ± is half the range: (max − min) / 2. This is a rough estimate, and rough is enough for Report I.
3
No measurement is exact — and that's fine
The stopwatch has human reaction time. The meter stick has its own precision limit. The track might not be perfectly level. Your job is to name those sources — one sentence in the Conclusion — not to make them disappear.

You are not doing error propagation in this course. You ARE expected to be honest about what your instruments can and cannot do.

Tile 2 is the operational definition of ± for this course: half the range. It's not the statistically correct standard deviation, and that's on purpose — Report I doesn't need it. Tile 3 is where students name their uncertainty sources (reaction time, track friction, meter stick precision) in the Conclusion. The chip is explicit about no error propagation — say it out loud so no student burns time on it.

Uncertainty · 2 of 2

Where uncertainty lives in your report

In the Data section

Your table shows the raw spread — three trials, plus the average with its ± range. The scatter in your graph IS the uncertainty made visible.

In the Conclusion

One paragraph: did your data support the hypothesis? If your slope gives a ≈ 1.02 m/s2 with an uncertainty of ± 0.08 m/s2, say so — and name at least two sources of that uncertainty (reaction time, track friction, measurement precision).

Uncertainty is not a confession of failure. It's the part of the report that says "I understand what my tools can do."

The Protocol in the Lab

A Lab IS a KDEA Cycle

You've been running this protocol on paper. Now run it on data.

The Deliverable

What's Due & When

And what a strong Report I looks like

Report I · Expectations

What a strong Lab Report I looks like

✓
Data table with units in headers
Three trials per position, average column, consistent sig figs. No slashes, no units in cells.
✓
Two graphs, both labeled
x–t (parabola) and x–t2 (straight line). Axes have variable names and units. Best-fit line on the linearized graph.
✓
Slope interpreted
"The slope is 0.51 m/s2, so a = 2 × slope ≈ 1.02 m/s2." A number alone is not analysis. The sentence connecting slope to acceleration IS the analysis.
✓
Uncertainty named and discussed
At least two sources named (reaction time, friction, measurement precision). One sentence about how they might affect the result. No error propagation required.
✓
Conclusion connects back to hypothesis
"The x–t2 graph was linear, supporting the hypothesis that acceleration was constant. The extracted acceleration was 1.02 m/s2 ± 0.08 m/s2."
✓
Full report, not just the data
All seven sections present. Question, Hypothesis, Materials, Procedure, Data, Analysis, Conclusion. A data table alone is half a report.
