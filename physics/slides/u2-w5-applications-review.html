Warm-Up · Setup

A crate pushed across a warehouse floor

A 10 kg crate is pushed with a horizontal force of 45 N. The floor has μk = 0.25. Find the crate's acceleration.

The plan
  1. Find N from vertical equilibrium.
  2. Find fk from N and μk.
  3. Run ΣF = ma horizontally, with friction opposing the push.

Convention: the direction of the push is positive.

Warm-Up · KDEA

m = 10 kg, Fa = 45 N, μk = 0.25

StepWhat you write
Km = 10 kg, Fa = 45 N, μk = 0.25
Dacceleration a (target unit: m/s2)
EN = mg; fk = μkN; ΣF = Fa − fk = ma
AN = (10 kg)(9.8 m/s2) = 98 N
fk = (0.25)(98 N) = 24.5 N
a = (45 N − 24.5 N) / 10 kg = 20.5 N / 10 kg = 2.05 m/s2

Friction always subtracts from the applied force in the direction of motion — the exact same layout from Week 2.

Point out this is identical in structure to Week 2's kinetic-friction worked example — same three moves every time: find N, find f, then ΣF = ma. This is the layout students should be able to reproduce without hesitation on Thursday.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 8 minutes

A crate sliding down a ramp

A 4.0 kg crate slides down a 25° ramp with μk = 0.15. Find its acceleration down the slope.

With your partner
  1. Tilt your axes with the ramp.
  2. Write N in terms of mg and θ — not just mg.
  3. Combine the parallel component of gravity and friction into one ΣF = ma.

Mass will cancel out of the final equation — watch for it, don't force it.

Circulate for the two classic incline errors: (1) using N = mg instead of mg cos θ, and (2) putting sine where cosine belongs or vice versa. The sanity checks from Week 4 (θ = 0° and θ = 90°) are the fix if a group gets stuck. Answer preview: a ≈ 2.81 m/s².

Practice · KDEA

θ = 25°, μk = 0.15 — find a

StepWhat you write
Kθ = 25°, μk = 0.15, m = 4.0 kg (cancels out)
Dacceleration a, down the slope (target unit: m/s2)
EN = mg cosθ; fk = μkmg cosθ; ΣF = mg sinθ − μkmg cosθ = ma → a = g(sinθ − μkcosθ)
Aa = (9.8)(sin 25° − 0.15 · cos 25°)
a = (9.8)(0.4226 − 0.15 × 0.9063)
a = (9.8)(0.4226 − 0.1359) = (9.8)(0.2867) ≈ 2.81 m/s2

The ≈ is honest — sin 25° and cos 25° are irrational, so every step after them carries the rounding.

A block, a table, and a hanging mass

A 6.0 kg block sits on a table with μk = 0.20, connected by a string over a pulley to a 4.0 kg mass hanging off the edge.

With your partner

  2. System view: find the acceleration.
  3. Individual view (hanging mass): find the tension.

This is Week 4's hardest problem type, back for one more round — systems AND friction together.

This is deliberately the most demanding problem of the week — combining the systems view from Week 4 with the friction mechanics from Week 2, minus the incline this time (for variety against Week 4's capstone, which combined systems with an incline instead of friction). The hanging mass's weight (39.2 N) exceeds the table block's max static friction ceiling before you even compute a — that comparison is what justifies assuming motion happens at all. The verification check at the bottom is worth doing live; it's free confidence that both views agree.

The Big Picture

Unit 2 is one story, not five topics.

Forces & FBDs → the three laws → friction → systems & inclines

This divider sets up the concept map. The biggest failure mode on the test is treating FBDs, the three laws, friction, and systems/inclines as separate things to memorize. They aren't — every one of them is the same ΣF = ma applied to a progressively richer picture.

The Big Picture · 1 of 1

One tool, five applications

Everything in Unit 2 is the same chain. Each step inherits everything from the step before it:

1
Free-body diagrams
Isolate the object, draw every force acting ON it. This is where every problem starts.
2
First & second laws
ΣF = 0 means equilibrium; otherwise ΣF = ma. This is the equation you always end up running.
3
Third law & friction
Every force pairs with one on another object. Friction adds one more force to the FBD: fs ≤ μsN, fk = μkN.
4
Systems & inclines
More objects (system view + individual view) or tilted axes (mg sinθ, mg cosθ). Same ΣF = ma, richer pictures.

There is no separate "incline equation" or "system equation." There's ΣF = ma, run on a better diagram. That's the whole unit.

Each tile builds on the one before it. If a student can draw an FBD (tile 1) and apply ΣF = ma (tile 2), then friction (tile 3) is just one more arrow on the diagram, and systems/inclines (tile 4) is just a harder diagram with the same equation. The chip is the takeaway — say it aloud and leave it on screen.

Common Test Traps · 1 of 4

Trap #1: Friction's direction depends on motion, not on your push

Friction always opposes relative sliding — not the direction you happened to push from.

The trap

A box is already sliding to the right. You give it a small push to the left — too small to actually reverse its motion. Friction still points left, opposing the box's actual motion (rightward) — the same direction as your push, not against it.

Before writing a friction term, ask: which way is the object actually moving (or trying to move)? That's the direction friction opposes — always.

Common Test Traps · 2 of 4

Trap #2: Units must travel with the math

An answer without units does not count. This is science, not math. Two unit facts trip people up this unit specifically:

The test-day check
  • Force is always in N = kg·m/s2. If your final unit isn't that, an algebra step went missing.
  • μ has no unit — it's a ratio of two forces. Writing "μ = 0.25 N" is always wrong.

A number with no unit — or the wrong unit — on a KDEA problem earns zero credit. Full stop.

Common Test Traps · 3 of 4

Trap #3: N is not automatically mg

N comes from vertical equilibrium on the FBD. It only equals mg when gravity is the only vertical force on a flat surface.

Breaks the shortcut

An angled pull with a vertical component (Week 2). A tilted surface, where N = mg cosθ (Week 4).

The check

Before writing N = mg, ask: is anything else pushing or pulling vertically, or is the surface tilted? If yes, rebuild N from ΣFvertical = 0 instead.

Common Test Traps · 4 of 4

Trap #4: Picking the wrong view on a system problem

You cannot find both a and T from the same single equation — they need two different diagrams:

WantUseWhy
acceleration asystem viewTension is internal — it cancels, leaving only external forces
tension Tindividual viewT is external to a single object — it has to reappear to be solved for

Find a with the system. Find T with one object. Every system problem, every time.

Confidence Check · Turn & Talk · 10 minutes

One last problem before Thursday

With your partner

An 8.0 kg sled is pulled across snow by a rope at 20° above horizontal with tension 25 N. The snow has μk = 0.10.

  1. Find the normal force — remember the rope has a vertical component.
  2. Find the kinetic friction force.
  3. Does the sled accelerate? If so, find how fast.

This is the exact shape of a test problem, with two of this unit's biggest ideas stacked in one scenario.

This closer deliberately combines Trap #3 (N ≠ mg when a pull has a vertical component, from Week 2) with a standard friction-and-acceleration solve. Full solution: N = mg − T sinθ = 78.4 − 25(sin20°) = 78.4 − 8.55 ≈ 69.85 N. f_k = μkN ≈ 6.99 N. Horizontal component of T = 25cos20° ≈ 23.49 N. Net force ≈ 23.49 − 6.99 ≈ 16.50 N. a = 16.50 / 8.0 ≈ 2.06 m/s². Have tables report their acceleration and confirm the sled does move — the judgment step matters as much as the number.

Recap

Screenshot this

Unit 2, in four sentences
  • FBDs & the first two laws — isolate the object; ΣF = 0 means constant velocity, ΣF = ma otherwise.
  • Third law & friction — every force pairs with an equal, opposite force on a different object; fs ≤ μsN, fk = μkN.
  • Systems — connected objects share one a; system view for a, individual view for T.
  • Inclines — tilt the axes; mg sinθ along the slope, mg cosθ into the surface, N = mg cosθ.
