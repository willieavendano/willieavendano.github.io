The Challenge

Design & Build a Working Circuit

Something that lights up, sounds off, or moves — on your own design

Frame this as genuinely open-ended within the constraints on the next slides — an LED array, a simple alarm, a fan-speed controller, a charging indicator. The goal is a functioning circuit that the group can explain and defend, not a specific pre-built kit.

The Brief

What you're building

In groups of two or three, design and build a circuit that does something observable — lights an LED, sounds a buzzer, spins a small motor, or some combination. Your circuit must use both series and parallel wiring, and you must be able to explain every component choice using this unit's physics.

The circuit doesn't have to be complicated. It has to be yours, and you have to be able to explain it.

"It has to be yours" is the key phrase — this isn't about copying a kit schematic off the internet without understanding it. A simple two-LED circuit that a group fully understands and can justify beats a complicated circuit copied from a tutorial that nobody in the group can explain.

Constraints & Requirements

Freedom Inside a Frame

Constraints aren't obstacles to the project, they're what makes it a physics project instead of a craft project. Every constraint on the next slide ties directly back to something taught in Weeks 1–3 of this unit.

Requirements

What your circuit must include

🔋
Safe voltage: 9 V or less
One or two batteries, nothing from the wall. Low voltage means low risk while you're learning to build.
🔗
Both series and parallel sections
At least one purely-series branch and one purely-parallel branch — this is a combination circuit, on purpose.
Ω
At least one Ohm's-law-justified choice
Pick at least one resistor value using a calculation, not a guess — and be ready to show the KDEA.
✅
A visible or measurable output
Something the audience can see happen — a light, a sound, a motion — or a multimeter reading that proves it works.

Four requirements, not forty — the rest of the design is your call.

Each requirement maps directly onto a unit topic: 9 V limit is a safety application of Ohm's law (low V keeps I manageable through typical resistances), series+parallel is Week 2's combination circuits, the Ohm's-law-justified resistor is Week 1's V=IR, and the visible output is what makes the presentation demo possible. Point this mapping out explicitly if students ask "why these rules."

The Design Process

Define. Calculate. Build. Test.

The same loop, every time you design anything

This four-step loop is the E step of KDEA scaled up to a whole project — it's worth saying that explicitly. Students who've internalized KDEA all year already have the mental model for this; they just haven't applied it to something with no single correct answer before.

Design Process · 1 of 2

Define the function, then calculate

Step 1 — Define

What will your circuit do? Pick something concrete: an LED that lights up, a buzzer that sounds, a small motor that spins, or a combination. Sketch the circuit diagram before you touch a breadboard.

Step 2 — Calculate

Every component has a rating (an LED's forward voltage, a motor's operating current). Use Ohm's law and KDEA to choose resistor values that keep every component in its safe range.

A circuit diagram with numbers on it is your Step 2 deliverable — due Monday, before any building starts.

The "diagram before breadboard" rule prevents the most common project failure mode: groups who start wiring before they've decided what they're building, then can't explain their own circuit at presentation time. Hold the line on requiring the sketch-and-calculate step to be done first.

Design Process · Worked Example

Choosing a resistor for an LED

An LED has a forward voltage of 2.0 V and needs about 20 mA of current to light safely without burning out. Your battery supplies 9.0 V. What resistor do you need in series with the LED?

StepWhat you write
KVbattery = 9.0 V, VLED = 2.0 V, I = 20 mA = 0.020 A
Dseries resistor R (target unit: Ω)
Ethe resistor takes the leftover voltage: R = (Vbattery − VLED) / I
AR = (9.0 − 2.0) / 0.020 = 350 Ω

Off-the-shelf resistors come in standard values, not exact ones — you'd grab a 330 Ω or 390 Ω, not a custom 350 Ω. That rounding choice is engineering judgment, not a KDEA step.

This worked example is the model for the "at least one Ohm's-law-justified choice" requirement — every group should be able to reproduce a table like this for their own component. The standard- value rounding note is a genuine, useful engineering fact: point out that rounding UP the resistor value (390 Ω over 330 Ω) is the safer choice, since it lowers current below the target rather than risking overdriving the LED.

Design Process · 2 of 2

Build, test, and troubleshoot

Step 3 — Build & Test

Build on a breadboard, exactly like last week's lab. Check your circuit with a teacher before powering it on. Use the multimeter to verify voltage and current match your calculations.

Step 4 — Troubleshoot & Iterate

If it doesn't work the first time, that's normal — not a failure. Use the multimeter to find where the circuit stops matching your prediction, and fix that one section.

Nobody's first build works perfectly. What matters is whether you can find and fix the problem.

Explicitly normalize failure here — a group whose LED doesn't light on the first try is having a completely typical experience, not a bad one. The troubleshooting slide that follows gives them the specific diagnostic tools; this slide just sets the expectation that they'll need those tools.

Troubleshooting

Four things that go wrong — and how to spot them

✖
Open circuit
Nothing works at all. Check every connection for a loose wire or a bad breadboard row — a multimeter's continuity check finds this fast.
🔥
Short circuit
A component gets hot, or the battery drains instantly. Two points that shouldn't touch are connected — usually a stray wire.
Ω
Wrong resistor value
The LED is too dim, too bright, or the motor is too slow. Re-check your Ohm's law calculation and measure the resistor's actual value.
⇄
Reversed polarity
An LED that won't light might just be backwards — LEDs only conduct one direction. Flip it and retest before assuming it's broken.
This is the practical diagnostic checklist students will actually reach for on Thursday. Walk each one with a quick physical demo if possible — especially reversed LED polarity, which is the single most common "my circuit is broken" report that turns out not to be a wiring problem at all.

The Presentation

What a Good Presentation Looks Like

The physics matters as much as the demo

This divider sets up the final block — students often assume "it lights up" is the whole bar for success. The next two slides make clear the physics explanation is graded just as heavily as the functioning demo itself.

Presentations · 1 of 2

Four things every presentation needs

1
Explain the function
What does your circuit do, and why did you design it that way? One clear sentence, up front.
2
Show your KDEA
The calculation behind at least one component choice, in the same K/D/E/A format you've used all year.
3
Live demo
The circuit actually working, in front of the class — not a photo or a video of it working earlier.
4
Name one failure and the fix
What went wrong at some point, how you diagnosed it, and what you changed. This is expected, not a confession.

A working circuit with no explanation is half a presentation. A calculation with no working demo is the other half.

Tile 4 deserves emphasis — students sometimes want to hide the fact that something didn't work right away, worried it looks like failure. Reframe explicitly: naming a real troubleshooting story is a REQUIRED part of the rubric, not an admission that costs points. A group with a clean first-try build should find something else worth explaining (a design tradeoff, a component swap they considered).
