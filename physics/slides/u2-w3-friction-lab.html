The Lab

Static & Kinetic Friction

What are μs and μk for this block, on this table?

Concrete, answerable question — no textbook value applies until it's been measured for these exact surfaces. That specificity is the point: μ is a property of a surface PAIR, not a universal constant.

The Lab · 1 of 3

The question we're answering today

A wooden block sits on the lab table. What are the coefficients of static and kinetic friction between this block and this table?

What we measure
  • Normal force (N, in N) — from the block's total weight, which you'll vary
  • Pulling force (F, in N) — read off a spring scale as you pull the block

From those two measurements at several different weights, you'll extract both μs and μk — the same way you extracted acceleration from a slope in Unit 1.

You are measuring a property of two specific surfaces, not looking up a universal constant.

Tie back explicitly to Unit 1's "slope of a graph gives you the physical quantity" habit — today's lab reuses that exact skill, just with force and normal force instead of position and time.

The Lab · 2 of 3

How we run it

Procedure — each group needs
  1. Weigh the block alone. This is your first Normal force, N = weight.
  2. Attach a spring scale to the block with a string. Pull horizontally, slowly increasing the force.
  3. Record the maximum reading right before the block breaks free — that's fs,max.
  4. Once it's sliding, pull it at a slow, constant velocity and record the steady reading — that's fk.
  5. Add a known mass on top of the block to increase N. Repeat steps 2–4.
  6. Collect at least five different Normal-force values.

One block, five weights, two readings each — ten measurements, one coefficient pair.

Step 3 is the hardest to execute cleanly — "the instant before it breaks free" is a judgment call, not a clean instrument reading, and that judgment call itself becomes an honest uncertainty source later in the report. Rotate roles (pulling, reading the scale, recording) the same way the cart lab did.

Lab Report II · 4 of 6

From one row of data, a rough μ

Using just the first row above: mass 0.300 kg, fs,max = 1.15 N. Estimate μs.

StepWhat you write
Km = 0.300 kg, fs,max = 1.15 N
Dμs (unitless)
EN = mg; fs,max = μsN → μs = fs,max / N
AN = (0.300 kg)(9.8 m/s2) = 2.94 N
μs = 1.15 N / 2.94 N ≈ 0.39

One data point gives a rough μ — but it's only one trial. That's exactly why your report needs all five.

The chip is the pedagogical hinge: a single-point μ estimate is legitimate but fragile, which is the honest argument for why the report requires a full graph, not just one calculation. This sets up the next divider on graphing perfectly.

The Analysis

Linearize It — Again

Plot force vs. Normal force. The slope IS μ.

Same linearization skill from the cart lab (x vs t²), now applied to friction. Naming the parallel explicitly helps students see this as one transferable skill, not two unrelated techniques.

Lab Report II · 5 of 6

Why the slope is μ

fs = μsN and fk = μkN are both straight lines through the origin, if you plot f vs. N — because μ is a constant multiplying N, exactly like y = mx.

Plot 1: fs,max vs. N

Straight line through the origin. The slope of this line is μs.

Plot 2: fk vs. N

Also a straight line through the origin, with a shallower slope — that slope is μk.

Your report needs both graphs, each with a best-fit line and axes labeled with units — same four rules from Report I.

Don't re-teach the "four things every graph needs" checklist from Report I in full — a fast callback is enough. The new idea here is specifically WHY the slope equals μ (because f = μN has the same shape as y = mx), which is worth deriving on the board.

Lab Report II · 6 of 6

Extracting μk from the graph's slope

Using the first and last rows of the sample table: (N = 2.94 N, fk = 0.88 N) and (N = 6.86 N, fk = 2.06 N).

StepWhat you write
KN1 = 2.94 N, fk1 = 0.88 N; N2 = 6.86 N, fk2 = 2.06 N
Dμk (unitless) — the slope of the best-fit line
Eμk = slope = Δfk / ΔN
AΔfk = 2.06 N − 0.88 N = 1.18 N, ΔN = 6.86 N − 2.94 N = 3.92 N
μk = 1.18 N / 3.92 N ≈ 0.30

We worked μk together. Extracting μs from your own fs,max data, the same way, is yours to do independently in the report.

The chip is the deliberate independence move for this deck: μ_k is modeled fully in class; μ_s is left for students to compute on their own data using the identical method. This is the "growing independence" theme made concrete, not just stated abstractly on slide 8.

Uncertainty in the Friction Lab

Where the wobble comes from this time

Turn and talk — 4 minutes

With your group, name at least two sources of uncertainty specific to today's setup — not the generic list from Report I. Consider: the spring scale's smallest markings, the judgment call of “right before it slides,” and whether the table surface is the same in every trial.

“The instant it breaks free” is a human judgment, not an instrument reading — that IS a legitimate uncertainty source.

This activity is the independence move for uncertainty: no worked example, no answer key on the slide — just a prompt. Circulate and listen for whether groups can go beyond "human error," which is a Report I habit we're trying to move students past.
