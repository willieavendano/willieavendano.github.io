Why this matters

Workshop I gave you the words. Now you need the moves.

You can now read a unit, use a Greek symbol, write scientific notation, and sanity-check an estimate. That's vocabulary. What's missing is a repeatable move — something you run the same way on a 30‑second warm-up and a 20‑minute lab problem.

That move has a name: KDEA.

Today: one protocol, run four different ways.

Set up the contrast explicitly: Workshop I = fluency in reading/writing physics; Workshop II = what you actually DO with that fluency when a problem lands in front of you. Students who "know" units but freeze on word problems are missing this piece, not the vocabulary piece.

The Protocol

K · D · E · A

“The Way to Solve ANY Problem in the World”

Let the claim be big on purpose — "ANY problem in the world" is the actual framing I use. It's not just hyperbole for the room: K-D-E-A works on physics, on CS Math, on a Fermi estimate, on a word problem you've never seen. The four letters are a checklist, not a formula.

KDEA · 1 of 4

K — what do you KNOW?

List every given the problem hands you. Every single one — and every single one gets a unit written right next to it.

Rule

A number without a unit isn't data. It's noise. If a problem says "a car travels 72," that sentence isn't finished until you write 72 km/h.

KDEA · 2 of 4

D — what DON'T you know?

Name the target. Not "solve for x" — the actual quantity, and just as important, the unit it has to arrive in.

Rule

If you can't name the destination unit before you start, you have no way to check whether your final answer even landed in the right place.

KDEA · 3 of 4

E — what EQUATIONS or models connect them?

This is the bridge between K and D: a formula, a definition, or a conversion factor that ties your knowns to your unknown.

Rule

Sometimes E is a kinematics equation. Sometimes it's just 1 km = 1000 m. Both count. The whole point of writing K and D first is that E usually becomes obvious once they're both on the page.

KDEA · 4 of 4

A — solve the ALGEBRA

Symbols first. Numbers last. Units travel with the math the entire way through — every multiplication, every division, every cancellation.

Rule

Rearrange the equation for your unknown before a single number goes in. Plugging numbers in early is how algebra mistakes hide.

The Core Dogma

Units travel with the math.

An answer without units does not count. This is science, not math.

This is the single most enforced rule in this classroom for the rest of the year. Say it exactly like this every time it comes up: "this is science, not math" — the distinction is deliberate. A correct number with no unit gets zero credit on any KDEA problem, full stop, starting today.

KDEA in Action

Four problems. Same four moves.

  1. The speed limit check — a straight unit conversion
  2. The school year — multi-step, with an estimation crossover
  3. The cart on the track — this previews Unit 1 Kinematics
  4. The trap — what happens when you skip KDEA entirely

Watch the units the whole way through — not just the final number.

Example 1 · Setup

The speed limit check

A car near campus is clocked at 72 km/h. Campus safety wants that speed compared to the 15 m/s limit posted by the pool. Before anyone can compare anything, the units have to match.

Convert 72 km/h to m/s.

Example 1 · KDEA

72 km/h → m/s

StepWhat you write
Kspeed = 72 km/h
Dspeed in m/s (target unit: m/s)
E1 km = 1000 m, 1 h = 3600 s
A72 km/h × (1000 m / 1 km) × (1 h / 3600 s) = 20 m/s

20 m/s > 15 m/s limit — still speeding, even after the "smaller number" looked safe.

Walk the cancellation out loud: km cancels with km, h cancels with h, leaving only m on top and s on bottom — that IS the check that the algebra was set up correctly, before you even multiply. The punch line matters: 72 "looks" bigger than 15, but after conversion the car is still over the limit. Units first, judgment second.

Example 2 · Setup

How many seconds in a school year?

The school year runs 180 instructional days. Nobody hands you an hours-per-day number — you have to estimate one, the way we practiced with Fermi problems in Workshop I, then finish the conversion with KDEA.

Estimate total seconds of instructional time in the school year.

Example 2 · KDEA

180 days → seconds

StepWhat you write
K180 days; ≈ 7 h/day (our estimate)
Dtotal instructional time (target unit: s)
E1 h = 3600 s
A180 days × 7 h/day × 3600 s/h = 4.536 × 106 s

≈ 4.5 × 106 s of class — vs. ≈ 3.15 × 107 s in the full calendar year.

This is the bridge slide between Workshop I and Workshop II: the "7 h/day" number is an honest estimate, not a given — and KDEA's K row is exactly where you write that assumption down instead of hiding it. Point out this is also good scientific notation practice from last workshop. The calendar year comparison (3.15×10⁷ s) is a nice sanity check that "class time" is a small slice of the year.

Example 3 · Setup

The cart on the track

A cart rolls at a constant 5.0 m/s down a 2.3 m track. How long does the crossing take?

This previews Unit 1 Kinematics — same protocol, new equation.

Example 3 · KDEA

d = 2.3 m, v = 5.0 m/s → t = ?

StepWhat you write
Kv = 5.0 m/s, d = 2.3 m
Dcrossing time t (target unit: s)
Ed = v t (constant velocity)
Asymbols first: t = d / v  →  t = 2.3 m / 5.0 m/s = 0.46 s

m cancels with m, leaving only s — the unit confirms the algebra before you trust the number.

This is literally the shape of every kinematics problem for the rest of the semester: rearrange symbols first (t = d/v), THEN substitute numbers with units attached. Flag explicitly: three weeks from now this exact E — d = vt — comes back in Unit 1, just with more letters added to it. KDEA doesn't change; the equation bank grows.

The Trap

What happens when you skip KDEA?

Watch this.

Example 4 · The Trap

3 km + 500 m = ?

The shortcut everyone tries

3 + 500 = 503

503 what? Kilometers? Meters? Both? The instant you add the raw numbers without matching units first, the result stops meaning anything — it isn't even wrong, it's meaningless.

Let this sit uncomfortably for a second before moving on — 503 is a real number that came from real arithmetic, and it's still garbage. That's the entire point: arithmetic can be flawless and the answer can still be nonsense if the units underneath it were never checked.

Example 4 · KDEA Fixes It

Run the protocol instead

StepWhat you write
K3 km, 500 m
Dtotal distance (target unit: pick one — m)
E1 km = 1000 m
Aconvert first: 3 km × 1000 m/km = 3000 m, then add: 3000 m + 500 m = 3500 m

This is why KDEA exists — the D step forces you to pick one unit before the arithmetic, not after.

Connect this directly back to the D definition from slide 5: "name the unit it has to arrive in." That single requirement is exactly what prevents the 3+500=503 error — you're forced to convert to a common unit before you're allowed to add anything. This is the whole reason the protocol has four letters instead of just "do algebra."

Not Just Physics

CS Math runs the same protocol

If KDEA looks familiar from another class, it should — CS Math uses the exact same four letters, in the exact same order, with the exact same expectations.

That's not a coincidence. One protocol, reused across courses, means you're not relearning a new "way to show work" every time you walk into a different room.

This is deliberate program design, not an accident — say so explicitly. Students carrying KDEA between Physics and CS Math should feel zero friction; the letters, the order, and "units/labels travel with the math" all transfer directly. If a student asks "is this the same as CS Math?" the answer is an unqualified yes.
