The Lab

Collision Carts

Build an elastic collision and a perfectly inelastic collision. Compare what conserves.

This divider opens the hands-on portion. The lab is deliberately built around the two "extreme" collision types from last week's tile grid (elastic and perfectly inelastic) because they're the ones a dynamics track can cleanly produce with magnetic bumpers vs. velcro tips. General inelastic collisions will show up on their own, as the imperfect middle ground, once real friction and measurement error enter the picture.

The Lab · 1 of 3

The question you're answering today

Two carts collide on a track. Is total momentum conserved? Is kinetic energy conserved? And does the answer depend on how the carts collide?

What you measure
  • Mass of each cart (kg), including any added mass bars
  • Velocity of each cart immediately before and immediately after the collision (m/s), from a motion sensor or photogate

From those measurements, you calculate momentum and kinetic energy before and after — and let the numbers tell you which collision type you built.

You are not told the collision type in advance. You determine it from your own data.

This mirrors the cart lab's framing from Unit 1 ("you are not proving the equations, you are testing a model") — here the model being tested is the classification scheme itself. The chip is the key difference from last week's worked examples: there, the collision type was given up front; here, students have to work backward from data to a classification, which is a meaningfully different (and harder) skill.

The Lab · 2 of 3

Two collision setups, same measurements

Elastic setup
Magnetic bumpers face the carts apart — they repel on contact without touching. Launch cart 1 at a stationary cart 2; record both velocities before and after.
Perfectly inelastic setup
Velcro tips face the carts — they lock together on contact. Same launch, but now there's only one final velocity to record, shared by both carts.

Run at least three trials of each setup. If time allows, vary the mass of one cart between trials — add a mass bar and see whether the outcome changes.

Three trials per setup, minimum — same non-negotiable rule as every lab this year. The optional mass-variation extension is for groups that finish early; it's not required for a complete report, but it's an easy way to generate a richer Analysis section for groups aiming higher on the rubric.

The Lab · 3 of 3

How you run it

Procedure — each group needs
  1. Weigh and record each cart's mass (with mass bars) before touching the track.
  2. Set sensors or photogates to capture velocity just before and just after contact.
  3. Three elastic trials (magnetic bumpers), then three perfectly inelastic (velcro tips).
  4. Record every velocity — both carts, before and after — into the table as you go.

One launches · one watches the sensor · one records — rotate roles between trial sets.

No worked numbers appear on this deck for the collision-classification analysis — that's deliberate; Report IV is independent and this is a student-facing deck, so no answer key. Circulate during Tue's session and check that groups are capturing BOTH before and after velocities for BOTH carts — the single most common data-collection miss is only recording the moving cart and assuming the stationary one stays at zero (it usually doesn't, especially in the elastic trials).

Tuesday 2/16 · 60 minutes

Run the lab

In your group

Complete both setups — elastic and perfectly inelastic — with at least three trials each. Leave the last 10 minutes to organize your raw data into a clean table before you leave the room.

Before you pack up: do your "elastic" trials show exactly equal kinetic energy before and after? They probably won't — and that's expected. Real bumpers and real tracks aren't frictionless.

This is the direct payoff of last week's "no real collision is perfectly elastic" point — students should see their own data confirm it: the magnetic-bumper trials will show momentum conserved almost exactly but kinetic energy conserved only approximately, with a small measurable loss. That's a finding, not an error. Flag any group whose momentum totals are wildly off (more than ~15–20%) before they leave — that usually means a missed velocity reading, not real physics.

Report IV · 2 of 4

What a strong Report IV looks like

✓
Complete, honest data tables
Every mass and velocity, every trial, units in the headers, consistent sig figs — the same non-negotiables from Report I, still non-negotiable.
✓
A classification backed by numbers
"This trial was perfectly inelastic" is not enough — show the momentum and KE values that prove it.
✓
Both conservation checks, every trial
Momentum before vs. after, AND kinetic energy before vs. after — for both the elastic and the perfectly inelastic setups.
✓
Uncertainty named, not ignored
Your "elastic" trial almost certainly lost some KE. Name why (friction, bumper imperfection, sensor timing) — don't just report the number and move on.

Report IV · 3 of 4

Self-check before you turn it in

The four mistakes that cost the most points
  • Claiming kinetic energy was conserved in the perfectly inelastic trial — check your own numbers before you claim it.
  • Picking a positive direction and not sticking with it for every velocity in every trial.
  • Reporting a classification with no supporting momentum or KE values next to it.
  • Submitting data and analysis with no Question, Hypothesis, or Conclusion — a data table alone is half a report.

Read your own report as if you're the grader: does every claim have a number backing it up?

This slide is essentially the teacher's grading rubric turned into a student self-check — worth naming out loud that these are the actual four things that cost the most points on Report III last month, so this isn't hypothetical. The sign-convention mistake (bullet 2) is the sneakiest one: a cart moving backward after collision needs a negative velocity, and a missed sign flip will make momentum look unconserved even when it wasn't.
