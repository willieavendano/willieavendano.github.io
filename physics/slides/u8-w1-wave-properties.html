Act I · Wave Properties

Describe the wave.

Shape, timing, and travel are three views of the same motion.

This divider is the conceptual launch. A wave is not a single formula; it is a repeating disturbance that can be described by geometry, time, and motion. Keep asking students which view a quantity belongs to: shape, timing, or travel.

Wave Properties · 1 of 6

A wave is a traveling disturbance.

A wave carries energy from place to place while the medium usually oscillates around an equilibrium position. The material does not have to travel with the disturbance.

Shape
space
How far the medium is displaced from equilibrium and how the pattern repeats.
Timing
time
How often the disturbance repeats: frequency f and period T.
Travel
motion
How quickly the pattern moves: wave speed v.
Use a rope, slinky, or a hand pulse if available. Emphasize energy transfer versus bulk transport: a pulse moves along the rope, but each small segment mostly moves up/down or back/forth around equilibrium. The common failure mode is treating the wave as a blob of material that travels unchanged.

Wave Properties · 2 of 6

Two directions: across or along.

Transverse
medium ⟂ travel
The medium oscillates perpendicular to the direction the wave travels. A rope pulse is the classroom model.
Longitudinal
medium ∥ travel
The medium oscillates parallel to the direction the wave travels. Sound in air is the familiar model.
Do not confuse

Transverse and longitudinal describe the direction of the medium's motion relative to the wave's travel. They do not mean "up/down wave" versus "sound wave" in every possible material.

Students often memorize "transverse = rope" and "longitudinal = sound" without the geometric definition. Put a travel arrow on the board and ask which way one dot of the medium moves. For sound, use compressions and rarefactions rather than a sine-looking transverse sketch.

Wave Properties · 3 of 6

Read the pattern in space.

A
amplitude · m
Maximum displacement from equilibrium. It measures the size of the oscillation, not the distance from crest to trough.
λ
wavelength · m
Distance between matching points in consecutive cycles: crest-to-crest or compression-to-compression.
2A
crest to trough · m
For a symmetric transverse wave, crest-to-trough distance is twice the amplitude.
λ/2
half a cycle · m
Crest-to-midline-to-trough spans half a wavelength, not one full wavelength.
This is the most important diagram-reading slide. Draw equilibrium first, then mark A vertically and lambda horizontally. The failure modes are predictable: calling crest-to-trough the amplitude, and measuring lambda from a crest to the next trough. Require students to say "matching phase" or "same point in the cycle."

Wave Properties · 4 of 6

Read the repetition in time.

Frequency

f = cycles / time

f counts cycles each second. Unit: hertz, Hz = 1/s.

Period

T = time / cycle

T is the time for one cycle. Unit: seconds, s.

They are reciprocals: T = 1/f and f = 1/T.

Use a metronome analogy: frequency is how many beats happen in one second; period is the time between beats. Students routinely invert the relationship or attach Hz to period. Make the units do the checking: 1 divided by seconds gives 1/s, which is Hz; period itself stays in seconds.

Wave Properties · 5 of 6

One cycle travels one wavelength.

If one cycle takes period T and moves a distance λ, then the wave speed is:

Core relationship

v = f·λ = λ/T

Units check: (1/s)·m = m/s. Wave speed is how fast the pattern travels, not how fast one bit of the medium oscillates.

v
wave speed · m/s
How fast the disturbance pattern travels through the medium.
f
frequency · Hz
How often the source repeats.
λ
wavelength · m
How far one cycle occupies in space.
The phrase "one cycle travels one wavelength" gives students a derivation they can remember instead of a formula to chant. Separate source-controlled frequency from medium-controlled speed in the next discussion: changing frequency at fixed medium changes wavelength, but the wave speed is not automatically multiplied by the frequency.

Quick Check · Whiteboard · 6 minutes

Name the property before calculating.

For each statement, write the symbol and unit
  1. A wave makes 12 cycles in 4.0 s.
  2. One cycle takes 0.25 s.
  3. Successive crests are 1.5 m apart.
  4. The pattern travels at 6.0 m/s.

First label it: f, T, λ, or v. Then choose the relationship.

This is deliberately low arithmetic and high classification. Circulate for symbols and units before checking numbers. Students who jump straight to multiplication usually confuse frequency and period. Ask, "Is this counting cycles, timing one cycle, measuring space, or describing travel?" Do not project an answer key; use student boards to surface errors.

Worked Example · KDEA · 1 of 2

A wave's wavelength from speed and frequency

A pulse train travels at 18.0 m/s with frequency 6.0 Hz. What is its wavelength?

StepWhat you write
Kv = 18.0 m/s, f = 6.0 Hz
Dwavelength λ, target unit m
Ev = f·λ
Asymbols first: λ = v/f
λ = (18.0 m/s)/(6.0 1/s) ≈ 3.0 m

The seconds cancel: (m/s)/(1/s) = m.

Model the exact KDEA layout students should use on future wave problems. In the Algebra row, rearrange before substituting. The unit cancellation is the strongest check here: if a student writes m/s as the wavelength unit, they have not divided by the frequency unit. Keep the 1/s form visible at least once.

Act II · Superposition

When waves meet, add them.

Overlap is temporary. The individual waves keep traveling.

This is the bridge from describing one wave to predicting interactions. Students may hear "interference" and think waves are destroyed. State the core idea now: while the waves overlap, their displacements add; after they pass, each disturbance continues.

Superposition · 1 of 4

Superposition is an addition rule.

At any instant, the total displacement is the sum of the displacements caused by each wave at that location:

The model

ytotal = y1 + y2

Use a sign convention: upward displacement is positive and downward displacement is negative, or choose the reverse and stay consistent.

Overlap
during the meeting
The medium's displacement is the algebraic sum at each point.
Pass through
after the meeting
In an ideal linear medium, each pulse continues with its original shape.
Demonstrate with two pulses on a rope or with sketches on a shared axis. The phrase "algebraic sum" matters: direction is encoded by the sign. Students often add magnitudes only, which makes destructive interference impossible. Revisit the medium's equilibrium line as the reference point.

Superposition · 2 of 4

Same sign adds. Opposite sign cancels.

Constructive
same-direction displacement
Positive + positive or negative + negative gives a larger displacement. The combined amplitude can be greater than either pulse alone.
Destructive
opposite-direction displacement
Positive + negative gives a smaller displacement. Equal and opposite displacements can make the instant total equal zero.
Important

"Destructive" means the displacements cancel during overlap. It does not mean the energy permanently disappears.

Use matched pulses first: +2 cm and +2 cm gives +4 cm; +2 cm and −2 cm gives 0 cm. Then vary the sizes so students see that destructive interference does not require perfect cancellation. The common failure mode is saying the wave is gone; ask what the pulses do after they separate.

Superposition · Turn & Talk · 8 minutes

Predict the overlap.

Sketch + explain
  1. Pulse A has displacement +3 cm at a point. Pulse B has displacement +1 cm. What is the total?
  2. Pulse A has displacement +3 cm. Pulse B has displacement −1 cm. What is the total?
  3. Two equal pulses have opposite displacements. What must be true at the instant of perfect overlap?

Use the sign. Name constructive or destructive. Then state what happens after the pulses pass.

This is a concept check, not a hidden answer slide. Have partners draw the equilibrium line and use arrows for signs. Listen for students who call any larger amplitude "constructive" without checking the direction of both displacements. The last prompt is the transfer: total displacement can be zero at one moment while the waves still exist and continue.

Worked Example · KDEA · 2 of 2

Find frequency from period

A sound wave has a period of 2.50 ms. What is its frequency?

StepWhat you write
KT = 2.50 ms = 2.50 × 10−3 s
Dfrequency f, target unit Hz
Ef = 1/T
Af = 1/(2.50 × 10−3 s) = 4.00 × 102 Hz

A short period means many cycles per second: f = 400 Hz.

The trap is milliseconds. Require the conversion to seconds in K before using the reciprocal. This is also an opportunity to connect a qualitative statement to the equation: smaller T means larger f. Keep the answer's unit as Hz, not ms−1, even though they are equivalent descriptions.
