Circles At Planetary Scale

Orbits

Gravity is the centripetal force. Nothing else changes.

Payoff of last week's closing slide. Say it plainly: everything from last week (ac = v²/r, Fc = mv²/r) is unchanged. The only new move is recognizing that Fg already IS Fc for anything orbiting under gravity alone.

Orbits · 1 of 4

Set Fg equal to Fc

The whole derivation, in one line each

GMm / r2 = mv2 / r

The orbiting object's mass, m, appears on both sides — it cancels completely.

v2 = GM / r

Walk the cancellation on the board — it's the single most important algebraic move in this section. M is the mass doing the pulling (the planet/star being orbited); m is the orbiting object and it vanishes. That's not a coincidence — it's why a feather and a hammer "fall" the same way in orbit, same idea as free-fall in Unit 1 where mass also canceled out of g.

Orbits · 2 of 4

v = √(GM / r)

What this formula says
  • Orbital speed depends only on the central mass M and the orbital radius r
  • It does not depend on the orbiting object's own mass
  • Bigger r → smaller v — farther orbits move slower

A pebble and a space station at the same radius orbit at exactly the same speed.

Tie directly back to last week's inverse-square quick check — same instinct, different formula shape. The "farther = slower" result surprises students who expect farther objects to need more speed to "keep up." Emphasize this with the real-world hook on the next slide (low orbit ≈ 7.7 km/s vs. the Moon's much slower ≈ 1 km/s at its much larger radius).

Worked Example · 1 of 2

How fast does a satellite orbit at 300 km altitude?

StepWhat you write
KMearth = 5.97 × 1024 kg, r = Rearth + h = 6.37×106 m + 3.0×105 m ≈ 6.67×106 m, G = 6.674 × 10−11 N·m2/kg2
Dorbital speed v (target unit: m/s)
Ev = √(GMearth / r)
Asymbols first, then substitute:
v = √[(6.674×10−11)(5.97×1024) / (6.67×106)]
v ≈ 7.73 × 103 m/s

That's about 7.7 km/s — roughly Mach 23, and very close to the International Space Station's real orbital speed.

The real-world hook matters — this is the ISS's actual altitude and speed range, not a made-up number. Students who've seen ISS footage now have a physical anchor for what 7.7×10³ m/s means. Note r is a sum (Earth's radius plus altitude) — a common setup error is using the altitude alone as r.

Orbits · 3 of 4

T = 2π√(r3/GM)

Combining two things you already have

Last week: T = 2πr / v. Today: v = √(GM/r). Substitute one into the other and simplify:

T = 2πr / √(GM/r) = 2π√(r3/GM)

Bigger orbit, bigger period — and not just because the circle is longer: it's also moving slower.

This is Kepler's Third Law (T² proportional to r³) falling directly out of Newton's law of gravitation — worth naming Kepler explicitly even though a full historical treatment isn't in this course. It's a nice "the math connects across centuries" moment. Don't require students to reproduce this derivation from scratch on the test; they need the final formula and how to use it, not the algebra steps unprompted.

Worked Example · 2 of 2

How long is one lap at 300 km altitude?

StepWhat you write
Kr = 6.67 × 106 m, v = 7.73 × 103 m/s (from the previous KDEA)
Dperiod T (target unit: s, then convert to minutes)
ET = 2πr / v
Asymbols first: T = 2π(6.67×106 m) / (7.73×103 m/s)
T ≈ 5.42 × 103 s ≈ 90.4 min

The real ISS period is about 92 minutes — our simplified altitude gets us close.

Reusing v from the prior slide's KDEA is intentional — show students that a K row can pull a value forward from a previous table instead of re-deriving it, which is exactly how multi-part test problems will be structured. The unit conversion (s to min) in D is a deliberate callback to Unit 0 dimensional analysis.

Quick Check

On your whiteboard

Reasoning, not calculating
  1. Satellite A orbits closer to Earth than Satellite B. Which one moves faster?
  2. Does orbital speed depend on the satellite's own mass? Why or why not?
Answers: (1) A — v = √(GM/r), so smaller r means larger v. (2) No — m cancels out of F_g = F_c before v is ever solved for; a heavier satellite at the same radius orbits at the same speed as a lighter one. This is the same cancellation walked through on slide 4 — check whether it stuck.

Now: The Whole Unit

One Story, Three Ideas

Centripetal motion → centripetal force → gravity as the force that makes orbits work

Transition into consolidation mode — the single biggest failure mode heading into the test is treating centripetal acceleration, centripetal force, and gravitation as three separate topics to memorize independently. They're one chain, and the concept map on the next slide makes that explicit.

The Big Picture · 1 of 1

Circular motion, one chain

1
Uniform circular motion
Constant speed, changing direction → ac = v2/r, always toward the center. This is where the acceleration comes from.
2
Centripetal force
Newton's 2nd law applied to that acceleration: Fc = mac. Supplied by a real force — tension, friction, gravity, or normal force. This is where F = ma lives.
3
Universal gravitation
Fg = Gm1m2/r2. One specific source of centripetal force — the one that never needs contact. This is where gravity gets its formula.
4
Orbits
Set Fg = Fc and solve: v = √(GM/r), T = 2π√(r3/GM). Two equations, one substitution.

There's no separate "orbit equation." It's F = ma, pointed at a center, with gravity supplying the pull.

Each tile inherits the one before it, same collapsing-four-weeks-into-one-model move as the Unit 1 review deck. If a student understands tile 2 (F=ma at a center) and tile 3 (gravity's formula), tile 4 is just setting them equal — not a new topic. Say the chip aloud and leave it on screen for a beat.

Common Test Traps · 1 of 3

Trap #1: Don't add "centripetal force" to your FBD

Centripetal force is not a force of nature — it's a role. Tension, friction, gravity, or normal force fill that role. Drawing a separate arrow labeled Fc in addition to the real force double-counts it.

The check

After drawing your FBD, ask: "which of these arrows I already drew points toward the center?" That arrow (or the sum of several) is Fc. You never add a new one.

This is the single most common point-loss on circular motion problems, worth repeating from last week's deck almost verbatim because it's that important. Cold-call: "for a car on a flat curve, what force is centripetal?" — the answer is friction, not a separate arrow.

Common Test Traps · 2 of 3

Trap #2: Capital G is not lowercase g

G is the universal gravitational constant — the same number everywhere in the universe. g is Earth's surface acceleration from Unit 1 free fall, ≈ 9.8 m/s2, and it only applies near Earth's surface.

The trap

Students plug 9.8 in for G, or write 6.674×10−11 for g. Check the units before you substitute — G's units are N·m2/kg2; g's units are m/s2. They cannot be interchanged.

Worth mentioning the actual relationship for the curious: g = GM_earth/R_earth² — g is what you GET when you plug Earth-specific numbers into the universal gravitation formula at Earth's surface. That's not required for the test, but it explains why the two symbols are related without being identical.

Common Test Traps · 3 of 3

Trap #3: Doubling r doesn't halve Fg — it quarters it

Gravitation is an inverse-square law, not inverse-linear. The distance is squared in the denominator, so small changes in r produce large changes in Fg.

Quick table
  • r × 2 → Fg ÷ 4
  • r × 3 → Fg ÷ 9
  • r ÷ 2 → Fg × 4

Same trap shape as an inverse-square light or sound problem — square the ratio, don't just scale it.

Same conceptual family as last week's quick check, revisited as a formal trap because it's the single most common algebra error on gravitation problems. If time allows, have students verify one row of the table with actual substitution rather than trusting the pattern blindly.

Midterm Scope · 2 of 2

A problem that borrows from three units

A frictionless banked curve has radius 120 m. At what angle should it be banked so cars traveling 25 m/s need no friction at all?

StepWhat you write
Kv = 25 m/s, r = 120 m, g = 9.8 m/s2
Dbank angle θ (target unit: °)
Evertical: FNcosθ = mg; horizontal: FNsinθ = mv2/r → divide: tanθ = v2/(rg)
Asymbols first: tanθ = (25)2 / [(120)(9.8)] ≈ 0.531
θ = tan−1(0.531) ≈ 28.0°

Forces (Unit 2), vectors and right-triangle trig (Unit 0 Workshop III), and circular motion (Unit 3) — same protocol, same table.

This is the flagship slide of the review — walk it slowly. The vertical/horizontal split of the normal force is a direct callback to projectile decomposition (Unit 1) and inclined-plane FBDs (Unit 2); dividing the two equations to cancel F_N and m simultaneously is worth pointing out as an algebra trick students will reuse. This is exactly the kind of cross-unit problem the midterm favors.

Confidence Check · Turn & Talk · 10 minutes

One last problem before Thursday

With your partner

A 0.20 kg ball on a string moves in a horizontal circle of radius 0.60 m, completing 3 laps every 2.0 s. Find the tension in the string.

  1. Find the period T of one lap.
  2. Find the speed v, then the centripetal acceleration ac.
  3. Find the tension Fc = mac.

This is the exact shape of a test centripetal-force problem — one period, one speed, one force.

Answers: T = 2.0 s / 3 ≈ 0.667 s. v = 2π(0.60)/0.667 ≈ 5.65 m/s. a_c = v²/r = (5.65)²/0.60 ≈ 53.2 m/s². F_c = ma_c = (0.20)(53.2) ≈ 10.6 N. This mirrors the ball-on-a-string KDEA from last week almost exactly — a deliberate confidence builder heading into Thursday. Circulate for the period calculation, which is the one new wrinkle (laps per given time, not time per lap directly).
