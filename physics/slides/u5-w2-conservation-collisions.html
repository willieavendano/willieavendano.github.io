The Big Idea

Conservation of Momentum

In an isolated system, total momentum never changes

State the headline law before deriving it — students should hear the destination before the proof. "Isolated system" is the key phrase to define carefully on the next slide: it doesn't mean nothing is happening, it means no external force is interfering with the objects colliding.

Where It Comes From · 1 of 1

Newton's third law is the reason momentum is conserved

Unit 2 gave you Newton's third law: every force has an equal and opposite reaction. When two objects collide, that law does all the work.

The logic
  1. During the collision, object A pushes on B with force FAB, and B pushes back on A with force FBA = −FAB (Newton's third law).
  2. Both forces act for the same contact time Δt.
  3. By the impulse–momentum theorem, ΔpB = FABΔt and ΔpA = FBAΔt = −FABΔt.
  4. So ΔpA + ΔpB = 0 — whatever momentum A loses, B gains. The total never moves.
This is this week's version of last week's F=ma derivation — walk it step by step on the board. Step 4 is the payoff: the two objects' momentum changes are exactly equal and opposite, so they cancel in the total. Students who see this derivation understand WHY momentum conservation isn't an arbitrary rule — it's Newton's third law plus the impulse–momentum theorem, both already known.

The Law · 1 of 1

ptotal, before = ptotal, after

Definition

m1v1i + m2v2i = m1v1f + m2v2f

  • Isolated system — no significant external force acts during the interaction (friction, air resistance, and outside pushes are ignored or negligible)
  • This holds for any interaction — collisions, explosions, objects pushing apart
  • Individual objects' momenta can change. The total cannot.

This is an identity, not an approximation — write it with =, never ≈.

Emphasize "isolated system" carefully — a cart on a real track still has a tiny bit of friction, so real labs will show momentum "almost" conserved, and that's fine; the law itself is exact for a truly isolated system. The chip about = vs ≈ matters: the conservation LAW itself is an identity (=), but any specific lab measurement of it will report ≈ due to real-world friction and measurement uncertainty — that distinction previews next week's lab write-up expectations.

Worked Example · 1 of 1

Two skaters push apart from rest

A 50 kg skater and a 70 kg skater stand at rest on ice and push off each other. The lighter skater moves off at 3.0 m/s. Find the heavier skater's velocity.

StepWhat you write
Km1 = 50 kg, m2 = 70 kg, v1i = v2i = 0 m/s, v1f = −3.0 m/s
Dv2f, the heavier skater's final velocity (target unit: m/s)
Em1v1i + m2v2i = m1v1f + m2v2f
Asymbols first: 0 = m1v1f + m2v2f  →  v2f = −m1v1f / m2
v2f = −(50 kg)(−3.0 m/s) / 70 kg
v2f ≈ 2.1 m/s

Total momentum starts at zero and stays at zero — the two skaters' momenta are equal in size, opposite in direction.

This is deliberately not a "collision" — it's the reverse, two objects pushing apart from rest — to show the conservation law is broader than just collisions. Pick a sign convention first (call the lighter skater's direction negative here) and stick with it. Students should notice the heavier skater moves slower — same magnitude of momentum, distributed over more mass means less speed.

Quick Check

On your whiteboard

One more push-apart problem

A 4.0 kg cannon (at rest) fires a 0.020 kg cannonball at 300 m/s. Find the cannon's recoil velocity. Why does the cannon barely move compared to the cannonball?

Answer: 0 = (0.020)(300) + (4.0)(v_cannon) → v_cannon = −(0.020)(300)/4.0 = −1.5 m/s. The cannon recoils at only 1.5 m/s despite the cannonball's blistering 300 m/s — same momentum magnitude (6.0 kg·m/s), but 200× the mass means 200× less speed. This is the "why does a gun kick less than a cannonball flies fast" intuition-builder, and a direct callback to slide 6's skater logic.

When Objects Meet

Three Kinds of Collisions

Momentum is always conserved. Kinetic energy is not always so lucky.

This divider sets up the central distinction of the second half of the week: momentum conservation is universal (any isolated system), but kinetic energy conservation is special — it only happens in one of the three collision categories. That asymmetry is the whole reason collisions get classified at all.

Collision Types · Overview

Sorted by what happens to kinetic energy

Elastic
Objects bounce apart. Momentum and kinetic energy are both conserved. An idealization — real collisions only approximate it.
Inelastic
Objects deform, bounce apart, or lose energy to heat and sound. Momentum conserved. Kinetic energy is not.
Perfectly inelastic
Objects stick together and move as one afterward. Momentum conserved. Kinetic energy loss is at its maximum for the collision.

Momentum is conserved in all three. Only elastic collisions conserve kinetic energy too.

This tile grid is the map for the rest of the deck — each tile gets its own detail slide next. "Inelastic" is the umbrella term some students confuse with "perfectly inelastic" specifically; be explicit that perfectly inelastic is a special CASE of inelastic (the sticking-together case), not a separate category floating outside it.

Collision Types · 1 of 3

Elastic: both quantities survive

In a truly elastic collision, objects bounce off each other with no permanent deformation and no energy lost to heat or sound. Both momentum and kinetic energy are the same before and after.

What conserves
  • Momentum: m1v1i + m2v2i = m1v1f + m2v2f
  • Kinetic energy: ½m1v1i2 + ½m2v2i2 = ½m1v1f2 + ½m2v2f2

Real-world examples get close but never perfectly elastic — billiard balls and steel ball bearings are the best everyday approximations.

No real macroscopic collision is perfectly elastic — some energy always escapes as sound or heat, even in the "click" of billiard balls. True elastic collisions mostly show up at the atomic/molecular scale. That honesty matters for next week's lab: even a well-tuned magnetic-bumper cart collision will show a small KE loss, and that's expected, not a failed experiment.

Collision Types · 2 of 3

Perfectly inelastic: they stick, and move as one

A 2000 kg train car moving at 5.0 m/s couples with a stationary 3000 kg car. Find their common velocity after they lock together.

StepWhat you write
Km1 = 2000 kg, v1i = 5.0 m/s, m2 = 3000 kg, v2i = 0 m/s
Dcommon final velocity vf (target unit: m/s)
Em1v1i + m2v2i = (m1 + m2)vf — one mass, one velocity after
Asymbols first: vf = (m1v1i) / (m1 + m2)
vf = (2000 kg)(5.0 m/s) / 5000 kg = 2.0 m/s

Both cars move at exactly 2.0 m/s after coupling — one combined mass, one shared velocity.

The equation collapses to one unknown because both objects share the same final velocity — that's the defining feature of "perfectly inelastic," and it's why these problems are algebraically the simplest of the three collision types even though conceptually they lose the most energy. Note the clean division (10,000/5000 = 2.0 exactly) — flag it as one of the rare KDEA answers that earns an honest = instead of ≈.

Collision Types · The Hidden Cost

Same collision: what happened to the kinetic energy?

Momentum was conserved exactly. Kinetic energy was not — check the same train cars:

Before

KE = ½(2000)(5.0)2 + 0

KE ≈ 2.5 × 104 J

After

KE = ½(5000)(2.0)2

KE ≈ 1.0 × 104 J

≈ 1.5 × 104 J — 60% of the kinetic energy — became heat, sound, and deformation in the coupling mechanism.

This is the payoff slide that makes "momentum conserved, KE not" concrete with real numbers instead of just a rule to memorize. 60% is a large, memorable fraction — perfectly inelastic collisions lose the MOST kinetic energy of any collision type for a given momentum exchange, which is worth stating explicitly since students sometimes assume "stuck together" sounds gentler than it is energetically.

Collision Types · 3 of 3

Inelastic: everything in between

Most real-world collisions are simply inelastic — objects bounce apart (they don't stick), but some kinetic energy is still lost to heat, sound, or permanent deformation.

What conserves
  • Momentum: always, for an isolated system — same equation as every other type
  • Kinetic energy: partially lost — somewhere between 0% (elastic) and the perfectly-inelastic maximum

A dropped basketball that bounces back to 80% of its original height lost 20% of its kinetic energy to the floor — inelastic, but not perfectly so.

The basketball example is a good everyday anchor — students have all watched a ball bounce lower and lower each time, which is exactly repeated inelastic collisions with the floor bleeding off energy each bounce. Reinforce: "inelastic" without qualification just means "not elastic" — it's the default, catch-all category, and elastic and perfectly inelastic are the two extreme edges of it.

Worked Example · Elastic Check

Equal-mass carts: velocities swap

Two identical 1.0 kg carts collide elastically on a frictionless track. Cart 1 moves at 4.0 m/s toward stationary cart 2. In an elastic, equal-mass collision, cart 1 stops and cart 2 takes over its speed. Verify both conservation laws hold.

QuantityBeforeAfter
Momentum(1.0)(4.0) + (1.0)(0) = 4.0 kg·m/s(1.0)(0) + (1.0)(4.0) = 4.0 kg·m/s
Kinetic energy½(1.0)(4.0)2 = 8.0 J½(1.0)(4.0)2 = 8.0 J

Both rows match exactly — momentum and kinetic energy are both conserved. This is the signature of a truly elastic collision.

This is a verification exercise, not a solve-for-the-unknown problem — the "cart 1 stops, cart 2 takes over" result is a genuine special case of equal-mass elastic collisions (the general two-body elastic formula is beyond this course's scope; we use the demonstrated outcome and check it against both conservation laws). This is also exactly the demonstration students will likely reproduce with magnetic-bumper carts in next week's lab.

Don't Mix Them Up

Momentum and kinetic energy are not the same rule

Momentum, p = mv
  • Vector — direction (sign) matters
  • Conserved in every isolated collision, all three types
  • Units: kg·m/s
Kinetic energy, KE = ½mv2
  • Scalar — always positive, no direction
  • Conserved only in elastic collisions
  • Units: J (joules)

If a problem says "the objects stick together," momentum conservation still applies — but you cannot also assume kinetic energy was conserved.

This is the single most common exam error in this unit: students reflexively write both conservation equations for every collision. Flag it hard — momentum conservation is the one universal tool; kinetic energy conservation is a bonus equation that ONLY applies when the problem tells you (or shows you) the collision is elastic. Perfectly inelastic problems in particular tempt students to (wrongly) set KE_before = KE_after.
