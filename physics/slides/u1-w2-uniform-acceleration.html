The Key Word

What Does “Uniform” Mean?

Acceleration that doesn't change. At all. Ever.

Uniform Acceleration · 1 of 3

Constant acceleration = steady velocity change

If acceleration is uniform, three things are true — and they're all the same statement:

a = constant
The number doesn't change
If a = 2.0 m/s2 at t = 1 s, it's still 2.0 m/s2 at t = 10 s. The acceleration has one value for the entire motion.
Δv = a · Δt
Velocity changes at a steady rate
Every second that passes, the velocity changes by exactly the same amount. No surprises, no jerks.
a–t graph
A flat horizontal line
The acceleration-vs-time graph is perfectly flat. The height of the line IS the value of a.

"Uniform" = "constant" = "does not change." All three mean the same thing.

Drill the synonym: uniform = constant. The three tiles are the same concept in words, in algebra, and on a graph. Students should be able to move between all three. The "velocity changes at a steady rate" tile is the one that connects back to Week 1's slope chain: a straight v-t line means constant slope = constant acceleration.

Uniform Acceleration · 2 of 3

The a–t graph is a flat line

When acceleration is constant, the a–t graph can only look one way:

a > 0
positive acceleration
A flat line above the t-axis. Velocity increases every second. Think: a car accelerating onto the highway.
a < 0
negative acceleration
A flat line below the t-axis. Velocity changes in the negative direction every second. Think: braking (if velocity is positive), or speeding up in reverse (if velocity is negative).

Compare this to Week 1: a flat v–t line meant constant velocity. A flat a–t line means constant acceleration. Same shape, one level up the slope chain.

Flat a–t ≠ no acceleration. Flat a–t = constant acceleration. Zero acceleration is a special case where that constant happens to be 0.

This slide deliberately mirrors the Week 1 v-t "flat ≠ stopped" correction. Students who mastered that will get this one instantly; those who didn't need the parallel drawn explicitly. The negative-a tile plants the seed for sign conventions later — don't dwell here, just let the example names ("braking," "speeding up in reverse") mark that there's more to come.

Uniform Acceleration · 3 of 3

The v–t graph is a straight ramp

If a is constant, then velocity-vs-time is a straight line. The slope of that line IS the acceleration.

The geometry you already know

From Week 1: a = Δv / Δt — the slope of the v–t graph. If the graph is a straight line, that slope has one value the whole time. That value is a.

The x–t graph under constant acceleration is curved (a parabola). You'll see why when we work through the equations — the t2 term is what makes it curve.

Constant a → straight v–t → curved x–t. This is not three separate facts — it's the slope chain.

This is the payoff slide for the slope chain from Week 1. Draw the three graphs on the board if time allows: flat a-t → straight v-t ramp → curved x-t parabola. The three graphs tell one story. The "t² term = curve" preview sets up the kinematic equations landing on the very next slide.

The Toolbox

The Four Kinematic Equations

When acceleration is constant, these four cover every motion problem

Frame this as the moment students have been building toward since Workshop I. The equation on slide 8c of Workshop I — vf2 = vi2 + 2aΔx — is back. Point to it and say: "you decoded this four weeks ago, and you didn't even know what it meant yet. Now you do." Let the callback land.

The Kinematic Equations · 1 of 3

Four equations. Five variables.

Every one of these assumes acceleration is constant. The variables are always the same five: vi, vf, a, t, Δx.

vf = vi + at
no Δx
Velocity after t seconds of constant acceleration. Use when you know (or want) vf, vi, a, t — and displacement doesn't appear.
Δx = vit + ½at2
no vf
Displacement under constant acceleration. Use when you need Δx but don't know (and don't need) the final velocity.
vf2 = vi2 + 2aΔx
no t
The “no-time-given” equation. Use when the problem never mentions time — you don't know it, you don't need it.
Δx = ½(vi + vf)t
no a
Displacement from the average velocity. Use when acceleration is constant but its actual value isn't given or needed.

A step-by-step derivation walkthrough video is coming — for now, focus on what each equation does and when to use it.

Present the equations together first, then unpack them individually. The "no Δx" / "no vf" / "no t" / "no a" labels are the E-of-KDEA key — each equation omits exactly one of the five variables. The derivation video is a student resource, not something to play in class; name it here so they know it exists. The ½(at²) term is why x-t graphs are parabolas under constant a — circle back to slide 6.

The Kinematic Equations · 2 of 3

Choosing the right equation = the E of KDEA

When you face a kinematics problem, you will always know three of the five variables and need a fourth. That means exactly one variable is neither known nor wanted — and exactly one equation omits it.

The selection rule
  1. Write down your K (knowns): list the values with units
  2. Write down your D (unknown, target): name the variable and its unit
  3. Identify the one variable that is NEITHER in K nor in D.
  4. Pick the equation that doesn't contain that variable.

Five variables. Three knowns. One target. One equation without the fifth. Every time.

This is the most important slide of the week. The "find the missing variable, then pick the equation that omits it" strategy is the single move that prevents students from staring at four equations frozen. Walk it aloud with a dummy example: "If I know vi, a, and t, and I want Δx — what variable don't I have? vf. Which equation has no vf? The second one." Do this three times with different combinations before moving on.

KDEA in Action

Worked Examples

Same four steps. New equations.

The protocol didn't change. K, D, E, A — exactly the same four-step move from Workshop II. The only thing that changed is the E row now has four equations to choose from, and the selection rule from slide 9 is how you pick. Say it out loud: "nothing new to learn, just a bigger equation bank."

Worked Example 1 · Setup

A car speeds up from rest

A car starts from rest and accelerates at a constant 3.0 m/s2 for 8.0 s. How far does it travel?

Before you touch a calculator

Read the problem. Which variable is missing? You know vi, a, t. You want Δx. vf is neither known nor asked for → use the equation with no vf.

Equation selection happens before KDEA's E row. Don't write E until you've picked.

Force students to answer "which equation" before they write the KDEA table. The callout box is the pre-E thinking step — do it aloud before moving to the next slide. The most common error is students reaching for vf = vi + at first, then realizing it doesn't give them Δx. The selection rule prevents that stall.

Worked Example 1 · KDEA

vi = 0, a = 3.0 m/s2, t = 8.0 s → Δx = ?

StepWhat you write
Kvi = 0 m/s, a = 3.0 m/s2, t = 8.0 s
Ddisplacement Δx (target unit: m)
EΔx = vit + ½at2 — the equation with no vf
Asymbols first: Δx = (0 m/s)(8.0 s) + ½(3.0 m/s2)(8.0 s)2
= 0 + ½(3.0 m/s2)(64.0 s2)
= (1.5 m/s2)(64.0 s2) = 96 m

Unit check: (m/s2)(s2) = m. The algebra cancels the time units and leaves you in meters — exactly what displacement demands.

Walk the unit cancellation inside the A row: s² in the denominator cancels with s² in the numerator, leaving only m. This is why we keep units the whole way — the algebra checks itself. The first term vanishes because vi = 0; flag this as a common gift from "starting from rest" problems, but warn students that it only works when vi is genuinely zero.

Worked Example 2 · Setup

The “no time given” case

A bicycle moving at 15.0 m/s brakes with a constant deceleration of 4.0 m/s2. What is its speed after traveling 20.0 m?

Workshop I callback

You decoded this exact equation four weeks ago, piece by piece, in Workshop I:
vf2 = vi2 + 2aΔx

Then you didn't know what it did. Now you do: it's the equation for when time is the missing variable.

No t in the problem? No t in the equation. That's the selection rule in action.

The callback to Workshop I slide 8c is the emotional beat of this week. Students who remember decoding the equation symbol-by-symbol (vf2 read as "final velocity squared," 2a as "two times the acceleration") should feel the arc: they went from "what is this alien equation" to "I can solve it." Let that land before moving to the KDEA table.

Worked Example 2 · KDEA

vi = 15.0 m/s, a = −4.0 m/s2, Δx = 20.0 m → vf = ?

StepWhat you write
Kvi = 15.0 m/s, a = −4.0 m/s2 (deceleration = negative a), Δx = 20.0 m
Dfinal speed vf (target unit: m/s)
Evf2 = vi2 + 2aΔx — the equation with no t
Asymbols first: vf2 = (15.0 m/s)2 + 2(−4.0 m/s2)(20.0 m)
= 225 m2/s2 − 160 m2/s2
= 65 m2/s2
vf = √(65 m2/s2) ≈ 8.1 m/s

Unit check: the square root of m2/s2 gives m/s. The ≈ is honest — √65 is not a tidy number.

Every student should notice the ≈ on the final line — call it out. 65 is not a perfect square, so 8.1 is an approximation, not an exact answer. The units inside the square root (m²/s²) unpack to m/s, which is how they know the algebra survived the radical. The negative a is intentional: it makes vf smaller than vi (slowing down), and the term goes from 225 to 65. If a student forgets the negative sign on a, they'll get 225 + 160 = 385 → vf ≈ 19.6 m/s — a bicycle speeding up while "braking." That's a teachable KDEA sanity-check moment.

The Fine Print

Signs Matter

Pick a positive direction and stick with it. Everything follows.

This is the hardest two slides of the week for students who haven't internalized velocity and acceleration as vectors. Week 1 planted the seed (negative position is real, negative velocity means moving left). This week extends it: negative acceleration does NOT automatically mean "slowing down." That distinction is the #1 kinematics error across all of Unit 1.

Sign Conventions · 1 of 2

Step one: pick a positive direction

Before you write a single equation, decide which direction is positive. Every number you plug in gets a sign relative to that choice. The equations don't care — you do.

Rule

A positive a means the velocity changes in the positive direction each second. A negative a means the velocity changes in the negative direction each second.

That is it. The sign of a tells you which way the change points — not whether the object is speeding up or slowing down.

a is a vector. Its sign tells you the direction of the velocity change, period.

"Sign of a = direction of Δv, not direction of v" — say it exactly like this. Students who collapse "negative a" into "slowing down" will be wrong on half the problems in free fall (Unit 1 Week 4). The callout is the definition they need to memorize; the next slide makes it concrete with examples.

Sign Conventions · 2 of 2

Four scenarios. Only two are “slowing down.”

v signa signWhat's happeningSpeeding up or slowing down?
++Moving forward, accelerating forwardSpeeding up
+−Moving forward, accelerating backward (braking)Slowing down
−−Moving backward, accelerating backwardSpeeding up (in the negative direction)
−+Moving backward, accelerating forward (braking in reverse)Slowing down
The real rule

v and a have the same sign → speeding up. v and a have opposite signs → slowing down.

Negative a alone tells you nothing about speeding up vs slowing down. You need the sign of v too.

Row 3 is the trap: v negative AND a negative = speeding up backward. Every student who equates "negative a" with "slowing down" will call this deceleration, and they'll be wrong. The "same sign = speeding up, opposite signs = slowing down" rule is what they should carry. This table is screenshot material — have them take a photo of it. Free fall in two weeks will hammer this exact table.

Quick Check

Which equation?

On your whiteboard — 3 minutes

For each scenario, name the missing variable and pick the equation:

  1. A rocket starts from rest, accelerates at 12.0 m/s2 for 5.0 s. You need its final velocity.
  2. A car moving at 22.0 m/s brakes and stops in 40.0 m. You need the deceleration. No time is mentioned.
  3. A runner goes from 2.0 m/s to 8.0 m/s over 3.0 s. You need the distance covered — acceleration is constant but you don't have its value.
Answers: (1) variable missing = Δx → vf = vi + at. (2) variable missing = t → vf2 = vi2 + 2aΔx. (3) variable missing = a → Δx = ½(vi + vf)t. Cold-call and ask for the reasoning, not just the answer. Students who can articulate "I don't know t and I don't need it, so I use the no-t equation" have it. Students who guess the equation without naming the missing variable haven't internalized the selection rule from slide 9 — redirect them.
