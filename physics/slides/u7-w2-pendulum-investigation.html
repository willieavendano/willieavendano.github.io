Act I · Design

Build a fair test.

One question. One manipulated variable. Evidence that can answer it.

Use this divider to shift the room from "follow the procedure" to "own the procedure." Students may choose a variable such as length, mass, or release angle, but their design must isolate that variable and produce repeated, quantitative measurements.

Design · 1 of 4

Start with a question that can be measured.

L
length
How does pendulum length affect period, T in s?
m
bob mass
How does bob mass affect period, T in s?
θ
release angle
How does release angle affect period, T in s?

A strong question names the variable you change and the measurement you compare.

These are examples of measurable questions, not a menu students must copy. A question that asks whether "the pendulum works" is not operational enough. Press students to name the independent variable and the dependent measurement before they write a hypothesis.

Design · 2 of 4

Name the variables before collecting data.

1
independent variable
The one factor you intentionally change between conditions. Include its symbol and unit, such as L in m.
2
dependent variable
The response you measure, such as period T in s.
3
controlled variables
Everything held constant: bob, pivot, release method, counting rule, and surroundings.
4
operational definition
Exactly how a variable is measured: pivot to bob center for L in m; ten cycles for elapsed time t in s.
The operational-definition tile is the one students often omit. "Length" is ambiguous unless they state where the ruler starts and ends. "Period" is ambiguous unless they define the crossing or reference point used for timing. Require these details in the method, not just in conversation.

Design · 3 of 4

A hypothesis predicts a pattern, not a slogan.

Model-based prediction

For small release angles, the simple-pendulum model predicts that increasing length L in m increases period T in s, because

T = 2π√(L / g),   T in s, L in m, g ≈ 9.8 m/s2

State the direction you expect, the physical reason, and the evidence that would support or challenge it.

“I think X will change Y because…” is a beginning, not a complete hypothesis.

Design · 4 of 4 · 5 minutes

Before the first swing: defend the design.

Partner audit
  1. Can your partner identify exactly what changes and what stays fixed?
  2. Can they repeat one trial from your written procedure alone?
  3. Does your measurement distinguish a real trend from stopwatch noise?
  4. What observation would make you reject or revise your hypothesis?

If the answer depends on something you did not record, the design is not finished.

This audit is a fast formative checkpoint. Do not turn it into teacher approval. Students should find the missing details in one another's plans: unlabeled lengths, a changing release angle, one trial only, or a vague timing rule. Have them revise the written plan before equipment time begins.

Act II · Run

Make the data trustworthy.

Good conclusions cannot rescue careless measurements.

Run-Day Logistics

Efficient work is organized work.

A
assembly
Secure the pivot. Measure from pivot to bob center. Record the actual L in m, not the intended value.
B
roles
Assign launcher, timer, counter, and recorder. Rotate roles without changing the protocol.
C
run order
Record condition, trial number, cycles counted, elapsed time t in s, and notes immediately.
D
reset rule
Return to the same release condition before every trial. Stop if the bob strikes, twists, or leaves the plane.
Students lose data during transitions, not during the swing itself. Require raw data on paper or in the shared table before they move to the next condition. A stopwatch value typed later from memory is not raw data. Check that every group has a recorder before releasing the first bob.

Data Quality · 1 of 4

Measure many cycles, then divide.

Timing rule

Time N complete cycles, not one. Use the same reference crossing to start and stop the timer.

T = t / N,   T in s, t in s, N in cycles

Repeat rule

Repeat each condition. If one value is surprising, investigate the procedure before deleting it.

Tavg = (T1 + T2 + T3) / 3,   Tavg in s

Raw elapsed times stay in the report. Averages do not replace evidence.

Table labels are part of the evidence: a reader should know what every number means without asking you.

This table is a model of structure, not a data set students should copy. Emphasize that the note "small twist" is valuable evidence. A student may decide later that the trial is questionable, but silently removing it destroys the audit trail and makes the conclusion less credible.

Data Quality · 3 of 4

Uncertainty is part of the measurement.

±0.001 m
instrument
Ruler resolution or reading limit for length L in m.
±0.1 s
timing
Stopwatch resolution and human reaction for elapsed time t in s.
spread
variation
Trial-to-trial differences in period T in s reveal repeatability.

More cycles reduce the fractional effect of start/stop reaction time; they do not erase every source of uncertainty.

Students often write a fake uncertainty with no connection to the instrument or process. Ask: where did this number come from, and what does it describe? Keep the focus on defensible estimates, not advanced formal propagation. For this course, identifying dominant sources and comparing spread is enough.

Data Quality · 4 of 4

Separate random scatter from systematic error.

Random
  • reaction time varies
  • trials scatter above and below a mean
  • repeat and average to reduce its influence
Systematic
  • length measured from the wrong point
  • release angle changes by condition
  • repeating the same flawed method does not fix it

Do not call every mismatch “human error.” Identify the mechanism.

Analysis · 1 of 4

Choose a graph that tests the model.

T vs. L
direct view
Tests the measured trend. A curve is expected if T follows √L.
T2 vs. L
linearized model
From T = 2π√(L/g), the model predicts a line through the origin.
residual
model check
Compare measured T in s with predicted T in s.

Axes need quantity names and units. A graph without units is unfinished evidence.

This slide gives students analysis options without dictating one independent variable. If a group tested length, T² versus L is a strong linearized test. If a group tested mass or angle, the direct graph and a model comparison may be more appropriate. Require students to justify the graph they choose in the report.

Worked Example · KDEA

Turn one timing run into a period.

A group times N = 10 cycles in t = 12.6 s. Find the period T in s.

StepWhat you write
KN = 10 cycles, t = 12.6 s
Dperiod T, target unit s per cycle
ET = t / N, with T in s, t in s, and N in cycles
AT = 12.6 s / 10 cycles ≈ 1.26 s/cycle

The measured period is not the same quantity as the total elapsed time.

This is the only fully worked numerical example in the deck. Keep the KDEA table visible while students calculate their own periods. Point out that dividing by cycles removes the number of swings from the measurement, leaving seconds per cycle. Do not let students average raw elapsed times from different cycle counts without first converting them to period.

Act III · Write

Make the evidence argue.

A complete independent report is a scientific story: question, method, evidence, claim.

Students may have produced these pieces in earlier reports with heavy scaffolding. This time, they must decide the structure and level of detail independently. The report is assessed as a complete argument, not as a collection of disconnected paragraphs.

Independent Report · 1 of 3

What a complete report contains

01
question + hypothesis
Operational question, variables, model-based prediction, and rationale.
02
method
Equipment, setup, measurements, roles, controls, trial count, and enough detail to reproduce the test.
03
data + analysis
Raw table, calculated quantities, uncertainty, graph(s), and equations with units.
04
claim + evaluation
Conclusion tied to evidence, limitations, error mechanisms, and a specific improvement or next test.
This is the report architecture, not a paragraph-by-paragraph scaffold. Students choose headings and order as long as the argument remains easy to follow. A polished introduction with no reproducible method is still incomplete; a beautiful graph with no claim is still incomplete.

Independent Report · 2 of 3

Evidence standards: show the reader your reasoning.

Quantitative
  • sample calculation with units
  • consistent significant figures
  • graph axes, labels, and units
  • comparison to a model or prediction
Scientific
  • claim points to specific data
  • limitations name mechanisms
  • outliers are explained or retained
  • improvement changes the method

“The data was close” is not evidence until you show what close means.

Independent Report · 3 of 3

Before you submit: audit the argument.

Read it as a stranger
  1. Can a reader identify the changed variable, measured response, and controls?
  2. Can a reader reproduce one trial from the method?
  3. Can a reader trace every conclusion to a table, calculation, or graph?
  4. Can a reader tell what uncertainty limits the claim?
  5. Can a reader tell whether the evidence supports, challenges, or revises the hypothesis?

Independence means the final reasoning and writing are yours.

Use this as a silent self-audit, not as a teacher-led checklist that recreates the scaffold. Students should mark places where a stranger would have to ask a question. Those gaps become the last revisions before the Thursday deadline. Be clear that peer feedback may identify confusion, but each student owns the final text.
