Warm-Up · Setup

A soccer ball kicked from ground level

A soccer ball is kicked at 16.0 m/s, angled 40° above the horizontal, from flat ground. How far does it travel before it hits the grass?

The plan
  1. Decompose the launch velocity into vx and viy.
  2. Vertical KDEA: Δy = 0 (it lands at the same height) → solve for t.
  3. Horizontal KDEA: use t to find the range Δx.

Convention: up = positive, right = positive. g = −9.8 m/s2.

Warm-Up · KDEA

vi = 16.0 m/s, θ = 40° → range

StepWhat you write
Decompose vx = 16.0 · cos 40° ≈ 12.3 m/s
viy = 16.0 · sin 40° ≈ 10.3 m/s
Kviy = 10.3 m/s, Δy = 0 m (same height), g = −9.8 m/s2
Dtime of flight t (target unit: s)
EΔy = viyt + ½gt2 — no vfy
Asymbols first: 0 = 10.3t − 4.9t2
t(10.3 − 4.9t) = 0
t = 0 (launch) or t = 10.3 / 4.9 ≈ 2.10 s

Now horizontal: Δx = vx · t = (12.3 m/s)(2.10 s) ≈ 25.8 m

Two KDEA passes. One problem. Layout this exactly on the test.

Walk the equation selection aloud: "I know viy, Δy, and g. What's missing from the four equations? vfy. Which equation has no vfy? Δy = viyt + ½gt²." The t = 0 root is the launch instant — naming it explicitly prevents students from thinking the factoring failed. The horizontal pass is one multiplication, so it doesn't get its own full KDEA table — just write it below, same as on the test. This is the model layout to screenshot.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 12 minutes

A water balloon off the roof

A water balloon is launched from a 10.0 m rooftop at 22.0 m/s, angled 35° above the horizontal. How far from the base of the building does it hit the ground?

With your partner
  1. Decompose the launch velocity.
  2. Vertical KDEA: Δy = −10.0 m (lands below launch). Solve for t.
  3. Horizontal KDEA: use t to find Δx.

The Δy is negative — it lands below the launch point. That sign travels through every line of algebra.

This is harder than the warm-up: elevated launch with negative Δy means the quadratic doesn't factor cleanly — students will need the quadratic formula. Circulate and check decomposition first (sin/cos swap is still the most common error). Then check that Δy = −10.0, not +10.0 — the sign is the physics. The quadratic discriminant isn't a perfect square, so ≈ is honest. Answer preview: vx ≈ 18.0 m/s, viy ≈ 12.6 m/s, t ≈ 3.21 s, Δx ≈ 57.8 m.

Practice · KDEA

vi = 22.0 m/s, θ = 35°, Δy = −10.0 m

StepWhat you write
Decompose vx = 22.0 · cos 35° ≈ 18.0 m/s
viy = 22.0 · sin 35° ≈ 12.6 m/s
Kviy = 12.6 m/s, Δy = −10.0 m, g = −9.8 m/s2
Dtime t (target unit: s)
EΔy = viyt + ½gt2 — no vfy
Asymbols first: −10.0 = 12.6t − 4.9t2
4.9t2 − 12.6t − 10.0 = 0

Horizontal: Δx = vx · t = (18.0 m/s)(3.21 s) ≈ 57.8 m

The negative root (≈ −0.64 s) is discarded. The ≈ is honest — the discriminant isn't a perfect square.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 15 minutes

A baseball off a cliff

A baseball is thrown horizontally at 25.0 m/s from a 44.1 m cliff.

With your partner
  1. How long is it in the air? (No decomposition needed — viy = 0.)
  2. How far from the base of the cliff does it land?
  3. What is its final velocity? Give both magnitude AND direction.

Horizontal launch = viy = 0. The vertical motion is pure free fall from rest. Part (c) requires a vector triangle.

The hardest of the three. Horizontal launch means viy = 0 — no sine needed because the entire launch speed is horizontal. Part (a) is the cleanest free-fall problem in the unit: Δy = ½gt², one unknown. Part (b) is one multiplication. Part (c) is the new move: combining vx (constant, horizontal) and vfy (from free fall, vertical) into a single velocity vector with magnitude and direction — same right-triangle trig from Workshop III. Circulate during part (c); students who forget that final velocity is a vector (not just a speed) will miss direction. Answers: (a) t = 3.00 s, (b) Δx = 75.0 m, (c) |v| ≈ 38.6 m/s at ≈ 49.6° below horizontal.

Practice · KDEA (c)

Final velocity: magnitude + direction

StepWhat you write
Kvx = 25.0 m/s (constant), viy = 0 m/s, t = 3.00 s, g = −9.8 m/s2
Dfinal velocity vector: magnitude |v| (target unit: m/s) and direction θ below horizontal
Evfy = viy + gt → vertical component. Then |v| = √(vx2 + vfy2), θ = tan−1(|vfy| / vx)
Avfy = 0 + (−9.8)(3.00) = −29.4 m/s (downward)

Final velocity is a vector — magnitude AND direction. Speed alone is half the answer. The ≈ is honest.

The Big Picture

Unit 1 is one story, not four topics.

Motion graphs → kinematic equations → free fall → projectiles

This divider sets up the concept map. The single biggest failure mode on the test is students treating graphs, equations, free fall, and projectiles as four separate things to memorize separately. They aren't — they're one tool applied to progressively richer situations. The concept map slide makes that chain explicit.

The Big Picture · 1 of 1

One tool, four applications

Everything in Unit 1 is the same chain. Each step inherits everything from the step before it:

1
Motion graphs
Position, velocity, acceleration. The slope chain: slope of x-t is v; slope of v-t is a; area under v-t is Δx. This is where the variables come from.
2
Kinematic equations
The algebra version of the same relationships. Four equations, four variables each. The selection rule: find the missing variable, pick the equation without it. This is where the math lives.
3
Free fall
Kinematic equations with a = g ≈ 9.8 m/s2 downward. Same four equations, same selection rule, same KDEA. a just got a name and a constant value.
4
Projectiles
Free fall in the vertical + constant velocity in the horizontal. Decompose the launch, solve each direction separately. Two KDEA passes sharing one t.

There is no "projectile equation." There's the same four equations, run twice. That's the whole unit.

Each tile builds on the one before it. Point explicitly: if a student can do step 2 (kinematic equation selection from Week 2) and step 3 (swap a for g from Week 4), then step 4 (projectiles) is just doing step 3 in the vertical direction AND multiplying vx by t in the horizontal. The concept map's job is to collapse four weeks of material into one mental model. The chip is the takeaway — say it aloud and leave it on screen.

Common Test Traps · 1 of 4

Trap #1: Sign errors

g is 9.8 m/s2 downward. If you pick up = positive, then g = −9.8 at every single instant of the flight — going up, at the peak, and coming down.

The trap

Students flip the sign of g at the peak. "It was slowing down, now it's speeding up — g must have changed sign." It didn't. g is constant the entire flight. If g were zero at the peak, the ball would hover there forever.

And watch your Δy sign: if it lands below the launch point, Δy is negative. That sign has to travel through every line of algebra that touches Δy.

Common Test Traps · 2 of 4

Trap #2: Units must travel with the math

An answer without units does not count. This is science, not math. But it's more than the final answer — the units have to cancel correctly through every algebraic step:

The test-day check

Before you circle any answer, trace the units from K to A:

  • In K, every number gets a unit written next to it.
  • In A, every multiplication and division carries its units through.
  • If m/s cancels with s and leaves m, and you were solving for a distance — the algebra is confirmed before you trust the number.

A number with no unit on a KDEA problem earns zero credit. Full stop.

Common Test Traps · 3 of 4

Trap #3: Mixing horizontal and vertical in the same equation

Horizontal and vertical motion are independent — they share only time:

Wrong

Δx = viyt + ½gt2

Mixing horizontal Δx with vertical viy and g. This equation doesn't exist.

Right

Δx = vx · t

All horizontal. vx is constant because ax = 0.

An x-subscript and a y-subscript in the same equation = broken independence. The only shared symbol is t.

Common Test Traps · 4 of 4

Trap #4: Using the wrong equation

The four kinematic equations each omit one variable. The selection rule from Week 2 is not optional — it's the fastest way to avoid solving for something you don't need:

EquationMissingUse when…
vf = vi + atΔxyou don't know or need displacement
Δx = vit + ½at2vffinal velocity doesn't matter (most projectile vertical solves)
vf2 = vi2 + 2aΔxttime is not given and not asked for
Δx = ½(vi + vf)taacceleration doesn't appear (rare in free fall)

Before you write E, check: which variable am I missing? Pick the equation without it. Every time.

The Test · 2 of 2

What KDEA looks like on test day

Your answer sheet should look like this
StepWhat the grader sees
KA list of every given, with units. "vi = 14.0 m/s." Not "vi = 14."
DThe target quantity AND its unit. "Find t, in seconds." Not "solve for t."
EThe equation, written in symbols. "vf2 = vi2 + 2aΔx — no t." State why you picked it.
ASymbols first: rearranged equation. Then numbers with units plugged in. Then the arithmetic. Finally the answer, circled, with its unit.

The grader is looking for the protocol, not just the answer. An organized KDEA table with a small algebra error scores higher than a correct answer with no work shown.

Confidence Check · Turn & Talk · 10 minutes

One last problem before Thursday

With your partner

A tennis ball is served horizontally at 30.0 m/s from a height of 2.45 m. Does it clear the net 11.9 m away if the net is 0.914 m high?

  1. Find how long it takes the ball to travel 11.9 m horizontally.
  2. Find how far the ball has fallen in that time.
  3. Compare the ball's height to the net height. Does it clear?

This is the exact shape of a test projectile problem, with one extra step: after the math, you make a judgment.

This is the confidence closer — a problem that looks test-like but with a built-in payoff (the yes/no judgment). Horizontal: t = 11.9/30.0 ≈ 0.397 s. Vertical: Δy = ½(−9.8)(0.397)² ≈ −0.772 m, so the ball has fallen 0.772 m. Height at the net: 2.45 − 0.772 ≈ 1.68 m. The net is 0.914 m, so yes, it clears by about 0.76 m. The judgment step is the whole point — physics answers real questions, not just numbers. Have tables report "clears" or "doesn't clear" with evidence.
