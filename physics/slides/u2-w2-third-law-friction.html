Newton's Laws · 3 of 3

Newton's Third Law

Every force is half of a pair — and the other half lands on a different object.

Frame this as completing the set — the first two laws described one object's motion; the third law is about the relationship between two objects. This is also the direct payoff of last week's FBD rule ("only forces ON the object") — now we name the force that got left off on purpose.

Third Law · 1 of 3

For every action, an equal and opposite reaction

If object A pushes on object B, then B pushes back on A with the same magnitude, in the opposite direction — at the exact same instant.

Rule

The two forces in a third-law pair never appear on the same FBD. They act on different objects — that's the whole reason they don't cancel each other out.

Last week's rule — “only forces ON the object” — excluded exactly this force. Now you know its name.

The "different objects" rule is the single most important idea on this slide — students who think action-reaction pairs cancel are drawing both forces on the same object's FBD, which is always wrong. Each object gets its own diagram with its own net force.

Third Law · 2 of 3

You've already met one pair — without the name

N
book & table
The table pushes up on the book with N. The book pushes down on the table with an equal, opposite force. Same pair, from Week 1's FBD.
←→
walking
Your foot pushes backward on the ground. The ground pushes forward on you. That reaction force is what actually moves you.
↑↓
rocket & exhaust
The rocket pushes exhaust gas downward. The gas pushes the rocket upward. No air required — this is why rockets work in space.
The book-and-table tile is the intended "aha" — students already drew N last week without knowing it was half of a third-law pair. Walking is the example that best kills the misconception that reaction forces are somehow weaker or delayed — the ground's push on your foot is what propels you forward, full strength, instantly.

Third Law · 3 of 3

Turn and talk: name the reaction force

With your partner — 3 minutes

For each action force, name the reaction force — magnitude, direction, and which object it acts on:

  1. A swimmer's hand pushes water backward.
  2. Earth's gravity pulls down on a falling apple.
  3. A hammer strikes a nail, pushing it downward.
Answers: (1) water pushes the swimmer's hand forward, with equal magnitude — this is what propels swimming. (2) the apple pulls up on Earth with equal magnitude (genuinely surprising to most students — Earth just has enormous mass, so its acceleration is unnoticeable). (3) the nail pushes up on the hammer with equal magnitude, which is why the hammer stops. Question 2 is the one worth dwelling on — it proves gravity itself is a third-law pair, not an exception.

Forces · The Last One

Friction

The force that resists sliding — or the tendency to slide.

This is the last common force added to the FBD toolkit this unit. From here forward, almost every problem includes it. Frame friction as usually unwanted in the story problems (it "costs" force) but also as the thing that lets you walk, drive, and hold objects in your hand at all — not purely a nuisance.

Friction · 1 of 4

Friction acts parallel to the surface, opposing motion

Friction is a contact force between two touching surfaces. It always points parallel to the surface, in the direction that opposes relative sliding — or the tendency to slide, if nothing is moving yet.

Rule

Friction never causes motion on its own. It only reacts — opposing whatever motion (or attempted motion) is already happening.

"Opposing, never causing" is the conceptual anchor. Students sometimes think friction has its own agenda (slowing everything down toward zero) rather than reacting to whatever push or pull is present.

Friction · 2 of 4

Friction isn't one number — it responds to what you push with

Push gently on a heavy box and it doesn't move — friction matched your push exactly. Push harder, friction still matches, up to a point. Push past that point, and the box breaks free and starts sliding — and the friction force actually drops.

Two regimes

Static — the box isn't moving; friction matches your applied force, up to a maximum. Kinetic — the box is sliding; friction holds steady at a single, lower value the whole time it slides.

You'll plot exactly this behavior — applied force vs. friction force — in next week's lab.

This is the conceptual setup for next week's lab graph, so plant it clearly now. The "friction drops once sliding starts" idea is the reason μs is generally bigger than μk — say that explicitly before the equations slide formalizes it.

Friction · 3 of 4

Two equations, one idea

fs ≤ μsN
static friction · a maximum, not a fixed value
Friction matches the applied force exactly, up to this ceiling. Below the ceiling, the object doesn't move.
fk = μkN
kinetic friction · a fixed value while sliding
Once the object is sliding, friction holds steady at exactly this value — no more “up to.”

μ (mu) is the coefficient of friction — a unitless number describing how rough two surfaces are together. N is the normal force between them, from your FBD.

Usually μs > μk — it takes more force to start sliding than to keep it going.

Hammer the "≤" vs "=" distinction — it's the single most-tested friction idea. f_s is whatever value balances the applied force, capped at μsN; students who plug in μsN as if it's always the friction force get static problems wrong. μ being unitless is also worth stating explicitly — students sometimes hunt for units on μ and won't find any.

Worked Example · 1 of 3

Does the box move?

A 20 kg box sits on a flat floor with μs = 0.40. Someone pushes horizontally with 70 N. Does the box move?

StepWhat you write
Km = 20 kg, μs = 0.40, Fa = 70 N (horizontal)
Dmaximum static friction fs,max (target unit: N) — then compare to Fa
EN = mg (flat floor, no vertical push); fs,max = μsN
AN = (20 kg)(9.8 m/s2) = 196 N
fs,max = (0.40)(196 N) = 78.4 N

Fa = 70 N < fs,max = 78.4 N — static friction can supply exactly 70 N to balance the push. The box does NOT move.

The key move is comparing F_a to f_s,max — students want to plug F_a into an equation and solve for something, but the actual answer here is a comparison, not an algebra step. Static friction is 70 N in this scenario, not 78.4 N — 78.4 N is only the ceiling. Say that distinction out loud.

Worked Example · 2 of 3

Now it's sliding — find the acceleration

A different 20 kg box is already sliding across the same floor, now with μk = 0.30. Someone pushes it with 100 N horizontally. Find its acceleration.

StepWhat you write
Km = 20 kg, μk = 0.30, Fa = 100 N
Dacceleration a (target unit: m/s2)
EN = mg; fk = μkN; ΣF = Fa − fk = ma
AN = (20 kg)(9.8 m/s2) = 196 N
fk = (0.30)(196 N) = 58.8 N
a = (Fa − fk) / m = (100 N − 58.8 N) / 20 kg = 41.2 N / 20 kg = 2.06 m/s2

Friction always subtracts from the applied force in the direction of motion — it never adds to it.

This is the template for nearly every friction problem for the rest of the unit: find N, find f, then run ΣF = ma exactly like a Week 1 problem with one extra force added. Students who solved Week 1 two-force problems should recognize this as the identical structure with friction playing the role of the "second force."

Quick Check

On your whiteboard

Friction force from μ and N
  1. A crate weighs 150 N and sits on a floor with μs = 0.35. What's the maximum static friction force?
  2. The same crate is now sliding, with μk = 0.25. What's the kinetic friction force?
  3. Which is bigger — the answer to (1) or (2)? Is that what you'd expect?
Answers: (1) f_s,max = 0.35 × 150 N = 52.5 N. (2) f_k = 0.25 × 150 N = 37.5 N. (3) (1) is bigger, which matches μs > μk from slide 10 — it's harder to start sliding than to keep it going. Note this problem gives weight directly (150 N) instead of mass, so there's no need to multiply by g — flag that shortcut for students who reflexively reach for m and g.

Putting It Together

When the Push Isn't Horizontal

An angled pull changes more than just the horizontal force.

This divider sets up the hardest problem of the day — an angled applied force that changes the normal force too. It's a deliberate preview of the "N isn't always mg" idea that becomes central in Week 4's inclines.

Worked Example · 3 of 3

A rope pulls up and forward at the same time

A 15 kg box is pulled by a rope at 30° above horizontal with tension T = 80 N. The floor has μk = 0.25. Find the box's acceleration.

StepWhat you write
Km = 15 kg, T = 80 N at 30°, μk = 0.25
Dacceleration a (target unit: m/s2)
Evertical: N = mg − T sinθ (T's upward part reduces N); horizontal: ΣF = T cosθ − μkN = ma
AN = (15)(9.8) − (80)(sin 30°) = 147 N − 40 N = 107 N
fk = (0.25)(107 N) = 26.75 N
T cosθ = (80)(cos 30°) ≈ 69.3 N
a = (69.3 N − 26.75 N) / 15 kg ≈ 2.8 m/s2

The rope's upward pull lightened the box on the floor — less N means less friction, even though nothing about the surface changed.

This is the hardest problem of the week on purpose — it directly overturns "N always equals mg" from Week 1. Walk the logic slowly: the rope has a vertical component holding some of the box's weight, so the floor doesn't have to push up as hard, so N drops, so friction drops too. This is exactly the mechanism that will reappear as "N = mg cos θ" on an incline in Week 4 — flag the connection explicitly.

Common Trap

Normal force is not automatically equal to weight

N comes from vertical equilibrium on the FBD — it equals mg only when gravity is the only vertical force. The instant another force has a vertical component (an angled pull, a ramp), N changes too.

The check

Before writing N = mg, ask: is anything else pushing or pulling this object up or down? If yes, N has to come from the full vertical ΣF = 0 equation, not a shortcut.

This is the single biggest friction-problem trap for the rest of the unit, and it only gets more important once inclines arrive in Week 4 (where N = mg cos θ, not mg at all). Students who memorized "N = mg" as a rule instead of a special case will struggle the moment a problem breaks the pattern.
