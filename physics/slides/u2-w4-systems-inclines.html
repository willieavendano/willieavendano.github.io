Why This Matters

Same FBD. Same KDEA. Harder pictures.

Nothing new gets added to the toolkit — no new law, no new force. What's new is the picture: more than one object, or a surface that isn't flat. Both are solved with the same free-body diagram and ΣF = ma you've used for three weeks.

  • Systems — two or more objects connected by a string, sharing one acceleration
  • Tension — the force a string transmits, the same magnitude at both ends
  • Inclines — gravity gets split into two components along tilted axes
  • N = mg cosθ — the incline version of the warning from Weeks 1–2

This week is combination, not addition. You already have every tool you need.

Systems

Systems of Objects

Connected objects share one acceleration — and a string that pulls both ways equally.

Set up the core idea before any numbers: a taut string forces two objects to move together, so they share a single value of a. That shared a is what makes "treat it as one system" a valid shortcut.

Systems · 1 of 4

Two ways to look at connected objects

The system view

Treat every connected object as one combined mass. Any force internal to the system (like the string's tension) cancels out — it pulls one object forward and the other backward with equal strength. Only external forces matter for the system's acceleration.

The individual view

Draw a separate FBD for just one object. The string's tension now shows up as a real, external force on that object — this is how you solve for T itself.

Use the system view to find a. Use one object's individual FBD to find T. Most problems need both.

This two-view idea is the single most important concept of the "systems" half of the week — students who try to find both a and T from one FBD get stuck. The system view eliminates T from the algebra entirely (it's internal); the individual view is the only place T reappears.

Systems · 2 of 4

Two blocks, one string, one push

A 3.0 kg block and a 2.0 kg block sit on a frictionless table, connected by a string between them. A 20 N force pulls the whole system forward. Find the system's acceleration and the tension in the connecting string.

The plan
  1. System view: total mass, external force only → find a.
  2. Individual view: isolate the back block (only touched by T) → find T.

The check on block 1 is worth doing live — it's free verification that the system/individual split was done correctly, and it shows T doing exactly what the "internal force" idea from the previous slide predicted: pulling block 1 backward by the same 8.0 N it pulls block 2 forward.

Systems · 4 of 4

Two hanging masses, one pulley

A 5.0 kg mass and a 3.0 kg mass hang from opposite ends of a string over a frictionless, massless pulley. Find the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string.

The plan
  1. The heavier mass falls; the lighter one rises — same string, same speed, same magnitude of a.
  2. System view: the driving force is the difference in weight. Find a.
  3. Individual view: isolate either mass to find T.
This is the classic Atwood machine — a genuinely famous setup, worth naming historically (used since the 1700s to measure g precisely, since a comes out much smaller than g itself). Students sometimes want to set T = weight of one side; walk through why that's only true if the system isn't accelerating.

Aa = (m1 − m2)g / (m1 + m2)

Notice: a ≈ 2.45 m/s2 is much smaller than g. The heavier side isn't in free fall — the lighter side is holding some of it back.

The "T ≠ either weight" point is worth stating plainly: T = 36.75 N is between the two weights (29.4 N and 49 N), which is the physical signature of a correctly-solved Atwood problem. If a student's T comes out equal to one side's weight, that's a sign they treated the system as non-accelerating by mistake.

Inclines

Inclined Planes

Tilt the axes with the ramp — gravity still points straight down.

The single hardest conceptual shift of the week: gravity itself never changes direction, but the AXES students draw do. Everything downstream (which trig function goes where) depends on getting this reorientation right first.

Inclines · 1 of 4

Tilt your axes, then split gravity in two

On an incline, it's easier to rotate your x-axis to point down the slope and your y-axis perpendicular to the surface. Gravity still points straight down — but relative to the new axes, it has two components. mg sinθ runs down the slope; mg cosθ presses into it. Only the perpendicular component is balanced, so N = mg cosθ — not mg.

mg sinθ
parallel component
Points down the slope. This is what actually accelerates the object along the incline.
mg cosθ
perpendicular component
Presses the object into the surface. This is what the normal force has to balance.
N = mg cosθ
normal force
Balances only the perpendicular component — not the full weight. This is the payoff of Weeks 1–2's warning.
This is the direct cash-in of "N isn't always mg" from the Week 1 book-on-table slide and Week 2's angled-pull problem. On a flat floor, θ = 0, cos 0° = 1, and N = mg falls right back out as a special case — worth showing that continuity explicitly. Draw the rotated axes on the board, not just describe them.

Inclines · 2 of 4

θ = 30°, frictionless — find the acceleration

StepWhat you write
Kθ = 30°, frictionless (no f term)
Dacceleration a, down the slope (target unit: m/s2)
EΣF = mg sinθ = ma → mass cancels: a = g sinθ
Aa = (9.8 m/s2)(sin 30°) = (9.8)(0.5) = 4.9 m/s2

Mass cancels out of a frictionless incline entirely — a heavier block and a lighter block slide down at the same rate.

The mass-cancellation result is worth pausing on — it's the incline version of the classic "all objects fall at the same rate" result from free fall in Unit 1, and it surprises students the same way. Once friction is added back in on the next slide, mass reappears in the friction term, which is worth flagging as the reason the cancellation breaks.

Inclines · 3 of 4

Same incline, now with friction

The same 30° ramp now has μk = 0.20 between the block and the surface. Find the acceleration.

StepWhat you write
Kθ = 30°, μk = 0.20
Dacceleration a, down the slope (target unit: m/s2)
EN = mg cosθ; fk = μkN; ΣF = mg sinθ − μkmg cosθ = ma → a = g(sinθ − μkcosθ)
Aa = (9.8)(sin 30° − 0.20 · cos 30°)
a = (9.8)(0.5 − 0.20 × 0.866) = (9.8)(0.5 − 0.173)
a = (9.8)(0.327) ≈ 3.2 m/s2

Friction is subtracted because it always opposes the direction of sliding — here, that's down the slope.

Mass canceled out again — friction depends on N, which is proportional to m, so the m's cancel the same way they did on the frictionless slide. That's worth pointing out: friction changes the NUMBER, not whether mass cancels. The ≈ is required here since cos 30° is irrational — flag it as an honest rounding, not a mistake.

Inclines · 4 of 4

On your whiteboard: does it even move?

The static case

A block sits on a 20° ramp with μs = 0.30. Does it slide on its own, or stay put?

Hint: compare tanθ to μs — think about why that ratio is the one that matters, using what you know about sinθ, cosθ, and the two forces along the slope.

Answer: tan 20° ≈ 0.364, which is greater than μs = 0.30, so the block slides on its own. The reasoning: the block stays put only if mg sinθ ≤ μsN = μs mg cosθ, which rearranges to tanθ ≤ μs — the mass cancels again. This is the "critical angle" idea; don't hand over the derivation, let the hint guide them to rediscover tanθ = μs themselves.

The Hardest Combination

A System, On an Incline

Everything from this week, in one diagram.

This is the capstone problem of the week — both new ideas (systems, inclines) in a single setup. Frame it as proof that nothing new is required, just careful bookkeeping across two FBDs.

The Hardest Combination

A block on a ramp, connected over a pulley to a hanging mass

A 5.0 kg block sits on a frictionless 30° incline. A string runs over a pulley at the top of the ramp to a 3.0 kg mass hanging off the edge of the table. Find the system's acceleration and the string's tension.

StepSystem: find aHanging mass alone: find T
Km1 = 5.0 kg (incline), m2 = 3.0 kg (hanging), θ = 30°m2 = 3.0 kg, a ≈ 0.61 m/s2 (m2 descending)
Dacceleration a (m/s2)tension T (N)
Em2g − m1g sinθ = (m1 + m2)a — T is internal againm2g − T = m2a
Am2g = 29.4 N, m1g sin30° = 24.5 N
a = (29.4 − 24.5) / 8.0 = 4.9 / 8.0 = 0.6125 ≈ 0.61 m/s2		T = m2g − m2a = 29.4 − (3.0)(0.6125)
T = 29.4 − 1.84 ≈ 27.6 N

The hanging mass wins the tug-of-war (29.4 N > 24.5 N), so it descends and drags the block up the ramp.

Walk the "who wins" comparison explicitly before computing a — it tells you the direction of motion (and therefore the sign of every term) before any arithmetic starts, exactly like the "which side is heavier" reasoning from the Atwood machine. If m1g sinθ had been larger, the block would slide down and the hanging mass would rise instead, flipping every sign.

Common Trap

Sine or cosine? Check the extremes

Parallel (down the slope) pairs with sine; perpendicular (into the surface) pairs with cosine — because θ sits between the incline and the horizontal.

Check at θ = 0°

Flat ground: sin 0° = 0, cos 0° = 1. Gravity should be all perpendicular, none parallel. If not, they're swapped.

Check at θ = 90°

Vertical wall: gravity all parallel, and N = 0. If your formula doesn't collapse to that, they're swapped.

These two sanity checks (θ = 0° and θ = 90°) are a powerful, reusable habit — students can verify ANY incline formula they've derived by checking it collapses correctly at the flat and vertical extremes, without needing to re-derive anything from scratch on a test.

Test Prep

Unit 2 is one story, not five topics.

Forces & FBDs → the three laws → friction → systems & inclines

This divider opens the review half of the week — folded in because the unit was compressed. The biggest failure mode on the test is treating FBDs, the three laws, friction, and systems/inclines as separate things to memorize. They aren't — every one of them is the same ΣF = ma applied to a progressively richer picture.

Test Prep · 1 of 3

One tool, four applications

Everything in Unit 2 is the same chain. Each step inherits everything from the step before it:

1
Free-body diagrams
Isolate the object, draw every force acting ON it. This is where every problem starts.
2
First & second laws
ΣF = 0 means equilibrium; otherwise ΣF = ma. This is the equation you always end up running.
3
Third law & friction
Every force pairs with one on another object. Friction adds one more force to the FBD: fs ≤ μsN, fk = μkN.
4
Systems & inclines
More objects (system view + individual view) or tilted axes (mg sinθ, mg cosθ). Same ΣF = ma, richer pictures.

There is no separate "incline equation" or "system equation." There's ΣF = ma, run on a better diagram. That's the whole unit.

Each tile builds on the one before it. If a student can draw an FBD (tile 1) and apply ΣF = ma (tile 2), then friction (tile 3) is just one more arrow on the diagram, and systems/inclines (tile 4) is just a harder diagram with the same equation. The chip is the takeaway — say it aloud and leave it on screen.

Test Prep · 2 of 3

Four traps that cost the most points

1
Friction's direction
It opposes relative sliding, not your push. A box sliding right while you push left still has friction pointing left.
2
Units must travel
Force is always N = kg·m/s2. μ has no unit — "μ = 0.25 N" is always wrong.
3
N is not automatically mg
Only when gravity is the only vertical force on a flat surface. Angled pulls and tilted surfaces change it — rebuild N from ΣFvertical = 0.
4
Right view on a system
You can't get both a and T from one equation: system view for a, individual view for T.

Every one of these is a KDEA or FBD discipline problem, not a physics one. The protocol prevents all four.

These were four separate slides in the old review week; compressed to one. Cold-call one student per tile to restate the trap in their own words — fast, and it surfaces who still thinks N = mg unconditionally. Trap #3 is the one that reappears most on the unit test.

Confidence Check · Turn & Talk · 10 minutes

One last problem before Thursday

With your partner

An 8.0 kg sled is pulled across snow by a rope at 20° above horizontal with tension 25 N. The snow has μk = 0.10.

  1. Find the normal force — remember the rope has a vertical component.
  2. Find the kinetic friction force.
  3. Does the sled accelerate? If so, find how fast.

This is the exact shape of a test problem, with two of this unit's biggest ideas stacked in one scenario.

This closer combines Trap #3 (N ≠ mg when a pull has a vertical component, from Week 2) with a standard friction-and-acceleration solve. Full solution: N = mg − T sinθ = 78.4 − 25(sin20°) = 78.4 − 8.55 ≈ 69.85 N. f_k = μkN ≈ 6.99 N. Horizontal component of T = 25cos20° ≈ 23.49 N. Net force ≈ 23.49 − 6.99 ≈ 16.50 N. a = 16.50 / 8.0 ≈ 2.06 m/s². Have tables report their acceleration and confirm the sled does move — the judgment step matters as much as the number.
