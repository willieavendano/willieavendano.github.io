Why This Matters

Forces told you how hard. Energy tells you how much got done.

In Unit 2 you learned to find the net force on an object. That force acted for some amount of time (Unit 1's variable). This unit asks a different question: what happens when a force acts over a distance?

  • Work — a force acting through a displacement
  • Kinetic energy — the energy of motion
  • Potential energy — stored energy, waiting to act
  • KDEA — same four steps, three new equations

New vocabulary, same protocol. If you can run KDEA, you can learn Unit 4.

This slide is the pitch: energy isn't a new way of thinking, it's a new set of equations plugged into the exact same four-step move students have used since Workshop II. Reassure them explicitly — the hard part of this unit is definitions and sign conventions, not a new method.

Force Meets Distance

Work

A force acting through a displacement

Frame work as the bridge concept between Unit 2 (forces) and today's new territory (energy). Every example this week should physically involve a force AND a displacement — if either is missing, no work is being done, no matter how hard something is pushing.

Work · 1 of 3

Work = force × distance × the angle between them

Only the part of the force that points along the direction of motion actually does work. A force pushing sideways to the motion contributes nothing.

Definition

W = F d cosθ

  • F — the applied force, in N
  • d — the displacement, in m
  • θ — the angle between the force and the displacement

Work is a scalar — no direction, just a signed number.

"Scalar" is worth pausing on — students just spent Unit 1–3 treating force, velocity, and acceleration as vectors. Work is the first major scalar quantity since distance/speed back in Unit 1. cosθ is the whole mechanism: it's what strips out the part of the force that isn't helping the motion along.

Work · 3 of 3

Work can be positive, negative, or exactly zero

θ = 0°
same direction
cos 0° = 1 — force helps the motion. Positive work, maximum size.
θ = 180°
opposite direction
cos 180° = −1 — force fights the motion. Negative work — friction's signature.
θ = 90°
perpendicular
cos 90° = 0 — force does nothing to the motion. Zero work, no matter how big F is.

Carrying a heavy backpack across a flat hallway: gravity pulls straight down, you walk straight forward — gravity does zero work on you.

The backpack example is the one that sticks — students' intuition says "carrying something heavy is work," but physics says gravity (perpendicular to the horizontal walk) does zero work on the backpack. This is the single most common misconception at the start of the unit; spend real time on it. Contrast with lifting the backpack straight up, where gravity does negative work on the way up.

Quick Check

On your whiteboard: positive, negative, or zero?

Sign of the work done by the named force
  1. You push a stalled car forward; the car moves forward. Sign of the work you do?
  2. A crate slides across a rough floor and slows down. Sign of the work friction does on it?
  3. A satellite orbits the Earth in a perfect circle at constant speed. Sign of the work gravity does on it over one full orbit?
Answers: (1) positive — force and motion align. (2) negative — friction always opposes relative motion. (3) zero — gravity (centripetal) is always perpendicular to the circular velocity; this is a deliberate callback to Unit 3 orbits, and the trickiest of the three. If students struggle with #3, redraw the circle and show the radius (force direction) is always perpendicular to the tangent (velocity direction).

Where Work Goes

Energy

The capacity to do work — moving, or stored, waiting

Pivot from "work" (the transaction) to "energy" (what gets transferred). Keep the framing simple: energy is the currency, work is how it moves from place to place. Two forms today — kinetic and gravitational potential — with more forms (elastic, thermal) mentioned only in passing.

Energy · 1 of 2

Kinetic energy: the energy of motion

Definition

KE = ½mv2

  • m — mass, in kg
  • v — speed, in m/s
  • Units: kg·(m/s)2 = kg·m2/s2 = J

KE can never be negative — v is squared, so a slow object and a fast one moving backward both give a positive number.

The squared-velocity structure means direction disappears entirely — KE only cares about speed. This is a preview of a bigger idea coming in Unit 5: kinetic energy is what distinguishes an elastic collision (KE conserved) from an inelastic one (KE lost to heat/deformation) in the Momentum unit's collision lab. Don't over-explain now — just plant the flag.

Energy · 2 of 2

Gravitational potential energy: stored height

Definition

PE = mgh

  • m — mass, in kg
  • g — 9.8 m/s2, the same g from Unit 1 free fall
  • h — height above a reference point you choose, in m

PE has no absolute value — only changes in PE matter physically. Set h = 0 wherever is convenient (the floor, the table, the bottom of the ramp) and stay consistent.

"Only changes matter" is the conceptual crux — students want a single correct h = 0, and there isn't one. Whatever reference point you pick, ΔPE comes out the same. This directly sets up next week's conservation-of-energy work, where PE differences (not absolute PE) are what the equation actually uses.

KDEA in Action

The Work-Energy Theorem

Where "force over a distance" meets "energy of motion"

This divider is the payoff of the whole week: work and kinetic energy aren't separate ideas, they're directly linked by one equation. Everything from slides 5–11 has been building toward this connection.

Work-Energy Theorem

Net work done = the change in kinetic energy

Definition

Wnet = ΔKE = KEf − KEi

If the total work done on an object is positive, its kinetic energy goes up — it speeds up. If it's negative, KE goes down — it slows down.

This is Newton's second law wearing a different outfit — same physics, energy language.

Explicitly tie back to F = ma from Unit 2: the work-energy theorem is derivable from Newton's second law (a constant-force, constant-acceleration case), it just gets you to the answer faster when you only care about speed, not time. Don't derive it algebraically here — that's more than this course needs — just assert the connection.

Worked Example · 1 of 2

Push a crate, find its final speed

A 15 kg crate starts at rest on a frictionless floor. You push it with a constant 60 N force, in the direction it moves, across 4.0 m. How fast is it going?

StepWhat you write
KF = 60 N, d = 4.0 m, θ = 0°, m = 15 kg, vi = 0 m/s
Dfinal speed vf (target unit: m/s)
EW = F d cosθ, then Wnet = ΔKE = ½mvf2 − ½mvi2
AW = (60 N)(4.0 m)(cos 0°) = 240 J
240 J = ½(15 kg)vf2 − 0  →  vf = √(2·240/15) ≈ 5.7 m/s

Two equations, one KDEA table — work first, then the theorem hands you speed without ever finding time.

Point out what didn't happen: no kinematics equation, no time variable at all. This is the selling point of energy methods — some problems that would need multiple kinematics steps collapse into one work-energy line. The √ leaves an irrational number, so ≈ is the honest choice, not =.

Worked Example · 2 of 2

Lift a backpack, find the change in PE

A 5.0 kg backpack is lifted from the floor onto a shelf 1.8 m high. What is the change in its gravitational potential energy?

StepWhat you write
Km = 5.0 kg, hi = 0 m, hf = 1.8 m, g = 9.8 m/s2
DΔPE (target unit: J)
EPE = mgh  →  ΔPE = mg(hf − hi)
AΔPE = (5.0 kg)(9.8 m/s2)(1.8 m − 0 m)
ΔPE = 88.2 J ≈ 88 J

The reference point (h = 0 at the floor) was our choice — a different choice would give the same ΔPE.

Round 88.2 to 88 J for sig figs (two sig figs from the given data), and ≈ is correct here too since we rounded. This is also a good moment to note the backpack got 88 J of stored energy without moving at all — potential energy is real energy, just not energy of motion.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 10 minutes

One problem, both new ideas

With your partner

A 2.0 kg ball is thrown straight up. At the moment it leaves your hand it has 18 J of kinetic energy. Air resistance is negligible.

  1. Find its launch speed using the KE definition.
  2. At its highest point, all 18 J has become potential energy. Find the maximum height.

Notice: the 18 J never disappeared — it just changed which equation was holding it.

Answers: (1) 18 = ½(2.0)v² → v² = 18 → v ≈ 4.2 m/s. (2) 18 = (2.0)(9.8)h → h ≈ 0.92 m. This is a soft preview of next week's conservation of energy without naming it yet — let students notice on their own that the 18 J "moved" from KE to PE. If a group says it out loud, highlight it to the room; don't force the vocabulary yet.
