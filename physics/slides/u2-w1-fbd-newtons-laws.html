Forces

What Is a Force?

A push or a pull — always a magnitude, a direction, and something doing the pushing.

Plant the seed for next week without naming it: every force comes from an interaction between two objects. That idea is Newton's third law, but don't say the words yet — let it surface naturally when the third law divider hits next week.

Forces · 1 of 2

Every force is a vector — the unit is the newton

A force has a magnitude (how hard) and a direction (which way). Draw it as an arrow: length for magnitude, direction for direction. The unit, the newton, is a derived unit you already met in Unit 0 — N = kg·m/s2, straight from F = ma.

Contact forces — touching required

Normal force, applied force, tension, friction — every one of these needs the object to be physically touching something.

Field forces — act at a distance

Gravity is the only field force we use this unit — it pulls on every object with mass, no contact required.

Friction gets its own full week starting next class — don't over-teach it here, just place it in the contact-force family. The N = kg·m/s² unit check is a direct callback to the Unit 0 Workshop I "units you'll earn this year" slide, where newton was previewed exactly for this unit.

Newton's Laws · 1 of 2

Newton's First Law

An object's velocity doesn't change — unless a net force acts on it.

The everyday version: at rest stays at rest, moving stays moving, unless something pushes or pulls. This is the law of inertia — mass is literally the measure of how much an object resists that change, which sets up the second law's m in the denominator.

First Law · 1 of 2

Inertia: matter resists a change in its motion

If the net force on an object is zero, its velocity does not change — not speed, not direction. That includes an object sitting perfectly still.

Rule — equilibrium

An object with ΣF = 0 is in equilibrium — it could be sitting still, or cruising at constant velocity in a straight line. Both count as “no net force.”

Constant velocity (including zero) means ΣF = 0. Every time.

This is the exact same idea as Unit 1's "flat v-t graph ≠ stopped" lesson, now framed in forces instead of graphs. The instinctive-but-wrong belief is that moving objects need a continuous push to keep moving — Aristotle's mistake, not Newton's. Name that misconception directly; it's the single biggest first-law error.

First Law · 2 of 2

Quick check — is it in equilibrium?

On your whiteboard
  1. A book sits motionless on a table.
  2. A hockey puck slides across frictionless ice at a constant 4 m/s.
  3. A car speeds up from a stoplight.

Which ones have ΣF = 0?

Answers: (1) yes — stationary. (2) yes — this is the one that trips students up, since it's moving, but constant velocity still means zero net force. (3) no — speeding up means accelerating, so ΣF ≠ 0. Cold call after a minute; scenario 2 is the formative check that actually matters here.

The Tool

Free-Body Diagrams

Isolate the object. Draw every force acting ON it. Nothing else.

The FBD is the visual tool that makes the first and second laws usable on an actual problem. Everything from here through the rest of the unit — friction, systems, inclines — starts with a correct FBD.

FBDs · 1 of 3

Four steps, every time

The method
  1. Isolate the object — draw it as a single dot or box, ignore everything else in the picture.
  2. Ask: what is touching this object, or acting on it at a distance? That's your list of forces.
  3. Draw each force as an arrow starting at the center, pointing in its true direction.
  4. Label every arrow with its symbol: Fg, N, T, Fa, f.

Only forces ON the object. Never a force the object exerts on something else — that's next week's law.

The chip is a deliberate, accurate forward pointer to Newton's third law next week — don't explain it yet, just flag that "forces the object exerts outward" are a different, later idea. Step 2 is where most FBD errors happen: students either invent a force that isn't there or forget one that is.

FBDs · 2 of 3

A book resting on a table

The book sits still. No one is touching it except the table underneath.

The forces

Fg down — the book's weight. N up — the table pushing back.

The equilibrium check

The book isn't accelerating, so ΣF = 0 → N = Fg, exactly.

A stationary object is always in equilibrium — draw the diagram, then let the arrows balance.

The normal force is NOT always equal to weight by definition — it equals weight here because this object happens to be in vertical equilibrium on a flat surface. That distinction matters in three weeks when inclines and vertical pushes change N. Plant the seed now: "N is whatever equilibrium requires," not "N always equals mg."

FBDs · 3 of 3

Turn and talk: draw it yourself

With your partner — 5 minutes

Draw the free-body diagram for each:

  1. A shop sign hangs motionless from a single rope attached to the ceiling.
  2. A shopping cart rolls at a constant velocity across a flat, frictionless floor — no one is touching it.
Sign: T straight up, Fg straight down, equal magnitude — same logic as the book. Cart is the real test: constant velocity + frictionless means ΣF = 0 horizontally too, so there is NO forward force at all — only N up and Fg down. Students who draw a forward "motion force" out of habit are showing exactly the Aristotelian misconception flagged on the first-law slide. Circulate for that specific error.

Newton's Laws · 2 of 2

Newton's Second Law

When forces don't balance, ΣF = ma.

The first law told us what happens when ΣF = 0. The second law tells us what happens the rest of the time — the net force determines the acceleration directly. Frame it as the natural next question.

Second Law · 1 of 3

ΣF = ma — net force causes acceleration

The net force is the vector sum of every force on your FBD. The acceleration it produces points the same direction as the net force. Mass is the resistance to that acceleration — the same inertia from the first law, now with a number attached.

Rule

Bigger net force → bigger acceleration (direct). Bigger mass → smaller acceleration for the same force (inverse). Same idea as inertia — just with numbers now.

Units check: N = kg·m/s2 — exactly what Unit 0 predicted when force earned its unit.

Direct callback to the Unit 0 Workshop I "units you'll earn this year" tile grid, where N was labeled "Unit 2, Forces" months before this unit started. Point it out explicitly — the map from Workshop I is finally paying off.

Worked Example · 1 of 2

Two forces, one direction each

A 5.0 kg box sits on a frictionless floor. One student pushes with 12 N to the right; another pushes with 4.0 N to the left. Find the box's acceleration.

StepWhat you write
KF1 = 12 N (right), F2 = 4.0 N (left), m = 5.0 kg
Dacceleration a (target unit: m/s2)
EΣF = ma — net force is F1 − F2 (opposite directions)
Asymbols first: a = (F1 − F2) / m
a = (12 N − 4.0 N) / 5.0 kg = 8.0 N / 5.0 kg = 1.6 m/s2 (to the right)

The direction of a matches the direction of the bigger force — right, the same way ΣF pointed.

Pick a positive direction before subtracting, and say so out loud. The most common error here is adding 12 + 4 instead of subtracting, because students forget the forces oppose. Tie the sign of the answer explicitly back to physical direction, not just a number.

Worked Example · 2 of 2

Two forces at a right angle

A 10 kg crate sits on frictionless ice. One person pushes east with 30 N; another pushes north with 40 N, at the same time. Find the net force and the crate's acceleration.

StepWhat you write
KF1 = 30 N (east), F2 = 40 N (north), m = 10 kg
Dnet force Fnet (N) and direction; acceleration a (m/s2)
Eright angle → \( F_{net} = \sqrt{F_1^2 + F_2^2} \), \( \theta = \tan^{-1}(F_2/F_1) \) — Workshop III trig; \( a = F_{net} / m \)
AFnet = √(302 + 402) = √(900 + 1600) = √2500 = 50 N
θ = tan−1(40/30) ≈ 53.1° north of east
a = 50 N / 10 kg = 5.0 m/s2, same direction as Fnet

Combining forces at an angle is the same right-triangle trig from Unit 0 Workshop III — only the labels changed.

First time this unit combines non-parallel forces — reassure students this is not new math, just a new application of SOH-CAH-TOA and the Pythagorean theorem from Workshop III. This exact move (combine two components into a magnitude and angle) comes back for inclines in Week 4.

Quick Check

On your whiteboard

Three F = ma questions
  1. A 2.0 kg cart is pushed with a net force of 6.0 N. Find its acceleration.
  2. A 1200 kg car accelerates at 3.0 m/s2. What net force does that require?
  3. True or false: a stationary object always has zero net force acting on it.
Answers: (1) 3.0 m/s². (2) 3600 N. (3) TRUE — stationary means constant velocity at v = 0, which is exactly the first-law equilibrium condition. This question deliberately loops back to slide 7–8 to confirm the first and second laws are landing as one connected idea, not two separate facts.
