Triangles & Vectors

Workshop III

SOH-CAH-TOA · right triangles only · components · Pythagorean theorem

Last workshop before Thursday's Skills Check. Everything today builds directly on Workshop I (units, Greek symbols — θ is back) and Workshop II (KDEA — every worked example today still runs K/D/E/A). Set the promise early: right triangles only, nothing more exotic, all year in this course.

Why This Matters

Vectors have direction. Direction needs a triangle.

Every quantity you've used so far this year — a speed, a mass, a distance — was just a number and a unit. Starting in Unit 1, some quantities get a second property: direction. A velocity of 20 m/s means nothing until you also know which way.

  • A drone climbing at an angle
  • A force pulling sideways and down at once
  • A ramp — literally a right triangle you can walk on
  • Any motion that isn't perfectly horizontal or vertical

Today's tool for handling direction: the right triangle.

The framing move: vectors aren't a new topic bolted onto physics, they're what happens the moment motion or force stops being purely horizontal or vertical. A ramp is the cleanest possible example — it's not "like" a triangle, it IS one, and that's why this workshop exists before Unit 1 gets moving.

The Promise

Right Triangles Only.

No unit circle. No radians. No trig identities. Every triangle we touch this year has a 90° corner — full stop.

Say this slide out loud, don't just show it. This is a genuine promise to a regular-level class, not a simplification we'll quietly abandon later — the entire year's trig stays right-triangle-only. If a problem doesn't have a 90° angle in it somewhere, it isn't a Workshop III problem.

Right Triangle Anatomy · 1 of 2

Three sides, one fixed, two relative

  • Hypotenuse — always the longest side, always opposite the 90° corner. Never moves, never changes name.
  • Opposite — the side across from the angle θ you're working with
  • Adjacent — the side touching θ that isn't the hypotenuse

Hypotenuse is fixed. Opposite and adjacent depend on which angle you pick.

Right Triangle Anatomy · 2 of 2

The classic trap: opposite and adjacent flip

Same 3-4-5 triangle, same two non-right angles — but which side is "opposite" changes depending on which corner you're standing at:

Reference angleOpposite sideAdjacent side
37° cornerthe 3-sidethe 4-side
53° cornerthe 4-sidethe 3-side

The hypotenuse (the 5-side) never changes. Opposite and adjacent swap.

This is THE error to watch for on the Skills Check — a student who memorizes "opposite is the top side" instead of "opposite is relative to θ" gets every SOH-CAH-TOA problem wrong the moment the triangle rotates. Make them relabel this same triangle from both corners before moving on.

SOH-CAH-TOA · 1 of 2

Three ratios, one mnemonic

SOH
sin θ
Opposite / Hypotenuse
CAH
cos θ
Adjacent / Hypotenuse
TOA
tan θ
Opposite / Adjacent

Pick the ratio that has the side you know and the side you want. That's the entire strategy — no guessing which formula to use.

Frame the "pick the ratio" line as the actual decision rule: look at what K gives you and what D asks for, then choose whichever of SOH/CAH/TOA connects those two sides. This previews KDEA's E step — the ratio IS the equation.

Example 1 · Setup

The kite

A kite string makes a 42° angle with the ground. The string is fully let out at 65 m. How high above the ground is the kite flying?

Which ratio connects the string (hypotenuse) to the height (opposite)?

Example 1 · KDEA

65 m string, 42° → height

StepWhat you write
Khypotenuse = 65 m, θ = 42°
Dheight above ground (opposite side), target unit m
ESOH: sin θ = opposite / hypotenuse
Asymbols first: opposite = hypotenuse × sin θ  →  65 m × sin 42° ≈ 65 m × 0.669 ≈ 43 m

≈, not = — sin 42° is a rounded decimal, so the final height is an approximation too.

Pythagorean Theorem

No angle? Use the sides instead.

SOH-CAH-TOA needs an angle. When you only have two sides and want the third — and you don't care about an angle at all — the Pythagorean theorem is faster:

The relationship

a² + b² = c²

a and b are the two legs (opposite and adjacent). c is always the hypotenuse.

This is where "magnitude" comes from — the length of a vector, once you know both components.

Tie this explicitly to the vector-components framing that closes the deck: once a vector is broken into x and y pieces, the Pythagorean theorem is how you get back its total length (magnitude). This slide plants that seed before components are formally introduced later.

Example 2 · Setup

The hiker

A hiker walks 8 km east, then 6 km north. How far is she from where she started, in a straight line — and in what direction?

Two questions, two tools: magnitude first (Pythagorean), direction second (inverse trig).

This is a classic component-style problem run backward: given the east/north legs, find the straight-line resultant. That's exactly the reverse of the drone problem waiting at the end of the deck — flag that connection here if there's time, or let it land as a surprise later.

Example 2 · Magnitude

8 km east, 6 km north → straight-line distance

StepWhat you write
Klegs = 8 km (east), 6 km (north)
Dstraight-line distance (hypotenuse), target unit km
Ea² + b² = c²
Ac = √(8² + 6²) = √(64 + 36) = √100 = 10 km

Exactly 10 km — no rounding needed here, the numbers work out clean.

Finding The Angle

Inverse trig undoes the ratio

sin, cos, and tan turn an angle into a ratio. tan−1, sin−1, and cos−1 turn a ratio back into an angle.

The "undo" framing matters more than the button on the calculator — students who think of tan−1 (tan inverse) as "a different, harder thing" freeze. It's the same relationship run backward, same as how division undoes multiplication. No unit circle needed to make this true.

Example 2 · Direction

Same hiker — which way, exactly?

StepWhat you write
Kopposite = 6 km (north), adjacent = 8 km (east)
Dangle θ north of east, target unit °
ETOA: tan θ = opposite / adjacent
Aθ = tan−1(6/8) = tan−1(0.75) ≈ 36.87° ≈ 37°

10 km at ≈37° north of east — magnitude AND direction, the full vector.

Land the phrase "magnitude AND direction" hard — that pair is literally the definition of a vector, and this is the first time in the workshop students have produced both halves of one. Keep the 37° in their head; it comes back on purpose in a few slides.

Quick Check

On your whiteboard

Three problems, three tools
  1. A ladder 10 m long leans against a wall at a 65° angle with the ground. How high up the wall does it reach?
  2. A boat sails 5 km west, then 12 km south. Find the straight-line distance back to the marina.
  3. A ramp has a rise of 3 m and a run of 4 m. What angle does it make with the ground?
Answers: (1) 10 sin65° ≈ 9.1 m — SOH. (2) √(5² + 12²) = √169 = 13 km — Pythagorean, another clean integer triple (5-12-13). (3) tan−1(3/4) ≈ 36.87° ≈ 37° — TOA, and yes, that's the same 37° from the hiker. Don't confirm it out loud yet if you want the payoff slide to land harder.

Vector Components

KDEA meets triangles

Every vector IS the hypotenuse of a right triangle. Break it into its horizontal and vertical pieces — its components — and SOH-CAH-TOA does the rest.

This is the payoff of the entire workshop: everything up to this point (anatomy, SOH-CAH-TOA, Pythagorean, inverse trig) was building toward this one idea. The hiker went sides → resultant; components go the other direction, resultant → sides. Same triangle, opposite direction.

Vector Components · Setup

Any vector splits into x and y

A vector of magnitude r at angle θ above the horizontal has a horizontal piece and a vertical piece — found with CAH and SOH, run in reverse:

Horizontal component

x = r × cos θ — adjacent side

Vertical component

y = r × sin θ — opposite side

Same two ratios from the SOH-CAH-TOA slide — just solved for the leg instead of the angle.

Explicitly point back to the SOH-CAH-TOA tile slide: nothing new is being taught here, it's the exact same two ratios rearranged. Students who think components are a separate topic from SOH-CAH-TOA are the ones who struggle with projectile motion in three weeks.

Example 3 · Setup

The drone

A survey drone climbs 5 km total distance, at an angle of 37° above the horizontal. How far did it travel horizontally? How far did it climb vertically?

This exact drone shows up again in this week's components video — we'll watch it after today.

This is the class Manim video (PHY-U0-vectors-components) — don't play it now, just name it and move on; a later slide points students to watch it. Keep the framing simple: same math as the hiker, run backward — resultant and angle given, components unknown.

Example 3 · KDEA

5 km at 37° → horizontal and vertical legs

StepWhat you write
Kr = 5 km, θ = 37°
Dx (horizontal), y (vertical), target unit km
Ex = r cos θ,   y = r sin θ
Ax = 5 × cos 37° ≈ 5 × 0.799 ≈ 4.0 km
y = 5 × sin 37° ≈ 5 × 0.602 ≈ 3.0 km

4.0 km east, 3.0 km up — check it: √(4.0² + 3.0²) ≈ 5.0 km. Back where we started.

The 3-4-5 Family

That 37° wasn't a coincidence

The hiker's 6-8-10 triangle and the drone's 3-4-5 triangle are the same triangle, just scaled. Both have the same two non-right angles — and those angles are worth memorizing:

Ratio37°53°
sin≈ 0.60 (3/5)≈ 0.80 (4/5)
cos≈ 0.80 (4/5)≈ 0.60 (3/5)
tan≈ 0.75 (3/4)≈ 1.33 (4/3)

3-4-5, 6-8-10, 9-12-15 — any right triangle in that ratio carries the same 37°/53° pair.

This is the deck's big "aha" — the hiker, the quick-check ramp, and the drone all quietly resolved to the same 37°/53° pair. Point out explicitly that this wasn't planned in the room — it's baked into the geometry. This pair is worth memorizing precisely because it keeps reappearing all year, starting with Unit 1 projectile motion and Unit 2 inclined planes.

Incline Preview

A ramp IS a right triangle

A loading ramp needs to reach a dock 1.5 m above the ground, rising at a 30° angle. How long does the ramp itself need to be?

StepWhat you write
Kopposite = 1.5 m, θ = 30°
Dramp length (hypotenuse), target unit m
ESOH: sin θ = opposite / hypotenuse
Ahypotenuse = opposite / sin θ = 1.5 m / sin 30° = 1.5 m / 0.5 = 3.0 m

In Unit 2, this same triangle comes back with forces on it — the ramp's angle decides how much weight the surface has to hold.
