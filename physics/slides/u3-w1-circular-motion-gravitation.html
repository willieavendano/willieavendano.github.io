What's Different Here

Uniform Circular Motion

Constant speed. Constantly changing direction.

Open with the paradox that anchors the whole unit: "constant speed" and "not accelerating" sound like the same thing — they aren't. Let that sit for a second before the next slide resolves it.

Uniform Circular Motion · 1 of 2

Speed can be constant while velocity isn't

Velocity is a vector — it has a magnitude (speed) and a direction. An object moving in a circle at a fixed speed still has its direction changing every instant, which means, by Unit 1's own definition, it is accelerating.

Definition, recalled from Unit 1

a = Δv / Δt — any change in the velocity vector counts, even if the speedometer never moves. Direction changing is enough.

This is the single biggest conceptual hurdle of the unit. Students hear "acceleration" and think "speeding up." Push back hard: acceleration is any change in the velocity vector, and direction change alone qualifies. If they don't buy this slide, nothing after it will make sense.

Uniform Circular Motion · 2 of 2

ac = v2/r, always toward the center

Definition

ac = v2 / r

  • Units: m/s2 — same unit as any other acceleration
  • Direction: always points toward the center of the circle, never outward
  • "Centripetal" literally means center-seeking

There is no outward "centrifugal force" pushing you — what you feel is your own inertia (Unit 1) resisting the turn.

Kill the centrifugal-force myth here, on day one, before it calcifies. What a passenger "feels" pushing them outward in a turning car is Newton's first law — their body's inertia trying to continue in a straight line while the car turns underneath them. There is no real outward force acting on them.

Quick Check

On your whiteboard

Two questions
  1. A ball on a string speeds up while circling at a fixed radius. Is ac constant? Why or why not?
  2. A car takes a curve of radius 2.0 m at 8.0 m/s. Find ac.
Answers: (1) No — ac = v²/r, so as v grows with r fixed, ac grows too. (Students don't need to know it yet, but there's also a tangential acceleration component whenever speed changes — worth a one-line mention if it comes up, full treatment isn't until Unit 6.) (2) ac = 8.0² / 2.0 = 32 m/s². Watch for students who forget to square v.

How Long Around?

Period & Frequency

Two ways to describe how fast something circles

Transition from "how hard is it turning" (ac) to "how long does one lap take" (T) — these feed into each other and both show up in every worked example this week.

Period & Frequency · 1 of 2

T is time for one lap. f is laps per second.

Definitions
  • Period, T — time for one full trip around the circle. Units: s
  • Frequency, f — laps per second. f = 1/T. Units: Hz
  • Speed from period: v = 2πr / T — circumference divided by time for one lap

Hz you met in Workshop I as 1/s — here it's literally "circles per second."

Direct callback to Unit 0 Workshop I's "units you'll earn this year" tile grid, where Hz was flagged as belonging to Units 7–8 (SHM & Waves) — this is actually its first real appearance, one unit early. Worth naming explicitly: the promise from Workshop I is paying off already.

Worked Example · 1 of 3

Ball on a string: r = 0.80 m, T = 0.50 s

StepWhat you write
Kr = 0.80 m, T = 0.50 s
Dspeed v (target unit: m/s) and ac (target unit: m/s2)
Ev = 2πr / T, then ac = v2 / r
Asymbols first: v = 2π(0.80 m) / 0.50 s ≈ 10.1 m/s
ac = (10.1 m/s)2 / 0.80 m ≈ 126 m/s2

≈, not = — π never terminates, so every downstream number is honestly rounded.

Model the KDEA layout exactly as it should appear on a test: two targets in D because the problem asks two questions, one E per target, symbols before numbers in A. The ≈ enforcement is deliberate — this is the first Unit 3 table, a good moment to re-anchor the "rounded values use ≈, never =" rule from Unit 0.

What Provides The Push?

Centripetal Force

Not a new force — just Newton's 2nd law, pointed at the center

Frame this divider as a warning as much as a topic: the word "centripetal" makes students think it's a brand-new kind of force to add to a free-body diagram. It isn't. The next few slides exist to prevent that exact mistake.

Centripetal Force · 1 of 3

Fc = mac = mv2/r

This is ΣF from Unit 2, aimed at a center

Centripetal force is just the name for whatever net force happens to point toward the center of the circle. Direction: always centerward. Units: N, same as any force.

Fc isn't a force you add to the FBD — it's the label for whatever real force (or combination) already points centerward.

THE #1 misconception in this unit: students draw tension or friction on a free-body diagram, then add a separate arrow labeled "centripetal force" pointing the same way, effectively double-counting. Correct framing: Fnet = Fc. There is no extra arrow. Say this explicitly before the next slide's examples or it will show up wrong on every homework.

Centripetal Force · 2 of 3

Real forces that can point toward the center

T
Tension
A ball on a string, swung in a horizontal circle — the string pulls inward.
fs
Friction
A car rounding a flat curve — static friction between tire and road supplies the inward pull.
Fg
Gravity
A moon or satellite in orbit — gravity itself is the centripetal force. Preview of next week.
FN
Normal force
A car on a banked, frictionless curve, or a rider at the top of a loop — the surface pushes inward.
Every one of these four is an FBD element students already know from Unit 2 — nothing new is being introduced, they're just being told which existing force plays the centripetal role in each scenario. Gravity is flagged as "next week" deliberately — it's the bridge into orbits.

Worked Example · 2 of 3

How much friction does a curve need?

A 1200 kg car rounds a flat curve of radius 50.0 m at 15.0 m/s. What minimum coefficient of static friction keeps it on the road?

StepWhat you write
Km = 1200 kg, v = 15.0 m/s, r = 50.0 m, g = 9.8 m/s2
Dminimum μs (no unit — a coefficient)
EFc = mv2/r; friction supplies it: μsmg = mv2/r
Asymbols first, mass cancels: μs = v2 / (rg)
μs = (15.0)2 / [(50.0)(9.8)] ≈ 0.459

μs, not μk — the tire's contact patch isn't sliding against the road.

Direct callback to the μs vs μk subscript distinction from Unit 0 Workshop I. Also point out the mass cancels entirely — a heavier car needs the same friction coefficient as a lighter one at the same speed and radius, which surprises students expecting "heavier = needs more grip."

Turn & Talk · 4 minutes

Identify the centripetal force

With your partner — name the source, not just "centripetal force"
  • The Moon orbiting Earth
  • A coin resting on a spinning turntable
  • A roller-coaster car at the very top of a loop
  • A car on a banked, frictionless curve
Answers: Moon — gravity. Coin — static friction between coin and turntable surface. Loop top — gravity AND the normal force both point downward (toward the center), so it's the sum of the two. Banked frictionless curve — the horizontal component of the normal force. The loop-top case is the hardest; don't be surprised if most pairs only name one force there — use it as the cold-call.

The Force That Holds Orbits Together

Universal Gravitation

Every mass pulls on every other mass

Transition from "forces that happen to be centripetal" to the one force that is centripetal by its very nature at planetary scale: gravity. This divider sets up the rest of the deck and next week's orbits.

Universal Gravitation · 1 of 3

Fg = Gm1m2 / r2

Definition
  • G = 6.674 × 10−11 N·m2/kg2 — a universal constant, the same everywhere in the universe
  • Inverse-square law — double the distance, and the force drops to a quarter
  • Always attractive, always along the line connecting the two masses

Same F, same units N as every other force this year — gravity isn't special, it's just usually weak.

Flag the G-vs-g distinction hard right now: capital G is this universal constant, brand new this unit. Lowercase g is Earth's surface acceleration from Unit 1 free fall (≈ 9.8 m/s2) — related (g = GMearth/Rearth2, not required knowledge this week, just plant the seed) but not the same symbol. Students will conflate these on the test if it isn't named explicitly.

Worked Example · 3 of 3

The pull between Earth and the Moon

StepWhat you write
KMearth = 5.97 × 1024 kg, mmoon = 7.35 × 1022 kg, r = 3.84 × 108 m, G = 6.674 × 10−11 N·m2/kg2
DFg (target unit: N)
EFg = GMearthmmoon / r2
Asymbols first, then substitute:
Fg = (6.674×10−11)(5.97×1024)(7.35×1022) / (3.84×108)2
Fg ≈ 1.98 × 1020 N
This is the entire point of Unit 0's scientific notation workshop paying off — every number here is absurdly large or small, and the exponent bookkeeping is exactly what that workshop trained. Walk the exponent arithmetic on the board: 24 + 22 − (−11) = 57 in the numerator, minus 16 in the denominator (2×8), landing at 1020ish before the leading digits refine it.

Quick Check

On your whiteboard

Reasoning, not calculating
  1. If you double the distance between two masses, what happens to Fg?
  2. Which pulls harder on you: the Earth or the Moon? Reason it out from mass and distance — don't reach for a calculator.
Answers: (1) Fg drops to 1/4 — inverse-square, not inverse-linear. This is the most common error on gravitation problems. (2) The Earth, by a landslide — its mass advantage over the Moon (roughly 81×) overwhelms any distance difference. This is order-of-magnitude reasoning straight out of the Unit 0 Fermi-problem instinct — no calculator needed, just comparing scales.

Setting Up Next Week

What if gravity is the centripetal force?

Every source tile from earlier this deck was a force supplying the inward pull for something moving in a circle. Gravity does that job for every moon, satellite, and planet in the universe — for free, with no string and no friction required.

Set Fg = Fc and solve for v — that's how orbits work. Next week: orbits.
