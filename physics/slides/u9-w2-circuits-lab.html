Act I

Series Circuits

One path. Every charge takes the same road.

Introduce "series" with a physical picture before the rules: a single loop of wire with resistors strung along it, like beads on a string. There is exactly one path from the battery's + terminal back to its − terminal — that single-path picture is what makes every series rule true.

Series · 1 of 2

Three rules for series circuits

I
Current is the same everywhere
One path, no branches — every charge that leaves the battery passes through every resistor
V
Voltage divides
Each resistor takes a share of the total voltage — the shares add up to the battery voltage
R
Resistance adds
Rtotal = R1 + R2 + … — more resistors in series always means more total resistance

One path in → current can't split. That single fact generates all three rules.

Push students to derive rule 2 and 3 from rule 1 rather than memorizing three separate facts. If current is forced to be identical everywhere (single path), then V = IR at each resistor with the same I means voltage HAS to scale with each resistor's share of the total R — the rules aren't independent, they're one geometric fact (single path) worked out three ways.

Series · 2 of 2

Worked example: three resistors in series

A 12 V battery drives three resistors in series: 2 Ω, 3 Ω, and 5 Ω. Find the total current.

StepWhat you write
KV = 12 V; R1 = 2 Ω, R2 = 3 Ω, R3 = 5 Ω (series)
Dtotal current I (target unit: A)
ERtotal = R1 + R2 + R3; then V = IRtotal
ARtotal = 10 Ω  →  I = 12 V / 10 Ω = 1.2 A

Each resistor's voltage drop: V1 ≈ 2.4 V, V2 ≈ 3.6 V, V3 ≈ 6.0 V — they sum to 12.0 V. That sum is a built-in check.

The individual voltage drops (using V = IR at each resistor, same I = 1.2 A throughout) are a second short KDEA pass, not shown in full table form — same move as the horizontal calculation in Unit 1's projectile problems. Have students verify the drops sum back to 12 V — that check is the whole point of teaching series circuits this way.

Act II

Parallel Circuits

Many paths. Every branch gets its own vote.

Physical picture: resistors wired between the same two junction points, each forming its own loop back to the battery. Every branch "sees" the full battery voltage independently — that's the single fact that generates all three parallel rules, mirroring how single-path did it for series.

Parallel · 1 of 2

Three rules for parallel circuits

V
Voltage is the same across every branch
Each branch connects the same two points — same push, no matter the branch's resistance
I
Current divides
Each branch draws its own current — the branch currents add up to the total current from the battery
R
Resistance combines by reciprocals
1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + … — more parallel branches always means LESS total resistance

Adding a parallel path gives current a new way through — it can only make the total resistance smaller, never bigger.

The "total R goes down" result surprises students every year — it feels backwards ("I added a resistor, shouldn't resistance go up?"). Tie it to the water analogy from last week: adding a parallel pipe gives water more ways through, so the combined resistance to flow drops. This is the single most-tested conceptual point on Thursday's review and the unit test.

Parallel · 2 of 2

Worked example: three resistors in parallel

A 12 V battery drives three resistors in parallel: 4 Ω, 6 Ω, and 12 Ω. Find the total current from the battery.

StepWhat you write
KV = 12 V; R1 = 4 Ω, R2 = 6 Ω, R3 = 12 Ω (parallel)
Dtotal current I (target unit: A)
E1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3; then V = IRtotal
A1/Rtotal = 1/2  →  Rtotal = 2 Ω  →  I = 12 V / 2 Ω = 6.0 A

Branch currents: I1 ≈ 3.0 A, I2 ≈ 2.0 A, I3 ≈ 1.0 A — they sum to 6.0 A. Same check, mirror image of series.

Note R_total (2 Ω) is smaller than the smallest individual branch (4 Ω) — call this out explicitly, it's the concrete proof of the "adding branches lowers resistance" rule from the previous slide. The branch-current check (sum equals total) is the parallel-circuit twin of the series voltage-drop check — same instinct, applied to the divided quantity instead of the shared one.

Quick Check · Turn & Talk · 4 minutes

Series or parallel — which stays the same?

With your partner

For each circuit type, name the ONE quantity that's identical across every resistor, and the ONE quantity that splits between them:

  • Series circuit: same ______, splits ______
  • Parallel circuit: same ______, splits ______
Answers: series — same current, voltage splits. Parallel — same voltage, current splits. This is the single fastest diagnostic for "did the student actually internalize the rules or just memorize two formulas" — a student who can answer this in 10 seconds is ready for combination circuits next.

Act III

Combination Circuits

Real circuits are rarely purely one or the other

Combination circuits are where the unit gets genuinely hard — most real devices (including whatever students build for the capstone) mix series and parallel sections. The strategy is always the same: find a purely parallel (or purely series) chunk, collapse it to one equivalent resistor, and repeat until the whole circuit is one resistor.

Combination · 1 of 3

The strategy: reduce, then solve

  1. Find a section that's purely series or purely parallel.
  2. Collapse it to a single equivalent resistance.
  3. Redraw the circuit with that one resistor in place of the section.
  4. Repeat until only one resistor is left — then apply Ohm's law to the whole circuit.
Shortcut for two resistors in parallel

Rparallel = (R1 × R2) / (R1 + R2) — "product over sum" — faster than the reciprocal formula when there are only two.

The "reduce, then solve" strategy is the E step of KDEA for combination circuits — it's a procedure, not a formula, and that's worth saying explicitly since it looks different from every other E students have written this year. The product-over-sum shortcut only works for exactly two resistors — flag that restriction or students will misapply it to three-branch parallel sections.

Combination · 2 of 3

Worked example: series-then-parallel

A 18 V battery drives R1 = 2 Ω in series with R2 = 6 Ω and R3 = 3 Ω, which are in parallel with each other. Find the total current.

StepWhat you write
KV = 18 V; R1 = 2 Ω (series); R2 = 6 Ω, R3 = 3 Ω (parallel with each other)
Dtotal current I (target unit: A)
Ecollapse parallel first: Rp = (R2R3)/(R2+R3); then Rtotal = R1 + Rp; then V = IRtotal
ARp = 18/9 = 2 Ω  →  Rtotal = 2 + 2 = 4 Ω  →  I = 18 V / 4 Ω = 4.5 A

Check: the voltage across the parallel section is I × Rp = 4.5 A × 2 Ω = 9 V, and across R1 is 4.5 A × 2 Ω = 9 V — they sum to 18 V.

Redraw the circuit on the board at each collapse step — students who only see the final table miss the "redraw" step from the strategy slide and try to skip straight to a formula. The verification sum (9 V + 9 V = 18 V) is the same series voltage-drop check from slide 5, applied one level up — point out explicitly that it's the identical check, just on a bigger circuit.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 10 minutes

Your turn: a combination circuit

With your partner

A 24 V battery drives R1 = 4 Ω in series with two equal 8 Ω resistors wired in parallel with each other. Find the total current from the battery.

Answer for the teacher: two equal 8 Ω resistors in parallel give Rp = 4 Ω (halves for equal resistors — worth pointing out as a pattern: n equal resistors R in parallel give R/n). R_total = 4 + 4 = 8 Ω. I = 24 V / 8 Ω = 3.0 A. Watch for students who forget to collapse the parallel section first and try to add 4 + 8 + 8 as if everything were in series.

The Lab

Measuring Real Circuits

Everything on paper today, now on a breadboard with a meter in your hand

Pivot from paper circuits to physical ones — same shift as the cart lab in Unit 1 (equations on the board become equations on the track). This is also the direct skill rehearsal for the capstone build two weeks from now, so name that connection out loud.

The Lab · 1 of 4

Three ways to use a multimeter

V
Voltmeter mode
Connect in parallel across the component — you're measuring a difference between two points, without breaking the circuit
A
Ammeter mode
Connect in series — you must break the circuit and route current through the meter itself
Ω
Ohmmeter mode
Measure resistance with the component removed from any powered circuit — never measure resistance on a live circuit

Wrong mode, wrong connection — both can blow a fuse in the meter or damage the component. Get this checked before you power anything on.

This is the single most important safety slide of the lab — the ammeter-in-series rule is the one students get backwards most often (they instinctively want to connect it like a voltmeter, in parallel, which can create a short circuit through the meter). Physically demonstrate correct probe placement on a sample circuit before releasing groups to their own boards.

The Lab · 2 of 4

Safety & setup

Ground rules
  • Batteries are low-voltage (1.5–9 V) — not dangerous, but still treat every circuit as live until you've checked it.
  • Build the circuit fully before connecting the battery. Never wire with power already flowing.
  • Have your teacher check your circuit diagram before you power it on the first time.
  • If a resistor or wire feels warm, disconnect the battery immediately — that's a sign of a wiring error, not something to push through.
Keep this brisk but non-negotiable — read the "warm component" rule aloud verbatim, it's the clearest physical warning sign of a short circuit at these voltages. The "build before powering" rule prevents the most common classroom mistake: students wiring live and getting a spark or a popped fuse in the meter.

The Lab · 3 of 4 · 30 minutes

Build, predict, measure

In your group
  1. Build the series circuit from slide 5 (2 Ω, 3 Ω, 5 Ω, 12 V battery) on your breadboard.
  2. Before powering on: write your predicted current and each predicted voltage drop.
  3. Power on. Measure the actual current (ammeter, in series) and each voltage drop (voltmeter, in parallel).
  4. Rebuild as the parallel circuit from slide 8. Repeat: predict, then measure.

Predicted vs. measured rarely match exactly — resistors have manufacturing tolerance (often ±5%). That's expected, not a mistake.

The predict-before-measure structure is deliberate — it's the hypothesis step from Lab Report I, revived without the full report. Circulate checking ammeter placement first (most common wiring error), then check that predicted and measured values are reasonably close (within the ±5% resistor tolerance, plus meter precision). A measured value wildly off from predicted almost always means a wiring mistake, not bad physics — help groups debug rather than accept the discrepancy.
