The Big Picture

Unit 4 Is One Story, Not Four Topics

Work → energy → conservation → power

Same framing move as Unit 1's review week — the biggest test-prep risk is students treating work, KE, PE, conservation, and power as five separate things to memorize instead of one chain where each idea builds on the last.

The Big Picture · 1 of 1

One chain, four links

1
Work
W = F d cosθ. A force acting through a displacement. Positive, negative, or zero. This is where the joule comes from.
2
Kinetic & potential energy
KE = ½mv2, PE = mgh. Two ways to store the same currency: motion, or height. Linked to work by Wnet = ΔKE.
3
Conservation
KEi + PEi = KEf + PEf, adjusted with +Wfriction when it isn't frictionless. Work and energy, combined into one law.
4
Power
P = W/t. How fast the work in step 1 gets done. The rate, not a new kind of energy.

There's no separate "conservation formula" to memorize — it's KE and PE from step 2, connected by the work from step 1.

Walk the arrows explicitly: work defines the joule (1), joules show up as KE and PE (2), those two get balanced against each other in the conservation law (3), and power just clocks how fast step 1 happens (4). If students can narrate this chain unprompted, they're ready for Thursday.

Common Test Traps · 1 of 4

Trap #1: Getting the sign of work wrong

The angle in W = F d cosθ is between the force and the displacement — not the force and the ground, not the force and "up."

The trap

Students see friction and assume it's automatically negative without checking direction, or forget that a force perpendicular to motion (like the normal force on flat ground) does exactly zero work — not "a little" work.

If total work on an object is positive, Wnet = ΔKE is positive too — the object speeds up. Check the sign before you trust the speed.

Re-drill the backpack example from Week 1 if time allows — it's the single best example for the perpendicular-force-does-zero-work case, and it's exactly the kind of scenario the test is likely to probe.

Common Test Traps · 2 of 4

Trap #2: Losing track of the reference height

PE only means something once you've picked h = 0. Two students can pick different reference points and both be correct — as long as they're consistent within their own solution.

The test-day check

State your h = 0 point explicitly in K, before you compute anything. Every h you use afterward measures from that same point — don't switch reference points mid-problem.

A number without a stated reference height is as incomplete as a number without a unit.

Common Test Traps · 3 of 4

Trap #3: Using the frictionless law when friction is given

If a problem mentions friction, rough surfaces, or gives you a coefficient of friction μ, the plain KEi + PEi = KEf + PEf is wrong — it's missing the Wfriction term.

The test-day check

Before writing E, ask: is this surface frictionless? If the problem doesn't say so explicitly or imply it (like "a frictionless track"), assume friction is present and include the term.

Leaving out Wfriction when it applies is the single most common Unit 4 test error.

Worth naming explicitly: unlike Unit 1 kinematics, where "no friction mentioned" usually meant frictionless, Unit 4 problems will often specify a rough surface or give μ specifically to test whether students remember to include W_friction. Flag the difference in defaults.

Common Test Traps · 4 of 4

Trap #4: Mixing up energy and power

Joules (J) measure how much work or energy. Watts (W) measure how fast. They are related by time, not interchangeable.

Wrong

"The power was 2058 joules."

Power is never measured in joules — that's an energy unit.

Right

W = 2058 J, done in 4.0 s → P = 510 W

Energy in joules, divided by time in seconds, gives power in watts.

If your D row asks for power and your A row answer has units of J, stop — you solved for the wrong quantity.

The Test · 2 of 2

What KDEA looks like on test day

Your answer sheet should look like this
StepWhat the grader sees
KEvery given, with units. Include your reference height if PE is involved.
DThe target quantity and its unit — "Find P, in watts," not "solve for P."
EThe equation, symbols only. If friction applies, the +Wfriction term is written in before any numbers appear.
ASymbols first, then numbers with units, then arithmetic, then the answer — circled, with its unit.

An organized KDEA table with a small arithmetic slip scores higher than a correct number with no work shown.

Practice · 1 of 2

A sled down a frictionless hill

A 4.0 kg sled starts from rest at the top of a frictionless hill 6.0 m high. Find its kinetic energy and its speed at the bottom.

StepWhat you write
Km = 4.0 kg, h = 6.0 m, vi = 0 m/s
DKEf (target: J) and vf (target: m/s)
EKEi + PEi = KEf + PEf  →  KEf = mgh, then vf = √(2KEf/m)
AKEf = (4.0)(9.8)(6.0) = 235.2 J ≈ 235 J
vf = √(2 · 235.2 / 4.0) = √117.6 ≈ 10.8 m/s

Two questions, one KDEA table — the second answer builds directly on the first.

Practice · 2 of 2

An elevator motor

A motor lifts a 250 kg elevator car 12 m in 20 s, at constant speed. Find the motor's power output.

StepWhat you write
Km = 250 kg, h = 12 m, t = 20 s
Dpower P (target unit: W)
EW = mgh (constant speed → ΔKE = 0, all work becomes PE), then P = W/t
AW = (250)(9.8)(12) = 29,400 J
P = 29,400 J / 20 s = 1,470 W ≈ 1.5 × 103 W

Constant speed means ΔKE = 0 — that's what let the whole calculation collapse into ΔPE alone.

The "constant speed → ΔKE = 0" reasoning step is worth walking out loud — it's the same move as Unit 1's "flat v-t graph ≠ stopped" idea, applied here to justify why only PE change matters. Scientific notation callback (1.5 × 10³ W) ties back to Workshop I.

Confidence Check · Turn & Talk · 10 minutes

One last problem before Thursday

With your partner

A 1,200 kg roller coaster car starts from rest at the top of the first hill, 25 m high. Friction and air resistance do a combined −62,000 J of work by the time it reaches the bottom.

  1. What would its speed be at the bottom with no friction at all?
  2. What is its actual speed at the bottom, accounting for friction?

This is the exact shape of a test conservation problem — compare both answers when you're done.

Answers: (1) frictionless: v = √(2×9.8×25) = √490 ≈ 22.1 m/s. (2) with friction: KE_f = mgh + W_friction = (1200)(9.8)(25) − 62,000 = 294,000 − 62,000 = 232,000 J → v_f = √(2×232,000/1200) = √386.7 ≈ 19.7 m/s. The comparison (22.1 vs 19.7 m/s) makes the cost of friction concrete on a dramatic, motivating example. Have pairs report both numbers before revealing the intended answer.
