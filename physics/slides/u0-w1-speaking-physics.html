Why this matters

Physics is a language before it's a subject

Every unit this year will hand you an equation, a graph, and a set of measurements. Before any of that means anything, you need to be fluent in four things:

  • Units — what a number is measuring
  • Symbols — the shorthand equations are written in
  • Notation — how to write very big or very small numbers
  • Estimation — sanity-checking whether an answer is even reasonable

Today: the toolkit. Not yet: the equations.

Set expectations: no formulas being "solved" today. This is entirely about being able to read and write physics correctly, so that Workshops II and III (and Unit 1 Kinematics) don't stall out on notation.

SI Units · 4 of 4

Quick check

On your whiteboard
  1. Convert 2.5 km to meters.
  2. Convert 450 ms to seconds.
  3. A car's engine is rated at 150 kW. How many watts is that?
Cold-call after 90 seconds. Answers: 2500 m, 0.45 s, 150000 W. Watch for students who move the decimal the wrong direction on the milli- conversion — very common first-week error.

Measurement · 1 of 2

Every measurement is a guess (a careful one)

No instrument is perfect. The last digit you write down is always an estimate between the smallest marks on the tool.

A ruler marked in mm lets you estimate to the nearest 0.1 mm. A stopwatch you click by hand is honestly good to about ±0.2 s, no matter how many decimal places the display shows.

Precision is a property of the tool, not the number.

Measurement · 2 of 2

Significant figures, fast

Count as significant:

  • All nonzero digits
  • Zeros between nonzero digits
  • Trailing zeros after a decimal point

Don't count:

  • Leading zeros (0.0034 → 2 sig figs)
  • Trailing zeros with no decimal (200 → ambiguous, usually 1–2)

Rule of thumb for this course: your answer can't be more precise than your least-precise measurement. We'll drill this hard once lab reports start in Unit 1.

Don't over-teach the full addition/multiplication sig-fig rules today — that gets revisited when lab report I happens in Unit 1. Today's goal is just recognition: can they count sig figs in a given number.

Greek Symbols · 1 of 3

Physics writes in Greek. Here's why.

English letters ran out fast. Physicists reuse the Greek alphabet as shorthand for specific ideas — and the same symbol almost always means the same thing, equation after equation.

Δ
delta · capital
"the change in" — Δx, Δv, Δt (Unit 1, everywhere)
Σ
sigma · capital
"the sum of" — ΣF, net force (Unit 2)
Emphasize: Delta doesn't mean "a number," it means an operation — final minus initial. Students who treat Δx as a single mystery variable struggle badly once Kinematics starts in three weeks.

Scientific Notation · 1 of 3

3 × 108
m/s
speed of light

kg
mass of an electron

Writing 0.00000000000000000000000000000091 kg on a test is how careless errors happen. Scientific notation exists so we stop losing zeros.

Scientific Notation · 2 of 3

The format: a × 10n

  • a is always between 1 and 10
  • Moving the decimal left to shrink a big number → exponent goes up
  • Moving the decimal right to grow a small number → exponent goes down (more negative)
Worked example

45,000 m → decimal moves 4 places left → 4.5 × 104 m

0.0067 s → decimal moves 3 places right → 6.7 × 10−3 s

Scientific Notation · 3 of 3

Quick check

On your whiteboard
  1. Write 812,000,000 m in scientific notation.
  2. Write 0.000045 kg in scientific notation.
  3. Which is bigger: 2 × 105 or 9 × 104?
Answers: 8.12 × 10⁸ m, 4.5 × 10⁻⁵ kg, 2×10⁵ is bigger (200,000 > 90,000). Question 3 catches students who compare the leading digit instead of the exponent first — worth a follow-up "why" if it comes up.

Estimation · 1 of 2

When you can't measure it, estimate it

A Fermi problem is a question that looks impossible to answer exactly — so instead of looking up the real number, you break it into smaller pieces you can reasonably guess, and multiply them together.

Physicists use this constantly: is an answer even in the right ballpark before trusting the calculator? We'll use this same instinct all year to sanity-check lab results.

Order of magnitude first. Precision later.

Estimation · 2 of 2

Pair work: the Miami Fermi problem

With your partner — 8 minutes

Estimate: how many cafecitos (Cuban coffees) are served in Miami on an average day?

Break it into pieces you can guess — you will not look any of this up:

  • Roughly how many people live in the greater Miami area?
  • What fraction of them drink a cafecito on a given day?
  • How many cafecitos does a typical drinker have?

Multiply your guesses. Report your order of magnitude (thousands? hundreds of thousands? millions?) — not a "precise" answer. There is no single correct number.

This is the flagship activity of the workshop — budget real time for it. Circulate and push groups to justify each guess out loud rather than pulling a number from nowhere. When reporting out, compare orders of magnitude across groups rather than exact totals; the point is the method, not the digit.

Recap

Your toolkit so far

  • SI units — base units, derived units, and prefixes from giga to pico
  • Measurement — every reading has built-in uncertainty; sig figs track it
  • Greek symbols — Δ, Σ, θ, ω, λ, ρ each mean something specific
  • Scientific notation — a × 10n, keeps huge and tiny numbers honest
  • Estimation — Fermi problems: break it down, guess each piece, multiply
