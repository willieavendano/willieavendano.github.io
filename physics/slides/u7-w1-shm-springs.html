The Big Idea

Pull it. Let it go. It comes back.

Simple harmonic motion is a repeating motion driven toward equilibrium.

Start with the physical experience before naming equations. A stretched spring and a displaced pendulum both have an equilibrium position and a restoring tendency. Do not overpromise that every oscillator is perfectly simple harmonic; the models work best for small displacements and idealized conditions.

SHM · 1 of 3

Three words organize every oscillator.

x
displacement · m
Distance and direction from equilibrium. The sign tells which side of equilibrium.
Fr
restoring force · N
A force that points back toward equilibrium. Its direction opposes the displacement.
T
period · s
Time for one complete repeat: out, back, and to the same state again.

Equilibrium is the reference point: x = 0 m.

Students often use period to mean "how long it moves." Correct that now: period is one complete cycle, measured in seconds. Keep x as displacement from equilibrium rather than total stretch from an arbitrary table edge. The restoring force points opposite x, which becomes the minus sign in Hooke's law.

SHM · 2 of 3

Hooke's law: the farther you pull, the harder it pulls back.

Model

Fr = −kx

  • Fr is restoring force in N
  • k is spring constant in N/m — stiffness
  • x is displacement from equilibrium in m
  • The minus sign means the force points opposite the displacement.

Linear spring behavior: double x in m → double |Fr| in N.

The minus sign is directional, not a claim that force has negative size. Pick a positive direction on the board and show that x = +0.10 m produces a force toward the negative side. Students commonly say "negative force means weaker"; keep returning to direction versus magnitude.

SHM · 3 of 3

The spring constant is a material property in the model.

large k
stiff spring · N/m
A given displacement produces a larger restoring-force magnitude. The spring resists stretch more strongly.
small k
soft spring · N/m
The same displacement produces a smaller restoring-force magnitude. The spring is easier to stretch.
Fr → 0
near equilibrium · N
At x = 0 m, the ideal spring's restoring force is 0 N.
limit
model boundary
Hooke's law is a model; real springs stop behaving linearly if stretched too far.
This is a good place to separate k from mass. Students often expect every quantity in a spring problem to affect every outcome. k belongs to the spring; mass belongs to the oscillator. The period formula later combines them, but the force law itself does not contain mass.

Worked Example · KDEA

k = 40 N/m, x = +0.15 m → Fr = ?

A spring has k = 40 N/m and displacement x = +0.15 m from equilibrium. Find the restoring force in N.

StepWhat you write
Kk = 40 N/m, x = +0.15 m
Drestoring force Fr, target unit N
EFr = −kx
AFr = −(40 N/m)(+0.15 m) = −6.0 N

Magnitude = 6.0 N; the negative sign says the force points opposite +x.

Emphasize unit cancellation: (N/m)(m) leaves N. The answer is not "negative six newtons" as a weaker force; it is a 6.0 N force in the negative direction under this sign convention. This is a compact KDEA model students can reuse when they write their own analysis later.

Timing the Motion

Period & Frequency

How long does one cycle take — and how often does it repeat?

Period & Frequency · 1 of 3

Period and frequency are reciprocals.

T
period · s/cycle
Time for one complete cycle. A longer T means slower repetition.
f
frequency · Hz
Cycles per second. One hertz equals one cycle per second: 1 Hz = 1/s.
f = 1/T
definition · Hz
If T is in s/cycle, the reciprocal is cycles/s, which we call Hz.
T = 1/f
same relationship · s
A high frequency means a short period; a low frequency means a long period.
Have students say both units aloud. Hz is not a mysterious new unit; it is 1/s, cycles per second. The most common failure is swapping the relationship and claiming f = T. Use a 2-second cycle as a quick anchor: two seconds per cycle means one-half cycle per second, or 0.50 Hz.

Period & Frequency · 2 of 3

Mass on a spring: mass and stiffness set the clock.

Ideal mass-spring oscillator

\( T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}} \)

  • T is period in s
  • m is oscillating mass in kg
  • k is spring constant in N/m
Changes T

More mass → longer T. Stiffer spring (larger k) → shorter T.

Ideal model says no effect

Amplitude does not change T for an ideal linear oscillator.

Do not let the square root turn into a proportionality misconception. Doubling m does not double T; it multiplies T by the square root of 2. Likewise, amplitude independence is an ideal-model claim and is worth testing as a contrast with real-world damping and nonlinearity.

Period & Frequency · 3 of 3

Simple pendulum: length and gravity set the clock.

Small-angle pendulum model

\( T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{g}} \)

  • T is period in s
  • L is distance from pivot to center of mass in m
  • g is gravitational field strength in m/s2
longer L
longer period · s
A longer pendulum swings more slowly because T grows with \( \sqrt{L} \).
mass? no
ideal pendulum
Bob mass does not affect T in this model; neither does amplitude for small angles.
The phrase "small angle" is a boundary condition, not decoration. Keep the investigation in a range where the small-angle model is reasonable, unless a student deliberately chooses amplitude as the independent variable. Length means pivot to the bob's center of mass, not just string length to the top of the bob.

Investigation design warning

To test one relationship, change one independent variable and keep the other plausible causes controlled.

A formula is also a design map: it tells you what to vary and what to hold constant.

This slide bridges content to experimental design. Ask students to point to the variable they could manipulate with the available pendulum materials. The formula alone does not make an investigation fair; controls and repeated trials do.

Worked Example · Setup

Predict a pendulum period.

An ideal pendulum has length L = 0.81 m on Earth, where g = 9.8 m/s2. Predict its period.

Plan
  1. Target: period T in s.
  2. Use the pendulum model, not the spring model.
  3. Check units under the square root before evaluating.

This is a model prediction your data can challenge.

Worked Example · KDEA

L = 0.81 m, g = 9.8 m/s2 → T = ?

StepWhat you write
KL = 0.81 m, g = 9.8 m/s2
Dperiod T, target unit s
E\( T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g} \)
AT = 2π√[(0.81 m)/(9.8 m/s2)]
T = 2π√(0.0827 s2) ≈ 1.80 s

Inside the square root: m ÷ (m/s2) = s2, so T lands in s.

Slow down at the units inside the radical. This is where students often see a formula and abandon unit reasoning. The square root of s² is s, so the dimensional check works. Keep the result as a prediction, not as an answer key for the later investigation.

Design Phase

Ask a question you can actually test.

Variables. Hypothesis. Controls. Data plan.

This is the pivot from direct instruction to independent lab design. Students should leave this act with a testable question and a plan, not a copied procedure. The teacher's role is to challenge fairness and measurability without supplying the final design.

Design · 1 of 4

Start with a question that names the variables.

IV
independent variable
What you deliberately change: for example, pendulum length L in m.
DV
dependent variable
What you measure in response: period T in s or frequency f in Hz.
C
controls
What stays the same: bob, pivot, release method, angle range, timing method, location.
Strong question frame

How does changing ___ affect the period T in s of a pendulum while ___ and ___ are held constant?

Require the question to make the direction of causality visible: IV affects DV. "What is the period of a pendulum?" is a measurement question, not yet an investigation. The frame forces students to name what changes and what remains controlled.

Design · 2 of 4

Write a hypothesis that makes a prediction.

If / then / because

If I change the IV, then the DV will change in a specific direction or pattern, because the model says…

good
testable prediction
If L increases in m, then T increases in s because \( T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g} \).
weak
not yet testable
Longer pendulums are better. Better how? Name the measurable quantity and expected pattern.

The formula is evidence for the prediction, not a substitute for collecting data.

Students may copy the formula without predicting a direction. Push for an observable claim: increase, decrease, proportionality, or no meaningful change. A null hypothesis is also legitimate if a student chooses a variable the model says should not affect period, such as bob mass in the ideal model.

Design · 3 of 4

A fair test changes one cause at a time.

Control checklist
  • same bob mass in kg
  • same release angle in °
  • same pivot and string
  • same timing method
Procedure checklist
  • measure IV with a named instrument
  • release without an extra push
  • time many cycles, not just one
  • repeat each IV level

A procedure is reproducible when another group could follow it without asking you what you meant.

The "time many cycles" move is central. If students time one cycle, reaction time becomes a large fraction of the measurement. Let them choose a cycle count, but require them to record total time and calculate T = total time / number of cycles with units attached.

Design · 4 of 4

Plan the data table before you touch the apparatus.

record what happened
Length, cycle count, total time, trial number, and any unusual event. Raw data keeps the investigation auditable.

calculate what it means
T = total time / cycles, with T in s. Then compare trials and levels.
Insist that the table distinguishes raw from processed data. Students should not erase an odd trial; they should mark what happened and decide later whether there is a defensible reason to exclude it. A complete plan also names how the data will be graphed, even if the graph is made after collection.

Design Studio · 20 minutes

Draft your investigation proposal.

Submit before you leave
  1. One question with an IV, DV, and at least two controls
  2. One if / then / because hypothesis
  3. A numbered, reproducible procedure
  4. A blank raw-data and processed-data table
  5. A prediction of the graph shape or relationship

Your design must be approved before data collection begins next week.

Use this as the Thursday work block. Conference with students in order: variable clarity, control quality, measurement feasibility, then graph/data plan. If a design cannot be completed with the available apparatus, redirect the question now; do not let students discover that after the investigation window opens.
