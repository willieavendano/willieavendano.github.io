Why This Matters

You built a model in your head. Today you test it against a real track.

Last week's conservation law — KEi + PEi = KEf + PEf — was pure algebra. Today it has to survive contact with an actual cart, an actual track, and actual friction.

You are not proving the law. You are testing whether it holds up — and accounting for what doesn't.

Direct echo of the Cart Lab's framing back in Unit 1 ("you are not proving the equations, you are testing whether a model fits real data"). Repetition of that exact sentence structure is deliberate — it's the same scientific stance, applied to a new model.

Day 1 · Tuesday 1/19

The Investigation

Does total mechanical energy stay constant on the track?

First of two block days. This divider opens the run day. Keep the room moving — data collection needs real time, and Thursday's draft day depends on having clean, complete data in hand by the end of today.

The Investigation · 1 of 3

The question you're answering today

A cart is released from rest at some height on a track and rolls down toward a level section. Does its total mechanical energy (KE + PE) stay the same from release to the level section?

What you measure
  • Release height (h, in m) — sets the starting PE
  • Speed at the bottom (v, in m/s) — gives the final KE
  • Cart mass (m, in kg) — measured once, on a scale

If Ei ≈ Ef, the frictionless model holds. If Ef is smaller, friction is real and measurable.

Frame this as a genuinely open question — a real track has some friction, so a small energy loss is the expected, correct outcome, not a failed experiment. The lab succeeds either way: it either confirms near-perfect conservation or it lets students measure how much friction removed. Both are good science.

The Investigation · 2 of 3

What's available at your station

Materials
  • Track with adjustable release points (or a ramp + level run-out section)
  • Cart, mass scale, meter stick
  • Timer or photogate for measuring speed at the bottom

Same tools, same categories as the Unit 1 cart lab. What's different this time is what you're asked to do with them.

Nothing on this materials list is new equipment — the point is that the hardware is familiar, only the procedure design responsibility has changed. Reassure anxious students: you already know how to use every item here.

The Investigation · 3 of 3

This time, you write the procedure

Reports I and II handed you the steps. Report III hands you the question and the equipment — the procedure is yours to design.

Decisions only you make now
  • How many release heights will you test?
  • How many trials per height, and how will you average them?
  • How will you measure speed at the bottom — and how will you know it's accurate?
Non-negotiables, no matter your design
  • Multiple trials per data point (you know why — Report I)
  • Units in every header, consistent sig figs (you know why — Report I)
  • A written procedure detailed enough for someone else to repeat it
The two-column structure is the whole pedagogical point: independence doesn't mean "no rules," it means "the rules you already internalized now apply without a checklist walking you through them." Push groups to commit to at least three release heights — one data point can't show a trend.

Activity · 45 minutes

Run your investigation

In your group

Design and run your procedure. Collect height, speed, and mass data at at least three different release heights, multiple trials each. Calculate KE, PE, and total E for the release point and the level section at every height before you leave.

Leaving today with an incomplete data table means Thursday's draft day starts behind. Budget your time.

Circulate aggressively today — this is the one day the data gets collected, and a bad data set can't be fixed on Thursday. Check specifically that groups are computing E_i and E_f live, not just recording raw numbers to crunch later; catching a broken photogate or an impossible KE > PE_i result today saves the report on Thursday.

Lab Report III · 3 of 4

KDEA still runs the whole investigation

StepIn this lab
KYour measured heights, speeds, and mass — whatever your own procedure collected
DYour question: does Ei ≈ Ef at every height you tested?
EKE = ½mv2, PE = mgh, KEi + PEi = KEf + PEf — last week's law, now the model under test
ACompute Ei and Ef at each height, compare them, and explain any gap using friction

Same four letters that opened the year in Workshop II. The equipment changed. KDEA didn't.

Direct structural echo of the Cart Lab's "KDEA in the Lab" slide from Unit 1 — deliberately reuse that framing so students recognize the pattern rather than treating it as brand new. This table is also a legitimate template for the Analysis section of their actual report.

Activity · Draft & Peer Review · 50 minutes

Write your draft, then trade

In your group, then with another group
  1. Draft all seven sections using your Tuesday data. Prioritize Data, Analysis, and Conclusion — same priority as Report I.
  2. Swap drafts with another group. Check their data table for units and consistent sig figs, their graph for labeled axes and a best-fit line, and their Conclusion for a real connection back to the hypothesis.
  3. Return the draft with at least two specific, written notes.
Circulate during the peer-review swap specifically — this is where "fully independent" gets tested in real time. Listen for groups giving each other vague feedback ("looks good") versus specific, rubric-anchored feedback ("your y-axis has no unit"). Model the second kind out loud if the first kind is common.

What a strong Report III needs

✓

Numbered steps, specific enough to repeat — written by you, checked by your peer reviewer.
✓

Units in headers, consistent sig figs, height/speed/mass columns, and computed KE, PE, and E columns.
✓
A graph you chose and justified
A one-sentence reason for your choice of graph, plus labeled axes and a best-fit line.
✓
A Conclusion that names your own uncertainty
Specific to your setup — not a generic list. Did Ei ≈ Ef? What accounts for any gap?
This is the rubric-in-tile-form students should screenshot, same pattern as Report I's expectations slide. No numeric answer key here on purpose — every group's E_i and E_f will differ based on their own measured heights and speeds, so there is no single "correct" number to hand out.
