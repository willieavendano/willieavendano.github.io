Warm-Up · Setup

A rear-end collision

A 1500 kg car moving at 15.0 m/s rear-ends a stationary 1000 kg car. Their bumpers lock on impact. Find their common velocity immediately after the collision.

Locked bumpers means one shared final velocity — perfectly inelastic.

Warm-Up · KDEA

m1 = 1500 kg, v1i = 15.0 m/s, m2 = 1000 kg, v2i = 0

StepWhat you write
Km1 = 1500 kg, v1i = 15.0 m/s, m2 = 1000 kg, v2i = 0 m/s
Dcommon velocity vf (target unit: m/s)
Em1v1i + m2v2i = (m1 + m2)vf
Asymbols first: vf = m1v1i / (m1 + m2)
vf = (1500)(15.0) / 2500 = 9.0 m/s

Both cars move off together at 9.0 m/s — the same protocol as every worked example since Week 2.

22,500/2500 = 9.0 exactly, so this answer honestly earns = — point that out, and flag that most test problems won't divide so cleanly; when the result is rounded, students write ≈, the same honesty rule applied all year.

Momentum in the Real World

Four places conservation of momentum quietly runs the show

This divider opens the applications section — the part of the week meant to answer "why does this matter beyond the test." Each tile on the next slide is a genuine engineering or physics application, not a toy example.

Applications · 1 of 1

Four real uses of momentum conservation

Rocket propulsion
A rocket has no ground to push against — it expels exhaust mass backward, and conservation of momentum pushes the rocket forward. No air needed, which is why rockets work in the vacuum of space.
Ballistic pendulum
A classic way to measure a bullet's speed: fire it into a hanging block, measure how high the block swings, and work backward through momentum (collision) and energy (swing) conservation.
Vehicle safety engineering
Airbags and crumple zones (Week 1) stretch the collision time to shrink the force — the same J = FΔt logic, engineered on purpose to protect passengers.
Contact sports
A tackle, a check, a collision on the field — momentum is conserved for the players-as-a-system the instant they collide, even though each player's individual momentum changes drastically.

Same law, four completely different settings — that's what makes it a conservation law and not a special-case trick.

Rocket propulsion is worth a beat of extra explanation since it's the least intuitive: students often assume rockets "push against the air," which is backward — rockets work BETTER in vacuum because there's no air resistance fighting the thrust. The ballistic pendulum tile is the direct setup for the next slide's worked example, so don't over-explain it here.

Worked Example · Two Laws, One Problem

The ballistic pendulum: momentum, then energy

A 0.010 kg bullet embeds in a stationary 2.00 kg wooden block hanging from strings. The block+bullet swing up to a height of 0.12 m. Find the bullet's speed just before impact.

StageWhat you write
Stage 1
Collision (momentum)		 K: m = 0.010 kg, M = 2.00 kg   D: v1 right after impact
E: mv0 = (m + M)v1 — perfectly inelastic
Stage 2
Swing (energy, Unit 4)		 K: h = 0.12 m, g = 9.8 m/s2   D: v1
E: \( \tfrac{1}{2}(m+M)v_1^2 = (m+M)gh \) → \( v_1 = \sqrt{2gh} \)

v1 = √(2 · 9.8 · 0.12) ≈ 1.53 m/s  →  v0 = (m+M)v1 / m = (2.010)(1.53) / 0.010 ≈ 3.1 × 102 m/s

Momentum solves the collision. Energy solves the swing. Two unit's worth of conservation laws, one apparatus.

This is the deck's deliberate Unit 4 tie-in, verified against the Course Outline: Unit 4 (Energy) covers "conservation of energy," which is exactly what powers Stage 2 here. Walk the logic explicitly: you CANNOT use energy conservation across the collision itself (Stage 1) because kinetic energy is lost when the bullet embeds — that's a perfectly inelastic collision. You CAN use energy conservation for the swing (Stage 2) because no further energy is lost once the bullet is embedded and the pendulum just swings. Mixing these two stages up is the classic ballistic-pendulum error.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 12 minutes

A head-on grab in hockey

An 80 kg player moving 6.0 m/s east collides with a 100 kg player moving 4.0 m/s west. They grab on and move together. Find their velocity right after the collision — magnitude and direction.

With your partner
  1. Pick a positive direction and assign signs to both velocities.
  2. Set up conservation of momentum for the system.
  3. Solve for the shared final velocity. Which direction do they end up moving?

Two objects moving toward each other — the signs are opposite. Don't lose that in the algebra.

Answer preview: with east positive, v_A = +6.0 m/s, v_B = −4.0 m/s. p_before = 80(6.0) + 100(−4.0) = 480 − 400 = 80 kg·m/s. v_f = 80 / 180 ≈ 0.44 m/s east. The surprising result — they end up drifting slowly EAST despite player B being heavier — is because player A's momentum magnitude (480) still edges out player B's (400). This is the sign-error trap from the next section, live in a practice problem before students see it named explicitly.

Practice · KDEA

vA = +6.0 m/s, vB = −4.0 m/s → vf = ?

StepWhat you write
KmA = 80 kg, vA = +6.0 m/s, mB = 100 kg, vB = −4.0 m/s
Dvf, shared final velocity (target unit: m/s, plus direction)
EmAvA + mBvB = (mA + mB)vf
Apbefore = 80(6.0) + 100(−4.0) = 480 − 400 = 80 kg·m/s
vf = 80 / 180 ≈ 0.44 m/s

Positive result → the pair drifts east at ≈ 0.44 m/s, even though player B is heavier — momentum, not mass alone, decides the outcome.

The Big Picture · 1 of 1

One idea, three applications

1
Momentum & impulse
p = mv, J = FΔt, and the theorem J = Δp linking them. This is where the variables come from.
2
Conservation of momentum
Newton's third law guarantees ptotal stays constant in an isolated system. This is the tool for any two-object problem.
3
Collision types
Elastic, inelastic, perfectly inelastic — sorted by what happens to kinetic energy. This is how you classify what you found.
4
Real-world applications
Rockets, ballistic pendulums, safety engineering — the same conservation law, wherever objects push, collide, or separate. This is where it all shows up.

There's one law underneath the whole unit: total momentum of an isolated system doesn't change. Everything else is applying it.

Point explicitly: tile 2 (conservation) is the engine that runs every problem in tiles 3 and 4 — collision classification and real-world applications are both just "conservation of momentum, applied to a specific situation." The chip is the single sentence students should be able to recite before Thursday.

Common Test Traps · 1 of 2

Trap #1: Momentum is a vector — the sign is not optional

Every velocity in a momentum problem needs a sign, tied to one consistent positive direction you pick before you start.

The trap

Two objects moving toward each other have velocities with opposite signs — drop the negative sign on one of them, and the total momentum comes out wrong, even if every number and every step of algebra was otherwise correct.

This is exactly what happened in the hockey-collision practice problem: reversing the sign on player B's velocity would have given a completely different (and wrong) final direction.

Tie this directly back to slide 10's practice problem — the concrete example makes the abstract warning stick. Also worth mentioning: an object AT REST still needs to be written with v = 0 in the K row, not omitted — skipping it is a common way students lose track of a term in the sum.

Common Test Traps · 2 of 2

Trap #2: Don't assume kinetic energy is conserved

Momentum conservation applies to every isolated collision, no exceptions. Kinetic energy conservation applies to elastic collisions only.

Always safe to write

m1v1i + m2v2i = m1v1f + m2v2f

Only if told it's elastic

½m1v1i2 + ½m2v2i2 = ½m1v1f2 + ½m2v2f2

If a problem says the objects stick together, that's your signal it's perfectly inelastic — kinetic energy is NOT conserved, guaranteed.

This is the single highest-value slide for test performance — restating the Week 22 comparison one more time, this close to the test, because it's also the single most common error the teacher sees on the actual Unit 5 Test each year. "Objects stick together" as the tell for perfectly inelastic is worth repeating exactly this way.

The Test · 2 of 2

What KDEA looks like on test day

Your answer sheet should look like this
StepWhat the grader sees
KEvery mass and velocity, with units and signs. "vi = −4.0 m/s." Not "vi = 4."
DThe target quantity, its unit, and (for momentum) its direction. "Find vf, in m/s, and which way it points."
EConservation of momentum, written in symbols — and a note on whether KE conservation also applies.
ASymbols first: rearranged equation. Then numbers with units and signs. Then the arithmetic. Finally the answer, circled, with unit and direction.

The grader is looking for the protocol, not just the answer. An organized KDEA table with a small algebra error scores higher than a correct number with no work shown.

Confidence Check · Turn & Talk · 10 minutes

One last problem before Thursday: who wins the bump?

With your partner

Bumper car A (200 kg with rider) moves at 3.0 m/s east and hits a stationary bumper car B (250 kg). After the collision, car A moves at 0.5 m/s east and car B moves at 2.0 m/s east.

  1. Check: is momentum conserved?
  2. Check: is kinetic energy conserved? What collision type is this?
  3. Judgment: which car ends up moving faster — who "won" the bump?

This is the exact shape of a test classification problem, with one extra step: after the math, you make a call.

Answers: p_before = 200(3.0) + 250(0) = 600 kg·m/s. p_after = 200(0.5) + 250(2.0) = 100 + 500 = 600 kg·m/s — momentum conserved exactly. KE_before = ½(200)(3.0)² = 900 J. KE_after = ½(200)(0.5)² + ½(250)(2.0)² = 25 + 500 = 525 J — KE dropped by 375 J (≈42%), so this is inelastic (not perfectly inelastic, since the cars separate rather than sticking). Car B ends up faster (2.0 m/s vs. 0.5 m/s) despite starting at rest — "winning" the bump. Same judgment-step closer as the Unit 1 review deck's tennis-net problem: physics answers a real question, not just a number.

Unit 5, in four sentences
  • Momentum & impulse — p = mv; J = FΔt; the theorem J = Δp ties them together.
  • Conservation of momentum — ptotal, before = ptotal, after for any isolated system, guaranteed by Newton's third law.
