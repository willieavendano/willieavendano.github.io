Part 1

Free Fall

When gravity is the only force acting

Start with the simplest case: straight up and straight down. Free fall means the object is ONLY under gravity — no air resistance, no engines, no hands still touching it. This is the cleanest uniform-acceleration problem there is, because the acceleration never changes and you already know its value.

Free Fall · 1 of 4

Near Earth's surface, everything falls at g ≈ 9.8 m/s2 downward

Drop a textbook and a pencil from the same height. In a vacuum, they hit the ground at the exact same instant — mass doesn't matter. Air resistance is the only reason you ever see a difference.

The feather and the hammer

Apollo 15 commander David Scott dropped a feather and a hammer on the Moon — no air, no difference. They fell at the same rate and hit the lunar surface together. The video is one minute long and it's worth watching.

g ≈ 9.8 m/s2 downward. On Earth, that number is the same for every object, every time.

The ≈ is honest — 9.8 is a rounded value, close enough for every problem in this unit. The feather-and-hammer story lands hard with this age group; naming Apollo 15 specifically gives it weight (pun intended). Let a student look it up and report back next class — it's genuinely one of the coolest physics demos ever filmed, and it takes 60 seconds.

Free Fall · 2 of 4

Free fall is uniform acceleration. a = g. That's it.

The four kinematic equations from Week 2 still work — exactly as written. The only difference is that the acceleration already has a name and a value:

vf = vi + gt
no Δy
Velocity after t seconds of free fall. Same as Week 2, with a = g.
Δy = vit + ½gt2
no vf
Vertical displacement under gravity. Use when you know vi, g, t and need how far it fell (or rose).
vf2 = vi2 + 2gΔy
no t
The “no-time-given” free-fall equation. Use when time is the missing variable.
Δy = ½(vi + vf)t
no g
Displacement from average velocity. Works when g's actual value doesn't appear in the problem.

The equations didn't change. x became y, a became g. Same selection rule: find the missing variable, pick its equation.

This slide is the bridge from Week 2 to free fall. Every student should see that nothing is new mathematically — the same four-equation table they already have, with a replaced by g and Δx by Δy. Walk one equation aloud: "vf = vi + at, but a is now −9.8 m/s², so vf = vi + (−9.8)t." The sign-of-g conversation is next.

Free Fall · 3 of 4

Pick a direction. g's sign follows.

Gravity always pulls toward the ground — 9.8 m/s2 downward. Whether g is +9.8 or −9.8 is entirely your choice of which direction you call positive.

Up = positive (recommended)

g = −9.8 m/s2. An object thrown upward has positive vi, negative a. At the peak, v = 0. On the way down, v is negative.

Down = positive

g = +9.8 m/s2. Everything falling gets positive Δy and positive v. But a ball thrown upward starts with negative vi — the sign moved to the initial condition.

Same physics. Same answer at the end. The sign of g is not a fact about nature — it's a choice you make before you start writing equations. The only rule: pick one and stick with it the whole problem.

The two-callout layout is deliberate: it shows both conventions are valid side by side, which prevents the "which one is right?" paralysis. I recommend "up = positive" because it keeps the sign convention consistent with Week 1's positive-right convention on the horizontal axis. The object thrown upward is the canonical test case — positive vi, negative a, v = 0 at the peak is a clean story.

Free Fall · 4 of 4

Same sign table, now with gravity

If you pick up = positive, then g = −9.8 m/s2 for the entire flight — going up, at the peak, and coming back down:

Phasev signa signWhat's happening
Going up+−v and a have opposite signs → slowing down
At the peak0−Velocity is zero for an instant. Acceleration is still g — it never stops.
Coming down−−v and a have the same sign → speeding up (in the negative direction)

The acceleration is −9.8 m/s2 the entire time. It does not change sign at the peak.

Row 2 is the trap. Many students think acceleration goes to zero at the peak because velocity is zero there. It doesn't — the object is still in free fall, still under gravity, a = −9.8 m/s² the whole way. If acceleration were zero at the peak, the object would hover there forever. Say that out loud: "If g turned off at the peak, the ball would never come back down."

Worked Example 1 · Setup

An egg thrown straight up

An egg is thrown straight upward at 14.0 m/s from ground level.

Two questions. Two separate KDEA passes.
  1. How high does it go? (Pick the no-vf or the no-t equation — vf = 0 at the peak.)
  2. How long until it returns to the ground? (Δy = 0 — it starts and ends at the same height.)

Convention: up = positive → g = −9.8 m/s2.

Two separate KDEA passes is intentional — it teaches that "one problem" often needs the protocol run more than once. Part (a) uses vf = 0 at the peak as a known (it's in K). Part (b) uses Δy = 0 as a known. The selection rule is the same for both: find the missing variable, pick the equation without it. Don't let students skip from part (a)'s height directly to part (b) — have them start K from scratch each time.

The side-by-side KDEA table is the heart of the lesson. Walk part (a) first: "I know vi, vf, and g. What don't I know? t. Which equation has no t? vf²." Then part (b): "Same vi and g, but now I know Δy = 0 (it comes back). What don't I know? vf. Which equation has no vf?" The t = 0 root is physically meaningful — it's the moment of launch — and naming it explicitly prevents students from thinking the quadratic is broken.

Part 2

Projectile Motion

Free fall + constant horizontal velocity

Transition: free fall was one dimension (vertical only). Now we add a horizontal dimension that doesn't feel gravity at all. The object moves sideways at a constant speed while free-falling vertically. Two completely independent motions happening at the same time to the same object — this is the conceptual centerpiece of the entire unit.

Projectiles · 1 of 5

A projectile is any object moving under gravity alone

Once launched, the only acceleration is straight down — gravity. There is no horizontal acceleration. The object's horizontal motion is completely separate from its vertical motion.

ax = 0
horizontal acceleration
Once it leaves your hand (or the cannon, or the seawall), nothing pushes it sideways. Horizontal velocity stays constant the whole flight.
ay = g
vertical acceleration
Gravity pulls straight down, exactly like free fall. The same four kinematic equations, the same g ≈ 9.8 m/s2, the same sign convention.

Air resistance is ignored (for now). Every projectile in Unit 1 moves in a vacuum.

The two-tile layout establishes the split from the start: horizontal row gets its own set of equations and variables; vertical row gets its own. The only thing they share is time — an object can't be at two different t values in two different directions. The "vacuum" chip is a setup for Unit 2, where air resistance finally enters through friction. Don't let it distract today.

Projectiles · 2 of 5

Step one: break the launch velocity into components

This is the exact move from Workshop III — Triangles & Vectors. If you launch at speed v and angle θ, the components are:

Workshop III callback

vx = v · cos θ      vy = v · sin θ

  • vx — the horizontal piece. Adjacent to the angle → cosine.
  • vy — the vertical piece. Opposite the angle → sine.

Same right-triangle trig. Same SOH-CAH-TOA. Same component method you used on the Workshop III Skills Check — now with a launch angle θ0 instead of a ramp angle.

The callback to Workshop III is explicit and should be loud: "You already know how to do this. We did it with force vectors on an incline in the Skills Check, we did it with displacement vectors — now we're doing it with velocity." The transition from θ (a generic angle) to θ0 (the launch angle) is the only new notation, and it's the same Greek letter tour from Workshop I. Nothing new.

Projectiles · 3 of 5

Horizontal: one equation. No acceleration.

With ax = 0, every horizontal problem reduces to this:

The horizontal equation (the only one you need)

Δx = vx · t

  • vx is constant — it was v · cos θ at launch and it stays that value the entire flight.
  • t is the total time the projectile is in the air — that comes from the vertical side.

Horizontal is the easy half. Constant velocity = constant speed in one direction. No acceleration = no kinematic equations needed.

"One equation" should feel like a relief after the four-equation table. The catch — and the whole reason projectiles are hard — is that you don't have t yet. You have to go get it from the vertical motion first. This is the independence in practice: solve vertically for time, then plug time into the horizontal equation.

Projectiles · 4 of 5

Vertical: all four kinematic equations, with ay = g

The vertical motion is identical to free fall — the object doesn't know or care that it's also moving sideways. The same sign convention applies, the same four equations, the same selection rule:

vfy = viy + gt
no Δy
Final vertical velocity. Use when you know viy, g, t.
Δy = viyt + ½gt2
no vfy
Vertical displacement. Use when Δy is known and vfy doesn't matter.
vfy2 = viy2 + 2gΔy
no t
Use when time is the missing variable — often for finding vfy at a given height.
Δy = ½(viy + vfy)t
no g
Rare in free fall (g is almost always known), but valid.

The key difference from part 1: viy is no longer the full launch speed — it's v · sin θ, the vertical piece after decomposition.

Students often freeze when they see the same equations with viy instead of vi. The subscript reminds them this is the vertical component only — the horizontal piece already got handled separately (and it's constant anyway). The Δy in these equations can be positive or negative: a projectile landing below its launch point has negative Δy (if up = positive).

Projectiles · 5 of 5

Horizontal and vertical are independent. Time is the only thing they share.

This is not a trick or a shortcut — it's a fact about how the universe works. Gravity pulls down. It does not pull sideways. The sideways motion neither helps nor fights the fall.

The Projectile Decomposition video

We'll watch this in the Projectile Decomposition video — a single launch, shown from the side (pure free fall) and from above (pure constant velocity), then overlaid. Once you see it split into two independent motions, the math stops feeling like two problems and starts feeling like one story with two threads.

Horizontal: constant vx. Vertical: free fall with g. Solve them separately. t is the bridge.

The video callout is a placeholder — the video (PHY-U1-projectile-decomposition) isn't embedded in the deck, but naming it here means students know to look for it. The key verbs are deliberate: "solve them separately" — don't let students try to write one giant equation that mixes x and y. The moment they treat horizontal and vertical in the same equation, the physics is wrong.

Worked Example 2 · Setup

A coconut launched off a seawall

A coconut is launched from a 15.0 m seawall at 12.0 m/s, angled 25° above the horizontal. How far from the base of the wall does it splash down?

The plan — two KDEA passes, one for each direction
  1. Decompose the launch velocity into vx and viy.
  2. Vertical: use Δy = −15.0 m (it lands below launch) to solve for t.
  3. Horizontal: use Δx = vx · t to find the range.

Convention: up = positive, right = positive. g = −9.8 m/s2. Δy is negative — it lands below.

Miami flavor: a coconut cannon on the seawall. The setup is deliberately more complex than the egg — an angled launch from an elevated position with a negative Δy. The three-step plan (decompose, vertical for t, horizontal for Δx) is the template for every projectile problem this year. Have students copy the plan before the KDEA table.

Worked Example 2 · Decompose & Vertical

Step 1: components. Step 2: vertical KDEA for time.

StepWhat you write
Decomposevx = 12.0 · cos 25° ≈ 10.9 m/s
viy = 12.0 · sin 25° ≈ 5.07 m/s
Kviy = 5.07 m/s, Δy = −15.0 m, g = −9.8 m/s2
Dtime t (target unit: s)
EΔy = viyt + ½gt2 — no vfy
Asymbols first: −15.0 = 5.07t − 4.9t2
4.9t2 − 5.07t − 15.0 = 0
quadratic: a = 4.9, b = −5.07, c = −15.0
t = [5.07 + √(25.7 + 294)] / 9.80
t = (5.07 + 17.88) / 9.80 ≈ 2.34 s

The negative root (≈ −1.31 s) is discarded — time can't be negative. The ≈ is honest: the discriminant isn't a perfect square.

The decompose row before KDEA is deliberate — it's not part of the protocol, it's a pre-step that feeds K. Walk the quadratic carefully: students need to see that a, b, c come from the equation in standard form (4.9t² − 5.07t − 15.0 = 0), not from the numbers in the problem. This is the hardest algebra in Unit 1, and naming each coefficient explicitly prevents sign errors inside the quadratic formula.

Worked Example 2 · Horizontal

Step 3: horizontal KDEA for range.

StepWhat you write
Kvx = 10.9 m/s (constant), t = 2.34 s (from vertical), ax = 0 m/s2
Dhorizontal range Δx (target unit: m)
EΔx = vx · t — constant velocity (ax = 0)
AΔx = (10.9 m/s)(2.34 s) ≈ 25.5 m
The full answer

The coconut splashes down ≈ 25.5 m from the base of the seawall — about the length of two school buses end to end.

Two KDEA passes. One problem. Time was the bridge — solved in the vertical pass, used in the horizontal pass.

The horizontal KDEA table is deliberately simple — four rows, one multiplication. That's how it should feel: the vertical side does the heavy lifting (quadratic, signs, components), and the horizontal side just multiplies by t. The \"two school buses\" translation is a Fermi-instinct callback from Workshop I: a number without context is just digits. If there's time, ask: would a 45° launch angle go farther or shorter? (Farther — sin 45° > sin 25°, giving more vertical time AND viy.) That's a preview for next week's practice.

Quick Check · 8 minutes

Pair work: the soccer ball problem

With your partner

A soccer ball is kicked from ground level at 18.0 m/s, 30° above the horizontal. How far does it travel before hitting the ground?

  1. Decompose the velocity.
  2. Run vertical KDEA to find the time of flight. (Δy = 0 — it lands at the same height.)
  3. Run horizontal KDEA to find the range.
This is a ground-level launch — simpler than the seawall because Δy = 0, which gives a factorable quadratic (no square roots). vx = 18.0·cos 30° ≈ 15.6 m/s, viy = 18.0·sin 30° = 9.00 m/s. Vertical: 0 = 9.00t − 4.9t² → t = 0 or t = 9.00/4.9 ≈ 1.84 s. Horizontal: Δx = 15.6 × 1.84 ≈ 28.7 m. Circulate and check the decomposition step first — students who swap sin and cos won't get past step 1.
