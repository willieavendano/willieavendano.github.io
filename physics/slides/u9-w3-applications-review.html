Warm-Up · Setup

Three lamps on one household circuit

A standard wall outlet supplies 120 V. Three identical lamps, each with resistance 60 Ω, are plugged into the same outlet — wired in parallel, the way every outlet in a house is. Find the total current drawn.

Why parallel? So one lamp burning out doesn't kill the other two.

Warm-Up · KDEA

V = 120 V, three 60 Ω lamps in parallel

StepWhat you write
KV = 120 V; R1 = R2 = R3 = 60 Ω (parallel)
Dtotal current I (target unit: A)
E1/Rtotal = 3(1/60); then V = IRtotal
ARtotal = 20 Ω  →  I = 120 V / 20 Ω = 6.0 A

A typical household breaker trips around 15–20 A — 6.0 A from three lamps is comfortably safe. That's a real sanity check, not just a units check.

This slide does double duty: it's KDEA practice AND a real engineering sanity check (breaker rating vs. drawn current). Tie it forward to the capstone — every group's circuit design will need this exact same "is my current within a safe range" reasoning.

Applications

Why Wiring Choices Matter

Series and parallel aren't just textbook categories — they're design decisions

This divider opens the applications block — the goal is for students to see series vs. parallel as a deliberate engineering choice with real consequences, not an arbitrary label on a circuit diagram. Every example on the next slide is a real design decision someone made on purpose.

Applications · 1 of 1

Series vs. parallel, in the real world

🏠
Household wiring — parallel
Every outlet and light gets full 120 V independently. Unplug the toaster, the lamp stays on.
🎄
Old-style holiday lights — series
Cheap to make, but one burnt-out bulb breaks the loop and the whole string goes dark. Modern strings use parallel wiring to avoid exactly this.
🚗
Car electrical systems — parallel
Headlights, radio, and dashboard all run off the same battery independently — one failing doesn't kill the others.
⚡
Circuit breakers — Ohm's law in action
Too much current (too little resistance) trips the breaker — I = V/R climbing past a safe limit is exactly what a breaker is designed to catch.
The holiday-lights tile is the memorable one — most students have a personal story about a dead string of lights. Use it to launch straight into the next slide's practice problem, which quantifies exactly why that design was replaced in modern strings.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 8 minutes

Why old holiday lights fail together

With your partner

An old-style string has 20 identical bulbs wired in series across a 120 V outlet. Each bulb has resistance 3 Ω. Run full KDEA to find the current through the string and the voltage across each bulb.

Answer for the teacher: R_total = 20 × 3 = 60 Ω. I = 120/60 = 2.0 A. V per bulb = IR = (2.0)(3) = 6.0 V — check: 20 × 6.0 V = 120 V. The point to land after they finish: because it's series, if any ONE bulb's filament breaks (open circuit), current everywhere becomes zero and all 20 go dark — that's the physics behind the real annoyance, not just a fun fact.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 10 minutes

A combination circuit, one more time

With your partner

A 30 V supply drives R1 = 10 Ω in series with two resistors, R2 = 20 Ω and R3 = 20 Ω, wired in parallel with each other. Run full KDEA to find the total current from the supply.

Answer for the teacher: two equal 20 Ω resistors in parallel give Rp = 10 Ω (halves, same pattern as last week's practice problem). R_total = 10 + 10 = 20 Ω. I = 30/20 = 1.5 A. If a group finishes early, ask them to find the voltage across the parallel section (I × Rp = 15 V) and each branch current (0.75 A each) as a bonus check.

The Big Picture

Unit 9 is one story, not five topics.

Charge → Coulomb's law → current/voltage/resistance → Ohm's law → circuits

Same move as the Unit 1 review deck's concept map — the biggest test failure mode is treating charge, Coulomb's law, Ohm's law, and circuit topology as unrelated memorization. They're one chain: each idea is the tool needed to understand the next.

The Big Picture · 1 of 1

One chain, four links

1
Charge & Coulomb's law
The force between two charges: F = k|q1q2|/r2. This is where the electrical force comes from.
2
Current, voltage, resistance
Charge in motion (I), the push behind it (V), and what opposes it (R). This is where the variables come from.
3
Ohm's law
V = IR ties the three variables together for any single resistor. This is where the math lives.
4
Circuit topology
Series adds resistance, parallel adds reciprocals, combination reduces step by step. This is where Ohm's law scales up to a real circuit.

There's no separate "circuits equation." It's Ohm's law, applied once per resistor, organized by how they're wired.

Common Test Traps · 1 of 3

Trap #1: Mixing up series and parallel rules

The single most common test error: writing "voltage is the same" for a series circuit, or "current is the same" for a parallel one — backwards from the actual rule.

The fix

Go back to the physical picture, not the memorized word. Series = one path → current can't split, so current is what's identical. Parallel = many paths sharing two endpoints → each branch feels the same push, so voltage is what's identical.

Reasoning from the picture (one path vs. many paths) is more durable under test pressure than reciting "series: same I" from memory — a nervous student who forgets the memorized line can still rebuild it by picturing the circuit. Encourage that on the test itself.

Common Test Traps · 2 of 3

Trap #2: Skipping the reduction step

On a combination circuit, students often try to apply Ohm's law to the whole thing before finding the equivalent resistance — there is no single R to use until you've collapsed the circuit down to one resistor.

The fix

Before writing E, ask: "is this whole circuit one resistor yet?" If not, collapse a section first. Ohm's law only applies to the circuit as a whole once it's fully reduced to Rtotal.

This is the combination-circuit twin of "pick the equation by the missing variable" from Unit 1 — same discipline, different check. Students who rush straight to V = IR on a combination circuit almost always plug in one branch's resistance instead of the equivalent, giving a wildly wrong current.

Common Test Traps · 3 of 3

Trap #3: Unit mix-ups — mA, kΩ, and decimal errors

Real components are often specified in milliamps and kilohms, not bare amps and ohms — this unit's version of the Unit 0 prefix trap.

The fix

Convert to base units (A, V, Ω) in your K step, before you touch E. 2.2 kΩ = 2200 Ω. 15 mA = 0.015 A. Do it once, up front — not mid-algebra.

Direct callback to Unit 0 Workshop I's prefix conversions (km→m, nm→m) — this is the same skill, new units. Students who convert mid-calculation instead of in K are the ones who lose track of a factor of 1000 somewhere in the algebra.

The Test · 2 of 2

What KDEA looks like on test day

Your answer sheet should look like this
StepWhat the grader sees
KEvery given, with units. "V = 12 V." Not "V = 12."
DThe target quantity AND its unit. "Find I, in amps." Not "solve for I."
EThe equation or model in symbols — including the reduction step for circuits. State why you picked it.
ASymbols first, then numbers with units, then arithmetic. Final answer circled, with its unit.

The grader is looking for the protocol, not just the answer. An organized KDEA table with a small arithmetic slip scores higher than a bare correct number.

Confidence Check · Turn & Talk · 10 minutes

One last problem before Thursday

With your partner

A 9.0 V battery drives a 15 Ω resistor in series with two parallel resistors, 10 Ω and 40 Ω. Find the total current, then check that a household breaker (rated to trip at 15 A) would never notice this circuit.

This is the exact shape of a test circuit problem, with one extra step: after the math, a real-world judgment.

Answer for the teacher: parallel section Rp = (10×40)/(10+40) = 400/50 = 8 Ω. R_total = 15 + 8 = 23 Ω. I = 9.0/23 ≈ 0.39 A — far below a 15 A breaker, so yes, completely safe. The judgment step mirrors the tennis-ball "does it clear the net" closer from the Unit 1 review deck — physics answers real questions, not just numbers. Have pairs report their current and their yes/no verdict.
