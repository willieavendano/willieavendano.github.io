Rotation · 1 of 3

Describe the spin.

Angular position, angular velocity, and angular acceleration tell one story.

Open with a familiar visual: a ceiling fan, a bicycle wheel, or a record. Ask students to describe what changes as it spins. The point is to move their language from "it goes around" to an angular quantity with a direction and a unit.

Angular Velocity · 1 of 2

Angular velocity is the rate of turning.

Angular velocity tells how quickly angular position changes. Its sign follows the direction convention you choose.

Definition

ω = Δθ / Δt

  • ω is angular velocity, measured in rad/s
  • Δθ is angular displacement, measured in rad
  • Counterclockwise can be positive; clockwise can be negative

One full revolution = 2π rad. A radian is a ratio, but it still travels with the model.

Students often treat radians like a decorative label and then lose the unit in the final answer. Keep rad visible in the definition and in examples. Also separate angular velocity from rotational speed: speed is a magnitude; velocity includes the chosen direction convention.

Angular Velocity · 2 of 2

Same angular velocity means the same angle per second.

ω > 0
positive rotation
The object turns in the direction you defined as positive. A larger magnitude means faster turning.
ω = 0
not rotating
Angular position is not changing. The object may still be translating.
ω < 0
opposite rotation
The object turns in the negative direction. The sign is direction, not "bad" or "less."

A wheel at 4.0 rad/s and a disk at 4.0 rad/s turn through the same angle each second.

Rotation · 2 of 3

Where is the mass?

Rotational inertia depends on mass distribution, not only total mass.

This is the conceptual center of rotational dynamics. Use the same-mass comparison: a compact disk versus a ring. Students should predict which is harder to spin up and explain why in terms of distance from the axis, not memorize a formula.

Rotational Inertia

More mass farther from the axis means more rotational inertia.

Think of I as resistance to angular change

A larger I makes it harder to increase or decrease angular velocity. For the same torque, the angular acceleration is smaller.

τ = Iα

Compare, do not calculate
  • same mass, mass moved outward → I increases
  • same mass, mass moved inward → I decreases
  • same shape, more mass → I increases

The axis matters: the same object can have different I values about different axes.

Worked Example · KDEA

Same torque, different inertia

A constant net torque of 6.0 N·m acts on a wheel. Compare the angular acceleration for a wheel with I = 2.0 kg·m2 and one with I = 6.0 kg·m2.

StepWhat you write
Kτ = 6.0 N·m; I1 = 2.0 kg·m2; I2 = 6.0 kg·m2
Dangular acceleration α for each wheel; target unit rad/s2
Eτ = Iα
Aα = τ / I
α1 = 6.0 N·m / 2.0 kg·m2 = 3.0 rad/s2
α2 = 6.0 N·m / 6.0 kg·m2 = 1.0 rad/s2

Tripling I with the same torque makes α one-third as large.

This is the only fully worked torque problem in the deck. Emphasize the comparison, not the arithmetic: the wheel with more rotational inertia responds less to the same net torque. The radian is treated as dimensionless in the unit algebra, but show rad/s² because that is the physical quantity students must report.

Rotation · 3 of 3

What can stay constant?

Angular momentum is the rotational quantity to track when external torque is negligible.

Ask students to predict what changes when a spinning skater pulls in their arms. Do not accept "momentum increases" as the first answer; ask what is conserved and what must change to make the product stay constant.

Angular Momentum · 1 of 3

Angular momentum combines inertia and angular velocity.

Model

L = Iω

  • L is angular momentum, measured in kg·m2/s
  • I is rotational inertia, measured in kg·m2
  • ω is angular velocity, measured in rad/s

For a fixed object, larger |ω| means larger |L|. For a changing body shape, I and ω can trade off.

Keep the algebra simple but precise. The direction of L follows the direction of ω for this course's one-axis problems. Students can use the product as a reasoning tool: if one factor changes and L is fixed, the other factor must compensate.

Angular Momentum · 2 of 3

No external torque means the angular momentum stays the same.

τext ≈ 0
condition
The system is isolated from meaningful external torque about the chosen axis.
Li = Lf
conservation
The total angular momentum before equals the total angular momentum after.
Iiωi = Ifωf
algebra
If I changes, ω changes in the opposite direction to keep the product constant.

Conserved does not mean every part stays unchanged. It means the system total stays unchanged.

The external-torque condition is where many students skip the physics. Make them say it before writing the conservation equation. A skater can change angular velocity because internal forces move mass, while the total angular momentum of the skater system remains constant.

Angular Momentum · 3 of 3

Spinning skater: pull in, spin faster.

Arms extended

More mass is farther from the axis → Ii is large.

To conserve L, angular velocity is smaller: ωi is small.

Arms pulled in

Mass moves inward → If is small.

With L conserved, angular velocity increases: ωf is large.

The skater does not create angular momentum from nowhere; internal forces redistribute I.

Worked Example · KDEA

A skater changes shape

A skater has Ii = 4.0 kg·m2 and ωi = 2.0 rad/s. She pulls in her arms until If = 1.6 kg·m2. Find ωf.

StepWhat you write
KIi = 4.0 kg·m2; ωi = 2.0 rad/s; If = 1.6 kg·m2
Dfinal angular velocity ωf; target unit rad/s
ELi = Lf → Iiωi = Ifωf
Aωf = Iiωi / If
ωf = (4.0 kg·m2)(2.0 rad/s) / 1.6 kg·m2 ≈ 5.0 rad/s

I decreases by a factor of 2.5, so ω increases by a factor of 2.5.

Before revealing the worked table, ask students for a direction-only prediction: faster or slower? Then use the algebra to quantify it. The common failure is flipping the ratio and predicting 0.8 rad/s. Have students check the result against the conceptual prediction before accepting any number.

Unit 6 Review

One protocol. Three rotation stories.

Use KDEA to make the model visible before the algebra begins.

Transition into the review act. Students should now practice choosing between angular kinematics, torque dynamics, and angular momentum conservation. The practice slides intentionally provide setups only. Do not project solution work; circulate, question, and collect reasoning.

Review · Concept Map

Which idea is doing the work?

ω = Δθ/Δt
describe the spin
Use when the question connects angular displacement and elapsed time. Check the direction sign and target unit rad/s.
τ = Iα
change the spin
Use when a net torque produces angular acceleration. Compare torque and rotational inertia before calculating.
L = Iω
track the spin
Use to describe angular momentum for a rotating object. L carries direction through ω.
Li = Lf
conserve the spin
Use when external torque is negligible. If I changes, ω responds oppositely.

KDEA question: what is known, what is the target, which model connects them, and what algebra follows?

Review Act · Practice 1 · 8 minutes

Turn and talk: compare two wheels

No answer key — build the setup

Wheel A and Wheel B each experience a net torque of 12 N·m. Wheel A has I = 3.0 kg·m2; Wheel B has I = 9.0 kg·m2.

  1. Write K, D, and E for each wheel.
  2. Predict which wheel has the greater angular acceleration before calculating.
  3. Run A with units and compare the results.

Defend the comparison in one sentence: same τ, different I.

This first practice setup is intentionally accessible. Look for students who write an angular-velocity equation because the word "wheel" triggers motion, even though the target is angular acceleration. Ask, "What quantity is the question asking you to find?" and "What causes angular acceleration?"

Review Act · Practice 2 · 10 minutes

Spinning platform: identify the conserved quantity

No answer key — reasoning first

A student stands on a low-friction rotating platform. She moves from an extended-arm position with Ii = 5.0 kg·m2 and ωi = 1.2 rad/s to a tucked position with If = 2.0 kg·m2.

  1. State the external-torque condition.
  2. Write K and D for ωf, including units.
  3. Write the conservation equation and solve symbolically before substituting.

Your first line should explain why L can be conserved.

This setup tests the conceptual prerequisite rather than just substitution. If students omit the low-friction condition, press them on what would make angular momentum change. If they solve directly with a memorized ratio, require the K and D rows first so the direction of the ratio is visible.

Review Act · Practice 3 · 12 minutes

Two-stage rotation: choose the model twice

No answer key — independent setup

A flywheel starts from rest. A constant net torque of 8.0 N·m acts for 4.0 s. Its rotational inertia is 2.0 kg·m2.

  1. Find the angular acceleration and final angular velocity.
  2. Make one KDEA table for the torque step and one for the angular-velocity step.
  3. Label every target with its unit: rad/s2 or rad/s.

Hint: torque changes ω; then ω connects to time.

Common Test Traps

Catch these before they cost you points.

1
wrong target
Torque problems ask for α or ω, not automatically both. Name the target and unit in D.
2
wrong ratio
From Iiωi = Ifωf, isolate the target symbol before numbers enter.
3
missing condition
Conservation requires negligible external torque. Say the condition, do not hide it.
4
missing units
Every given and every final result needs units. An answer without units does not count.
Use this as a fast self-audit, not a lecture. Have students point to the trap they are most likely to make and write one prevention move next to it. The most important habit is slowing down for D and E before the calculator appears.
