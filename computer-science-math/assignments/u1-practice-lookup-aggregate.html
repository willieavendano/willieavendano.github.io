The job. Your own tracker is already underway — you logged your first day on Monday, out there in real life. Today you build the machine that turns those raw rows into numbers. Two moves do almost all the work in Cornerstone I: VLOOKUP fetches a food's macros out of a reference table, and SUMIF / AVERAGEIF rolls a column of the log up into one number. You rehearse both on a week of someone else's data, then apply them to your own log before you leave the room.

What you receive

Two CSV files · one week
0A reference table and a logThe same two tabs you build this unit
  • csm-p1-foods-reference.csv — the reference table: 15 foods, each with its calories, macros, serving, and Source. This is what VLOOKUP searches.
  • csm-p1-sample-log.csv — a past student's 30-row, five-day log. Only Date, Meal, Food, and Servings are filled in. The Source and macro columns are yours to compute.

Import them: in Google Sheets, File → Import each file into its own tab and name the tabs Foods and Log. In Excel, Data → From Text/CSV. Keep every value — nothing here is messy on purpose; the mess is in the formulas you write.

One trap, and it is the real lesson: exactly one food in the log is not in the reference table. Your VLOOKUP will tell you which one. Do not retype its numbers — that is the one thing this class never does.

The build — step by step

1Set up the sheetStructure first

Two tabs, named Foods and Log. On Log, the header row stays in row 1 and the data starts in row 2. Add a clear space to the right of the log for your summary block — you will build it in steps 4 and 5. Before you write a formula, write the question it answers at the top of the block.

2Look it upVLOOKUP · the first move

In the Log tab, the Source cell of the first data row should not be typed — it should be found. Write one VLOOKUP that pulls the food's details out of Foods:

K
Each row's food name · the Foods table
D
This row's Source and macros — without retyping them
E
=VLOOKUP(food, Foods!$A$2:$G$16, column, FALSE)
A
One formula, filled right and down · the $ signs locked once
  1. Look up the food name in the Foods table. The food is the row's Food cell.
  2. Return column 7 for Source. Then change the column number for each macro, or write the four macro lookups and multiply by Servings.
  3. Multiply by servings. A row of 2 eggs is two times the one-egg row — the reference value never changes, the servings do.
  4. Lock the table with $ (Foods!$A$2:$G$16) before you fill down. This is the single most common VLOOKUP mistake.
  5. Do the whole column with one formula. If you typed a number anywhere, delete it — a typed number is a picture of a model, not a model.
3Break it, then fix it#N/A is information

Look down the log. One row shows #N/A. That is not a mistake in your formula — it is a fact about the data: that food is not in the reference table.

  1. Name the food. Which row, and why did VLOOKUP fail? (One letter off? Different punctuation? Simply not there?)
  2. Fix it one of two honest ways: add the food to the Foods table with a real source, or wrap the lookup in =IFERROR(..., "not in table") so the cell says so instead of screaming.
  3. Now break it yourself. Change one food name by a single letter and watch the lookup die. Put it back. You will do this again on your own data at 11 p.m.; better to meet it now.

Why it matters A model that hides its missing data is lying. #N/A on purpose beats a made-up number.

4Add it upSUMIF · the second move

Now roll the log up. In your summary block, one row per day:

K
Five dates · a calories column · the Source column
D
Calories eaten on each day — and whether any day looks wrong
E
=SUMIF(Log!$A$2:$A$31, date, Log!$F$2:$F$31)
A
Write it once for Monday, then fill down for the other four dates
  1. Total calories per day. The date is the condition; the calories column is what gets added. Note the range rows: $A$2:$A$31 is thirty rows.
  2. Total protein per day too, pointing the sum at the protein column.
  3. Sanity-check Wednesday. One of your five daily totals is low for a reason that is not nutrition. Say what it is.
5Average itAVERAGEIF · the second move, part two
  1. The average day. =AVERAGE(...) of your five daily totals — one number for the week.
  2. Average calories by source. Use AVERAGEIF on the Source column: =AVERAGEIF(Log!$E$2:$E$31, "Restaurant nutrition", Log!$F$2:$F$31), then repeat for the other sources.
  3. Read the result. Which source produces the biggest average row? Is that because those foods are bigger, or because you trust their numbers less? One sentence.
  4. Exit ticket — answer all three in a cell or on the back: Why does a VLOOKUP return #N/A, and what are the two honest fixes? What is the difference between SUMIF and AVERAGEIF? Name the one food your log could not find, and what you did about it.
6Apply it to your own logFrom rehearsal to the real thing

Same machine, your plate. Open csm-p1-tracker-starter.xlsx and build what you just rehearsed on the practice data — on your own log, which already has Monday and today in it.

  1. VLOOKUP your own rows. Same formula you just wrote, pointed at your own food names. Any food you ate that is not in your table yet, add it to the table first — that is exactly how the table grows.
  2. SUMIF by date and AVERAGEIF by source in the Weekly Summary tab — the same two formulas, your data.
  3. Conditional-format the Source column. Give each of the six sources its color from the key, so trust is visible at a glance. (This is the week's scheduled skill, folded in here.)
  4. Leave the workbook ready to grow. Tomorrow you log the next day and the totals move on their own — proof the machine works.

Keep logging Collection runs every day from Monday, October 5 — class and non-class days alike. A missed day is a hole in the data, and the model cannot get it back.

Where students trip up

Read this before you start
  • Unlocked range. Fill a VLOOKUP down without the $ and the table slides out from under each row. Lock it once: $A$2:$G$16.
  • Wrong column number. VLOOKUP counts the column in the table, not in the sheet. Food is 1, Source is 7. Off by one and you return the wrong macro, silently.
  • Typing a value. If you can change a serving and the total does not move, that number was typed. Delete it and write the formula.
  • Assuming a miss is your error. #N/A means the name is not in the table — a spelling, a punctuation, or a genuinely new food. Diagnose before you retype.
  • Ranges that do not match. In SUMIF, the condition range and the sum range must cover the same rows. Both $A$2:$A$31 and $F$2:$F$31 — same 30 rows.
  • Summing the whole column. Point a total at column F:F and it will happily include the header or a stray note. Bound every range.

What you hand in

One workbook
