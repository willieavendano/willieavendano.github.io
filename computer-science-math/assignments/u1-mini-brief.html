The Food & Macro Tracker — Mini-Brief

Computer Science Math 2026–27 · project window Sep 14 – Oct 16 · presentations Thursday, October 15

Name
Date
The job. Track what you actually eat for 14 days. Look up the calories and macros for every food you log, and record where each number came from. Build the lookup table and the dashboard that turn your log into a model. Then present one insight about yourself — something the data told you that you did not already know.

Preview activity — Source Three Foods

Pick three foods you actually eat. For each one, find its calories and protein in two different sources. Record the number and the URL for each. Where the two sources disagree, circle it and explain why in one sentence.

Use at least two different sources per food. A source is only useful if you can name it — “the internet” is not a source.

How to pull down your data

Get your own copy
2Four sources, four routesThen into Excel or Sheets
  1. USDA FoodData Central (fdc.nal.usda.gov). Search a food, open its page, then download the data — Download Data → CSV. The CSV gives you the full nutrient table for that food.
  2. Open Food Facts (world.openfoodfacts.org). Search the product, open its page, and use the export/download option to get a CSV of the product's nutrition fields.
  3. MyFitnessPal. Use your own log: from the app or website, export your diary data, or copy the per-food nutrition you already logged. This is your data, so cite it as MyFitnessPal.
  4. Into Excel or Google Sheets. In Excel use Data → From Text/CSV (or File → Import) and pick the file; in Sheets use File → Import and choose the tab. No file? Paste the columns straight in — keep the header row, and keep every URL in its own column.

Keep the URL next to every number. A macro you cannot trace back to a source is a guess wearing a number's clothes.

The source color key

Use these six, consistently
  • MyFitnessPal — crowdsourced entries; handy, unverifiable
  • USDA FoodData Central — government reference; strongest for whole foods
  • Open Food Facts — open database; good for packaged goods
  • Manufacturer label — the brand's own numbers; check the serving size
  • Restaurant nutrition — published by the chain; portions vary by location
  • Estimate/other — your best guess; the least reliable of the six

Colour-code the Source cell on every row of your Food Table and Daily Log. At the end you will be able to see how much of your data comes from the sources you trust least.

What you hand in

Two deliverables
  • Final spreadsheet — the Food & Macro Tracker: a reference table with sourced macros, a 14-day Daily Log with lookups, and a dashboard with aggregated totals, averages, and at least one honest chart.
  • Final presentation — one insight about yourself, supported by your data, with a caveat about what would change it. Thursday, October 15.

Scored on two rubrics — one for the final spreadsheet (Data Design, Sourcing & Trust, Lookup & Aggregate Correctness, Formula Honesty, Chart & Insight) and one for the final presentation (The Insight, Evidence & Honesty, Visuals, Delivery, Response to Questions). Both are on the kit page.
