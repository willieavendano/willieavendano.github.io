Cornerstone I · Sep 14 – Oct 16

Food & Macro Tracker

Computer Science Math · 2026–27

Track what you actually eat for two weeks, source where every macro number comes from, then build the model that turns that log into an answer about yourself.

How this kit runs

  1. Concept2 decks — see the idea and why it matters.
  2. PracticeLookup & Aggregate Practice — rehearse the mechanic before it counts.
  3. ExploreMacro Explorer — play with a working version, then rebuild its engine.
  4. BuildFood & Macro Tracker (Thu Oct 15) — the cornerstone itself.

Concept

Deck The Data You Generate

Every model starts as a table someone had to design — and every macro you log was looked up somewhere. Rows are observations, columns are variables, and a number without a source is a rumor. This deck also runs the preview activity and the dataset-download walkthrough.

Deck Lookup & Aggregate

Two moves do most of the work in every model this year: fetch a fact from a reference table (VLOOKUP — including a second lookup that returns each food's source), and summarise many rows into one number (SUMIF/AVERAGEIF). Then turn the aggregate into one honest chart that states a finding.

Practice

Lookup & Aggregate Practice — csm-p1-tracker-practice.xlsx

  1. Given a 15-food reference table, return the calories for a named food — then break it on purpose by misspelling the name, and fix it with IFERROR.
  2. Total calories for a single day using SUMIF; then average across days with AVERAGEIF.
  3. Add a food to the reference table and confirm every downstream number updates without editing a formula.
  4. Find the deliberately planted hard-coded cell and replace it with a formula.

Explore

Macro Explorer

Log foods and watch macros recompute live; drag a portion slider to see how one change ripples through daily totals, and watch the source-mix bar show how much of today's calories came from your least reliable sources. Students meet the behaviour before they build the mechanism.

Build — what you hand in

Food & Macro Tracker (Thu Oct 15)

Two deliverables. (1) A working spreadsheet over ≥14 days of your own logged data: a reference table with a Source for every food, a daily log where VLOOKUP fills the macros and the source, SUMIF/AVERAGEIF aggregation into a dashboard, and one honest chart. (2) A short presentation of one insight about your own eating — a specific claim your data supports, the evidence behind it, and the caveat that would change it.

Stretch (optional). Add a second reference table (cost per serving) and answer a second question: what does a day of your eating cost — and is the cheapest day also the most trustworthy data?

Rubric — Final Spreadsheet

The tracker itself, scored out of 4.0. A 4.0 is a spreadsheet someone else could open, trust, and reuse — not one that happens to print the right number today.

Criterion4 Advanced3 Proficient2 Developing1 Beginning
Data DesignAre the rows, columns, and reference table designed so the model can answer a question? Every food is one row; the reference table holds one serving's macros and a source for each food; every column is labelled with its unit; the design scales as the log grows. A workable reference table and log with clear, unit-labelled headers; minor inconsistencies but the structure holds. Foods, servings, and macros are mixed together, or the table cannot grow without rework. Some headers have no units. No coherent table — foods, servings, and macros are typed wherever they fit. Nothing can be recomputed.
Sourcing & TrustDoes every macro's origin travel with the number? Every food's Source is recorded and color-coded with the key, and the write-up names which sources are weakest and why that matters to the conclusion. Sources are recorded and color-coded for most foods, and the write-up acknowledges that estimates are weaker than labels. Sources recorded for some foods, or recorded but never color-coded; no sense that sources differ in trustworthiness. No source recorded anywhere — the numbers are unattributed and uncheckable.
Lookup & Aggregate CorrectnessDo VLOOKUP, SUMIF, and AVERAGEIF compute the right answer, for the right reason? Every lookup returns the right macro and source; SUMIF per day and AVERAGEIF across days stay correct when a food is renamed, a source changes, or rows are added. Lookups return the right macro and source for the log as built; SUMIF/AVERAGEIF are correct and adding a row works. Some formulas work for the one row that was tested; a renamed food or a new log row breaks the sheet silently. Numbers are typed in; no working lookup or aggregation.
Formula HonestyIs the arithmetic real, or is it typed in? Nothing is hard-coded that should be computed; servings and targets are labelled inputs the formulas reference; the whole model recalculates cleanly from the source table. Formulas do the work; inputs (servings, targets) live in their own labelled cells. Any constant is a genuine, labelled constant. Some results are typed in rather than computed, or magic numbers are buried inside formulas. Key results are typed in. Changing an input changes nothing — the sheet is a picture of a model.
Chart & InsightDoes the chart answer your question, and does the write-up say what it means? The chart is chosen for the question, axes labelled with units and honest, and the write-up states a specific insight the data supports plus the caveat that would change it. The chart is appropriate and labelled with units; the insight is stated, if not quantified. A chart exists but misleads or decorates — truncated axis, wrong chart type, missing labels. The write-up describes what was built rather than what it means. No chart, or an unreadable one; no stated insight.
Stretch (optional)Optional — did you push past the floor? The cost extension answers a genuinely new question and explains why it was worth building — including whether the cheapest day was also the best-sourced. The second reference table is built correctly and its question is answered with a computed figure. The cost table is attempted but not integrated, or the second question is answered with a typed number. Not attempted (no penalty — this criterion is optional).

4 Advanced — Publishable. Someone else could pick this up and use it.3 Proficient — Works and is honest. The expected standard.2 Developing — Partly works, or works for the wrong reasons.1 Beginning — Incomplete, or the numbers cannot be trusted.

Rubric — Final Presentation

The presentation of your findings, scored out of 4.0. The model is only half the deliverable — the other half is whether you can tell someone what it means.

Criterion4 Advanced3 Proficient2 Developing1 Beginning
The InsightIs there a real, specific claim about your own eating — or just a walkthrough? Opens with a specific, non-obvious claim the data supports (e.g. restaurant days are 78% of my over-target days, and they are also my least reliable rows) — a claim that could have been wrong. A clear claim is stated and supported by the model, if broad. A claim is made but the data does not support it, or it is so broad it cannot be wrong. No finding stated; the presentation describes the file.
Evidence & HonestyCan the audience trace the claim to the numbers, and do you name the uncertainty? The number behind the claim is shown and traceable; the weakest sources are named; what would change the conclusion is stated explicitly. The evidence is shown and traceable to the model; the main assumption is stated. The claim is asserted without the number, or the number cannot be found in the model. No evidence; claims are unconnected to the data.
VisualsDo the charts earn their place and tell the truth? One or two charts, chosen for the question, labelled with units, honest axes, and readable from the back of the room. Charts are appropriate and labelled with units. Charts are crowded, mislabelled, or decorative. No chart, or one that cannot be read.
DeliveryCould a listener follow it without seeing the file? A clear arc (question → method → finding → caveat), within time, spoken not read, with any key term defined the moment it is used. Organized and on time; mostly spoken rather than read. Reads the slides, runs long or short, or jumps around. Disorganized or unfinished.
Response to QuestionsCan you defend the model and its assumptions? Answers with reference to the model; distinguishes what is known from what is assumed; names a limit or a next step. Answers reasonably and can point to where a number comes from. Cannot locate a number in the model or defend an assumption. Cannot answer, or guesses.
Stretch (optional)Optional — did you push past the floor? A second question is answered and connected back to the first — showing how the two findings explain each other. A second question is answered with its own evidence. A second question is mentioned but not shown. Not attempted (no penalty — this criterion is optional).

4 Advanced — The audience leaves knowing something about you they did not know before.3 Proficient — Clear, supported, and honest. The expected standard.2 Developing — A tour of the spreadsheet with a finding attached.1 Beginning — Unfinished, unsupported, or unreadable.
