    5 How the math works

    This is the same model you build in the spreadsheet: pick a level payment that drives the balance to exactly zero after n payments.

    P = out-the-door price − down payment      (amount financed)
r = annual contract rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100          (monthly rate)
n = term in months

payment  = P × r × (1+r)ⁿ ÷ ((1+r)ⁿ − 1)      when r > 0
payment  = P ÷ n                              when r = 0

each month
  interest  = balance × r
  principal = payment − interest
  balance   = balance − principal

total loan interest = (payment × n) − P
purchase + financing = down payment + P + total interest
    r = 0
    The formula above divides by zero, so the payment is simply the amount financed split evenly. Every payment is principal.
    P = 0
    Nothing is financed (down payment equals the out-the-door price): payment and interest are $0, and there is no schedule.
    final payment
    Computed at full precision and clipped to the remaining balance, so the schedule ends at exactly $0.00 instead of drifting a cent off.

    Contract rate is not APR, and neither one is an APY

    The contract interest rate is what the amortization formula uses. APR folds in certain borrowing fees, so the same loan can show a higher APR than its contract rate. This model charges no financing fees, so the two would match here. A savings APY is different again: it already includes compounding, so converting it to a monthly rate needs (1+APY)^(1/12) − 1 — never APY/12. Do not paste a savings APY into the loan rate field and expect a loan result.

    Assumptions in this model

    • Price is fixed. No negotiation, no trade-in, no price change while you save.
    • Equal monthly periods with payments at month-end, on time, for the full term. Real auto loans may accrue interest daily.
    • Fixed nominal rate compounded monthly. The rate does not change and there is no balloon payment.
    • No finance fees, insurance, gap coverage or extended warranty added to the loan.
    • Ownership costs are excluded: insurance, fuel, maintenance, registration, parking, repairs.
    • Display rounds to cents; the calculation does not. A column of rounded rows can differ from the rounded total by a cent.
    • Every rate on this page is a fictional classroom value, not an offer from any lender.

    Checking your own worksheet

    With P, r and n in cells, monthly payment is =IF(r=0, P/n, -PMT(r,n,P)) in Excel or Sheets (PMT returns a negative number because it is an outflow). Sum the interest column and compare it to payment × n − P.

    Compare unrounded cells. If you round each row to cents before adding, the column total can miss the true total by a few cents — this page does exactly that, and its 60-payment schedule shows $2,200.00 of interest while the rounded rows add to $2,199.96. That is display rounding, not an error. For a schedule with n rows, rounding each interest value can change its sum by up to n × $0.005. Investigate differences beyond that bound; check formulas, inputs and payment timing.
