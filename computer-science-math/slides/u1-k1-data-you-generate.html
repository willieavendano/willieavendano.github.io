The Data You Generate

Concept Deck I

where macro numbers come from · source validity · importing a dataset

Opening concept deck of Cornerstone I. Students believe their food data is "just given" — they plug a food into an app and a number appears. The whole deck is about pulling that belief apart: the number is sourced, and the source is a choice they are responsible for. Build dates: presentations are Thursday, October 15. Do not rush the validity section; it is the intellectual core, not a detour.

Why this matters

Your model starts as a table someone had to design

You did not measure your food. You looked it up — and that choice is baked into every number that follows it.

Decide the rows and columns badly now, and no formula later can undo it.

Set the stakes: the tracker is not "a spreadsheet assignment," it is a model of the student's own life. The design decisions in the first ten minutes are the ones that are expensive to reverse. A student who logs "one meal per row" cannot later ask per-food macro questions without rebuilding.

Rows and Columns · 1 of 3

Rows are observations. Columns are variables.

Each row is one thing you observed. Each column is one thing you recorded about it.

The test

If you cannot finish the sentence “one row is one …,” you do not yet have a dataset — you have a list.

Rows and Columns · 2 of 3

One food per row, or one meal per row?

DesignOne row is…Can answerCannot easily answer
One food per rowa single food eatenprotein from rice this weekwhich meal it belonged to

Both are valid. Only one answers the question you actually have — that is the decision.

The tracker uses one food per row, but do not declare that as the "right" answer before letting students see the trade-off. The failure mode to name: students mix the two in one sheet, so some rows hold a food and some hold a meal, and every downstream SUMIF silently double-counts.

The Food column is the lookup key next session. If spellings drift from the Food Table, every VLOOKUP returns #N/A — so enforce exact matching now, not after the formulas are built. Servings is a plain number with no unit because the Food Table's Serving column carries it.

Part Two

Where do the numbers come from?

Macro values are not given. They are sourced.

This divider is the pivot of the whole deck. Everything before it was structure; everything after it is trust. Slow down here and let the question sit: if the app on your phone did not measure your banana, who did, and how?

Macro Sources · 1 of 2

Five places a macro number can come from

MyFitnessPal
your own logged foods; fast to use, but values are user-submitted
USDA FoodData Central
lab-measured reference values; best for whole, unbranded foods
Open Food Facts
community-entered branded foods; good when the package is gone
Manufacturer label
the exact product in your hand; use the serving printed on it
Restaurant nutrition
what the chain publishes for a menu item; use their own page
Estimate / other
your best guess or a generic reference; flag it as low trust
Do not present these as ranked. Each is the best choice for a different situation: label beats every database for the packaged food on your desk, USDA beats everything for a raw apple, MyFitnessPal wins only on convenience. Students default to the first app they opened; this slide exists to break that default.

Validity · 1 of 3

One banana. Three sources. Three numbers.

SourceCaloriesWhat was measured
MyFitnessPal105 kcaluser-submitted average banana
USDA FoodData Central121 kcallab-measured large banana
Estimate / other89 kcalsmall banana, generic reference

All three are correct. They are simply measuring different bananas.

This is the slide students remember. Let them react — some will insist one number must be "the right one." That instinct is the thing to correct: there is no single true banana. The useful question is never "is this number right?" but "does this number match the banana I actually ate?"

Validity · 2 of 3

Why the same food gives three answers

Portion
a small banana and a large banana are not the same food
Variety
a plantain and a Cavendish share a name, not a macro profile
Raw vs cooked
water leaves when food cooks, so the same mass carries different macros
User vs lab
one value was measured with instruments, the other typed by a stranger

When two numbers disagree, one of these four is almost always the reason.

Common student failure: averaging disagreeing numbers to "be safe." That hides the disagreement instead of explaining it. The goal is to identify which of these four causes produced the gap and then pick the source that matches what they ate.

Validity · 3 of 3

A number without a source is a rumor.

The course dogma, extended

Labels and units travel with the math. Now the source travels with the number — write down where it came from, every single time.

A cell full of numbers with an empty Source column is unverifiable by design.

Tie this straight back to Unit 0's "labels and units travel with the math." Same rule, one level up: a macro value without a source earns no more trust than a physics answer without a unit. It is the exact same enforcement posture, so students recognize the standard immediately.

You will color every food's Source cell with this key so the reader can see how much to trust it.

Hand out or post the key now so students use the same hex values all unit — consistency is what makes the colors readable at a glance. The rule to state plainly: the Source column is never blank. If a value came from a guess, it earns the gray tile and says so out loud.

Pull Down a Dataset · 1 of 3

USDA FoodData Central: search to CSV

  1. Go to fdc.nal.usda.gov and search a food — try “banana.”
  2. Open a result to see per-100 g values and common serving sizes.
  3. Click Download Data, choose CSV, and save the file.
  4. The CSV opens in Excel or imports to Google Sheets with its headers intact.

Lab-measured, documented, and free — the class import starts here.

Walk this on the projector first, then let students repeat it themselves. Watch for two things: the download button on USDA sometimes yields a multi-file dataset rather than one clean CSV, and students often grab per-100 g values while their Food Table is per serving. Name both traps before they hit.

Pull Down a Dataset · 2 of 3

Two more exports you will actually use

Open Food Facts
search a barcode or product name → Download / Export → CSV. Best for packaged, branded foods.
MyFitnessPal
open a day → the … menu → Export / Print, or copy the table. This is your own log.

One source for whole foods, one for brands, one for what you actually ate.

MyFitnessPal's export is behind its own menu and varies by app version — do not promise a specific button, promise the idea: there is always a way to get your own log out, even if it means copying the table. That act of exporting is itself the lesson that your data is yours to move between tools.

Pull Down a Dataset · 3 of 3

Getting a CSV into your workbook

  1. Download or copy the file to your computer or Drive.
  2. Excel: Data → Get Data → From Text/CSV, pick the file, then Load.
  3. Google Sheets: File → Import → Upload, choose the file, then pick a destination.
  4. Check that headers landed in row 1 and every column landed whole.

We import a CSV live in class — bring a food you want to look up.

The most common failure is a silent one: the import splits on the wrong delimiter and the whole CSV lands in column A. Teach the check, not the button — "headers in row 1, one column per field" catches every bad import before the data is trusted.

Preview Activity · 12 min

Source Three Foods

The drill
  1. Pick three foods you actually eat.
  2. Find each food's calories and protein in two different sources.
  3. Record the number and the URL for each source.
  4. Circle where the two sources disagree; write one sentence explaining why.

This is the rehearsal for the real tracker.

This is the rehearsal, so grade nothing — the point is for students to feel the friction of sourcing a number before the graded build. Circulate and probe the disagreement sentence: "portion? variety? raw vs cooked? user vs lab?" A student who can name the cause is ready for next session.

Preview Activity · Debrief

What to notice when you compare

The URL is the proof
a number with no link cannot be checked by anyone, including future you
Disagreement is normal
two good sources disagree all the time — it is information, not an error
Your sentence is the insight
explaining the gap matters more than the size of the gap

Keep both numbers. Do not average them away.

Debrief hard on the third tile. Students want to collapse two numbers into one "safe" number; the tracker's whole credibility comes from keeping the source attached and the disagreement visible. If time allows, ask two students to read their disagreement sentences aloud and compare causes.

KDEA Tie-In

A source is part of the given

StepWhat changes for the tracker
Kthe number comes with a source — write it down with the value
D“calories for my banana” also names which banana and which source
Ethe lookup points at the Food Table whose rows you sourced
Athe formula returns the value; the Source cell tells you how far to trust it

K just got stricter. Know includes where the number came from.

The protocol has not changed — it has been extended. In Unit 0, K meant "list every given with units." Here it means "every given with units and a source." Students who internalized the Unit 0 version should feel this as a natural tightening, not a new rule.
