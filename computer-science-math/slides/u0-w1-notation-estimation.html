Why this matters

Notation is compression

Every formula, every spreadsheet function, every model you build this year leans on a handful of symbols. They're not decoration — they're shorthand that saves you from writing a paragraph every time.

The trade

Σ spends 3 characters on what would otherwise take a full sentence: "add up every value in this list."

Today: read the symbols. Not yet: build the spreadsheet.

Set expectations up front — no Excel today. This is entirely about being fluent enough to read and write the shorthand so that Week 2 (Excel) and Week 3 (functions/lookups/KDEA) don't stall out on "wait, what does that symbol mean."

Symbols · 1 of 4

Two symbols carry most of the weight

You'll see these two everywhere this year — in formulas on the board and, soon, in Excel functions with different names but the exact same jobs.

Σ
sigma · capital
"the sum of" — add up every value in a list. Becomes Excel's SUM() in Week 3.
Δ
delta · capital
"the change in" — final value minus starting value. Shows up constantly once we start tracking data.
Plant the Excel connection early — Σ becomes SUM(), Δ becomes "this cell minus that cell." Students who see these as the same idea in two different clothes will fly through Week 3.

Symbols · 2 of 4

Σ in money: a week of spending

Worked example

You spent $12, $8, $15, $6, $20, $30, $18 on Monday through Sunday. Total weekly spending:

Σi=17 spendingi = 12 + 8 + 15 + 6 + 20 + 30 + 18 = $109

Read it out loud: "the sum, as i goes from 1 to 7, of spending sub i." That's the whole sentence Σ replaces.

Symbols · 3 of 4

Δ in money: a stock's move

Worked example

A stock opens Monday at $142.50 and closes Friday at $138.20.

Δprice = pricefinal − priceinitial = 138.20 − 142.50 = −$4.30

The negative sign matters — it's not just "a change," it's a drop of $4.30. Δ keeps direction, not just size.

Emphasize: Δ is always "final minus initial," never the other way around, and the sign carries real meaning. This exact idea (Δ on a stock price) comes back for real in Unit 4, Stocks — worth saying out loud so it doesn't feel like a random example.

Symbols · 4 of 4

Subscripts say which one

Both examples above used a small number tucked below the line — that's a subscript, and its only job is to label which item in a list you mean.

  • spending1, spending2 … spending7 — one subscript per day
  • spendingi — a generic "day i," used when you don't want to write all 7
  • priceinitial, pricefinal — a subscript can be a word, not just a number
  • Same rule Physics uses for vi vs vf — it's a universal notation habit, not a CS-only one

A subscript is a name tag, never a math operation.

Quick check

Match the symbol

Turn and talk — 2 minutes

Which symbol goes with each phrase, and what would the Excel version of it be called?

  • "Add up every grade in the gradebook."
  • "How much the temperature rose between noon and 3pm."
  • "The third item in a list of prices."
Answers: Σ (becomes SUM in Excel), Δ (becomes "end cell minus start cell"), a subscript — price₃. Cold-call after time is up; this doubles as a formative check before functions.

Functions

Input → Rule → Output

Every function you'll ever write — on paper or in a spreadsheet — is this same machine.

This divider is the pivot of the deck: symbols were about reading notation, functions are about the idea that will carry the entire course, since Excel formulas are literally this machine.

Functions · 1 of 3

A function is a machine, not a mystery

Feed it something in, it always applies the same rule, something comes out. That's it — that's the entire idea of a function.

IN
input
hours worked: 8
RULE
the machine
multiply by $15/hour
OUT
output
paycheck: $120

Same input, same rule, same output — every single time.

Functions · 2 of 3

Writing the machine: f(x)

Worked example · a paycheck function

f(hours) = 15 × hours

  • f is just the machine's name — could be called anything
  • (hours) is the input slot — whatever you plug in
  • f(8) = 15 × 8 = $120 — plug in 8, the rule runs, $120 comes out

Change the input, the machine still runs the same rule: f(20) = $300.

Functions · 3 of 3

Preview: Excel formulas ARE functions

f(hours) = 15 × hours
on paper
the input is written in the parentheses
=15*A2
in Excel
the input is a cell reference instead of a letter

Same machine. Different way of writing "plug something in here."

This is the whole payoff of the notation half of the workshop — students who can read f(x) will recognize an Excel formula as the exact same idea on sight next week. Don't rush this slide; let it land before moving to estimation.

Quick check

Run the machine

On your whiteboard
  1. If f(x) = 3x + 2, what is f(4)?
  2. A rideshare charges f(miles) = 2 + 1.5 × miles. What's the fare for 6 miles?
Answers: f(4) = 14; fare for 6 miles = 2 + 1.5×6 = $11. Watch for students who forget order of operations (multiply before add) — a very common first-pass error on question 2.

Estimation

When you can't count it, estimate it

A Fermi problem looks impossible — until you break it into pieces you can actually guess.

Estimation · 1 of 3

The Fermi move: break it, guess it, multiply it

A Fermi problem is a question with no obvious data source. Instead of freezing, you split it into smaller pieces you can reasonably estimate, then multiply them together.

  • Is your answer in the right ballpark — not the exact right digit?
  • We'll use this same instinct all year to sanity-check spreadsheet output before trusting it

Order of magnitude first. Precision later.

Estimation · 2 of 3

Pair work: the Cushman Fermi problem

With your partner — 8 minutes

Estimate: how much does Cushman spend on printer paper in a year?

Break it into pieces you can guess — you will not look any of this up:

  • Roughly how many classrooms/offices print regularly?
  • About how many reams of paper does each one go through in a week?
  • How many weeks is the school year, and what does a ream cost?

Multiply your guesses. Report your order of magnitude (hundreds of dollars? thousands? tens of thousands?) — not a "precise" answer. There is no single correct number.

Flagship activity — budget real time. Circulate and push pairs to justify each guess out loud rather than pulling a number from nowhere. When reporting out, compare orders of magnitude across pairs rather than exact totals; the point is the method. A reasonable pair might land: ~30 rooms × 2 reams/week × 36 weeks × $5/ream ≈ $10,800 — low thousands to low tens-of-thousands is the expected band, and that spread is fine.

Estimation · 3 of 3

Orders of magnitude: the scale that matters

Before a precise number means anything, you need to know roughly how big it is. Powers of ten give you that scale fast:

10¹
tens
a stapled packet of paper
10²
hundreds
one ream
10³
thousands
a classroom's paper for the year
104+
ten-thousands+
the whole school's paper budget

Two estimates that land in the same order of magnitude basically agree — even if the digits differ.

This slide is why the Fermi problem debrief works even though every pair gets a different number: the goal was never digit-for-digit agreement, it was landing in the same order of magnitude. Tie it back to the printer paper activity explicitly.
