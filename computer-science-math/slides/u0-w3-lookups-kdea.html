Why this matters

Excel already knows how to do most of the math

You don't have to write =B2+B3+B4+B5 by hand for a list of 50 numbers. Excel ships with built-in functions for exactly this — and one function in particular, VLOOKUP, is the workhorse behind your very first cornerstone project.

Today: functions you'll use constantly, and the protocol for solving anything.

The Workhorse

VLOOKUP

"Look this up in that table, and hand me back one specific value."

Frame VLOOKUP as the single most-used function in the entire course — it reappears explicitly in the Tracker (food → calories), and the same lookup logic underlies later units too. Worth the extra time it takes to land the anatomy slide clearly.

VLOOKUP · 1 of 4

The problem: a table of data, one question at a time

You have a table — foods and their calories, students and their grades, anything with a row-per-item structure. You don't want to scroll and squint to find one row by eye. VLOOKUP searches a column for a match and returns a value from that same row.

Type the name, get the number — no scrolling required.

VLOOKUP · 2 of 4

Anatomy of the call

=VLOOKUP(lookup_value, table_array, col_index, [range_lookup])
  • lookup_value — what you're searching for (a food name, a student ID)
  • table_array — the full table to search, first column always holds the lookup value
  • col_index — which column of that table to pull the answer from (1 = the first column)
  • range_lookup — type FALSE almost always: "match exactly, no guessing"
col_index and the FALSE argument are the two places students break VLOOKUP most often — col_index off by one, or leaving range_lookup blank/TRUE and getting an approximate (wrong) match. Flag both explicitly; they'll hit this for real in the Tracker.

VLOOKUP · 3 of 4

Worked example: food → calories

A: FoodB: Calories
1Banana105
2Apple95
3Chicken Breast165
4Rice (1 cup)206

Table lives in A1:B4. To find the calories in "Chicken Breast":

=VLOOKUP("Chicken Breast", A1:B4, 2, FALSE) → 165

VLOOKUP · 4 of 4

Same idea, pointed at a cell instead of typed text

In real use, you don't type the food name into the formula — you point at a cell where a student typed or picked it, so the formula works for any entry, not just one:

Worked example

If D2 holds whatever food name a student entered:

=VLOOKUP(D2, $A$1:$B$4, 2, FALSE)

Table range is locked with $ — the same absolute-reference trick from Week 2 — so it doesn't slide when the formula gets filled down to the next student's entry.

Next week, this exact pattern becomes your Food & Macro Tracker's lookup table — Cornerstone I.

This slide is the direct bridge into Unit 1. The Tracker starter workbook has a Food Table built the same way this example is — students build the VLOOKUP formula themselves rather than getting it handed to them, so make sure the $A$1:$B$4 pattern actually lands here.

The Protocol

K · D · E · A

“The Way to Solve ANY Problem in the World”

Present this as this course's own protocol — do not say "you've seen this in Physics." For most students this is genuinely the first time. Let the claim be big on purpose: KDEA works on a spreadsheet problem exactly the same way it works anywhere else. Four letters, no exceptions.

KDEA · 1 of 4

K — what do you KNOW?

List every given the problem hands you. Every single one — and every single one gets a label or unit written right next to it.

Rule

A number without a label isn't data, it's noise. "72" isn't finished until you write 72 kcal or 72 dollars — whatever it actually is.

KDEA · 2 of 4

D — what DON'T you know?

Name the target. Not "figure it out" — the actual quantity, and the unit or label it has to come back in.

Rule

If you can't name the destination before you start, you have no way to check whether your final answer even landed in the right place — or the right cell.

KDEA · 3 of 4

E — what EQUATIONS or models connect them?

This is the bridge between K and D. In a spreadsheet, E has a very specific translation:

In Excel, E means: which function or model?

Sometimes E is AVERAGE(). Sometimes it's a VLOOKUP against a table. Sometimes it's just subtraction. Writing K and D first is what makes E obvious — you already know what you have and what you need, so the right function usually names itself.

KDEA · 4 of 4

A — solve the ALGEBRA

In a spreadsheet, "algebra" means building the actual formula — cell references first, correct function, then check that the result comes out with the label or unit you named in D.

Rule

Build the formula before you trust the number. A correct-looking number in an unlabeled column is exactly as useless as a physics answer with no unit.

The Core Dogma

Labels and units travel with the math.

Even in a spreadsheet. A column without a header doesn't count.

This is the single most enforced rule in this classroom for the rest of the year — same energy as Physics's "an answer without units does not count," just applied to columns and headers instead of handwritten work. A correctly-computed column with no header gets zero credit on any KDEA problem, full stop, starting today.

KDEA in Action

Worked example: how far off was today?

You logged calories eaten for 5 days: 2100, 1950, 2300, 2050, 2200 kcal. How far is today's total (2200 kcal) from your 5-day average?

StepWhat you write
K5 daily totals in B2:B6, each in kcal; today's total is B6 = 2200 kcal
Ddifference between today and the average (target unit: kcal)
EAVERAGE() for the mean, then subtraction
A=B6-AVERAGE(B2:B6) → 2200 − 2120 = 80 kcal above average
Walk the K row explicitly — the label kcal is not optional, and it's exactly what D also has to name. This example previews the Tracker's Dashboard math almost exactly: comparing a day's total against a rolling average is literally next week's project.
