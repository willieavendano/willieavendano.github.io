Frame it big: this isn't a toy day, it's the smallest possible version of everything the course does. Name-drop the cornerstones so students hear that the skill compounds. Tuesday gave them f(x) and the Fermi move — today those two ideas meet a spreadsheet.

The Problem

How many cafecitos does Miami drink today?

Nobody knows. No dataset exists. By the end of class you'll have a number you can defend.

Let this land — ask the room for gut reactions first (thousands? millions?) and hold the answers on the board for the debrief. The hook is that a question with no data source is still answerable, and that's precisely the situation every modeling project starts in.

The Problem · 1 of 2

The Fermi move, one more time

Tuesday's printer-paper problem, same three steps:

  • Break it into pieces you can actually guess
  • Guess each piece — with a reason, not from nowhere
  • Multiply — then report the order of magnitude, not the digits

Two answers in the same power of ten basically agree.

Fast recap, not a re-teach — 2 minutes max. If Tuesday's debrief surfaced a pair who guessed well, call back to their reasoning as the example of "a guess with a reason."

The Problem · 2 of 2

Break the cafecito count into three guesses

P
people
How many people live in the greater Miami area?
f
fraction
What fraction of them drink a cafecito on a given day?
c
cups each
How many cafecitos does a typical drinker have?

cafecitos(P, f, c) = P × f × c

That's Tuesday's function machine — three inputs, one rule, one output.

Writing it as cafecitos(P, f, c) is deliberate — it's the f(x) notation from Tuesday with three input slots instead of one. Say that out loud; the payoff comes when the same expression reappears as =B2*B3*B4 in a few slides.

On Paper · 1 of 2

Pair work: guess the pieces

With your partner — 8 minutes · paper only, no phones

For each of P, f, and c, write down:

  • your guess, and
  • one sentence of reasoning — where did that number come from?

Then multiply, and circle your order of magnitude: thousands? hundreds of thousands? millions?

Circulate and enforce the reasoning sentence — "I just felt like 50%" doesn't count; "half my family drinks one every day, but not everyone in Miami is Cuban" does. Keep the guesses on paper; they type these exact numbers into the sheet later, so nobody should erase anything.

Board tally, one mark per pair. Most defensible band: metro Miami ≈ 6 million people, a fraction around 0.2, about 2 cups each ≈ 2.4 million — so 10⁶ is the expected center, with 10⁵ entirely respectable. Don't declare a winner; ask outliers which single guess drove them high or low. That question — "which assumption is doing the damage?" — is the seed of sensitivity analysis.

Into the Sheet

Your guess deserves a machine

On paper, changing one guess means redoing all the arithmetic. Let's fix that permanently.

The pivot of the day. On paper their model is frozen; the spreadsheet makes it live. Everyone opens a laptop from here on — sheets.new gets a blank Google Sheet in one step.

Google Sheets · 1 of 5

What a spreadsheet actually is

On paper:

  • The guesses and the arithmetic are welded together
  • New guess → erase, redo, hope you didn't slip

In a sheet:

  • Assumptions live in their own cells
  • One formula reads them — change a guess, the answer updates instantly

A model = assumptions you can change + formulas that react.

This one-liner — assumptions you can change plus formulas that react — is worth putting on the wall; it's the working definition of "model" for the entire year. Everyone should have sheets.new open before advancing.

Google Sheets · 2 of 5

Step 1: label everything first

Go to sheets.new. Column A gets the names of your assumptions — before a single number goes in.

Rule one, forever: a number with no label next to it is noise.

Labels-first is a deliberate habit, not fussiness — it's the spreadsheet version of "a number without a unit isn't data." Walk the room and check everyone has the six rows before moving on. Click a cell and point out the name box showing its address; addresses star on the next slide.

The fraction goes in as a decimal (0.2, not 20 — a pair that types 20 will get 240 million and the sanity check catches it later, which is a teachable moment, not a disaster). Addresses get one sentence here and a full week of drilling next week; today they only need "B2 means column B, row 2."

Step 3: one formula wakes the machine

Click B6 and type — starting with the = sign, which tells Sheets "this is math, not text":

on paper

Same machine. The inputs are addresses now. (* means ×.)

The side-by-side is the payoff of Tuesday's f(x) slide — pause on it. Common stumble: typing B2*B3*B4 without the leading = and getting literal text. With Mr. A's example inputs the sheet shows 2400000; every pair's number will differ and that's the point.

Google Sheets · 5 of 5

Step 4: now break your own model

Click an assumption, type a new guess, hit Enter. The answer in B6 reacts instantly — no erasing, no redoing arithmetic.

  • What if only 5% of Miami drinks cafecito today?
  • What if "greater Miami" really means 3 million people, not 6?
  • What if the average drinker has 3?

Change the guess. Never the formula. That's what-if analysis.

This is the moment the spreadsheet stops being a calculator and becomes a model — let them play for a couple of minutes before the structured task. "Change the guess, never the formula" is the discipline that separates a model from a pile of typed numbers; it comes back in every cornerstone.

You try — 10 minutes

Interrogate your model

With your partner — answers in cells A8, A9, A10 as sentences
  1. Cut your fraction in half. What happens to the total — and why exactly that?
  2. Find a value of "cafecitos per drinker" that pushes the total past 10 million. Is that value believable?
  3. Which of your three assumptions are you least sure about? Nudge it up and down — how hard does the answer swing?
Q1 checks proportional reasoning (halving one factor halves the product — some students expect something messier). Q2 forces them to run the machine backwards. Q3 is sensitivity analysis without the name; the fraction f is usually the shakiest guess and the one the model swings hardest on. Writing answers as sentences in cells starts the habit of annotating models — graded models this year always carry written reasoning.

Triangulate · 1 of 2

Don't trust one route. Build a second one.

Estimate the same quantity a completely different way — from the supply side:

cafecitos ≈ places serving cafecito × cafecitos per place per day

  • Ventanitas, cafeterias, bakeries, restaurants — how many spots in greater Miami?
  • A busy window pours how many a day? A slow one?

Two independent routes landing in the same power of ten = believe the ballpark.

Triangulation is how real estimators check themselves when no ground truth exists — same trick engineers and analysts use. Sharp students will notice this route misses cafecitos brewed at home on the stovetop moka pot; celebrate that catch out loud — spotting what a model leaves out is a top-of-rubric skill all year.

Triangulate · 2 of 2

Build route 2 under route 1

With your partner — 10 minutes, same sheet
  1. Rows 8–9: label and guess the two new assumptions
  2. Row 11: label Cafecitos today (route 2), then write the formula =B8*B9
  3. Compare B6 and B11. Same power of ten? Within 10× of each other?

If the two routes disagree wildly, don't panic — figure out which single guess is doing the damage, and say so in a sentence in A13.

No step-by-step slides this time — that's deliberate; they just did the identical build with training wheels. A defensible route 2: ≈ 3,000 spots × ≈ 300 cafecitos/day ≈ 900,000 — within 3× of the demand-side ≈ 2.4 million, i.e. the same power of ten. Pairs whose routes disagree by 100× almost always have the fraction-vs-percent slip from step 2 or a lost zero in the spot count.

Debrief

What just happened

  • A question with no data source became a defensible number — twice
  • Your guesses became labeled inputs; your arithmetic became one formula
  • Changing a guess cost zero effort — so you actually explored, instead of defending your first answer
  • Two independent routes agreeing is evidence; where they disagree points at the weakest assumption

You will run this exact loop on every cornerstone this year.

Collect a few B6-vs-B11 pairs on the board and celebrate agreement in the same power of ten. If time allows, ask: "what would you look up first if you were allowed one real number?" — usually the metro population, which is the least interesting guess. The fraction is where the real uncertainty lives.

Recap

Your toolbox after today

  • The Fermi move — break it, guess each piece with a reason, multiply, check the power of ten
  • Labels first — every number gets a name in the cell next to it
  • Cell addresses — B2 is column B, row 2; formulas point at addresses, not numbers
  • = starts a formula — =B2*B3*B4 is f(x) wearing spreadsheet clothes
  • What-if analysis — change the guess, never the formula
  • Triangulation — a second independent route is your check
