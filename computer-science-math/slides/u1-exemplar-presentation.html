14 Days, One Question

Maya R. — a 4.0 presentation

14 days · 96 logged rows · one finding I did not expect

This deck is the demonstrative exemplar for Cornerstone I: what a 4.0 looks and sounds like. It is written as Maya's finished presentation, so play it straight — do not narrate it as a template during class. Use it before the build so students know the target, then again at rehearsal so they can self-assess against it. Maya's numbers are internally consistent: 7 restaurant days, 7 home days, 9 days over her 2,000 kcal target, 7 of those 9 restaurant days. If a student challenges a number, all of them trace back to that table.

The Finding · up front

I assumed my problem was protein. The data says it is restaurant calories.

My one-sentence conclusion

“78% of my over-target days were restaurant days — and restaurant days are exactly the days my source data is weakest.”

State the finding first. The rest of the deck is the evidence for it.

Lead with the conclusion, not the process — the rubric's Communication criterion rewards a stated insight over a tour of the spreadsheet. Maya spends the next slides earning this sentence. Push students to notice that the insight names both a behaviour (restaurant calories) and a data-quality problem (weak sources) in the same breath. That pairing is the 4.0 move.

Part One

The Evidence

What I logged, how I built it, and what the numbers actually said.

Act break. Keep it short — this is a pacing beat. The next four slides are data, method, dashboard, chart; students can follow the same order in their own presentations.

Every food in my reference table carried its provenance. I logged the source on every row — not just the food name — so I could ask where the calories came from, not only how many.

The Source column is data too. Most people leave it out.

The source column is the design decision that makes this deck's finding possible. Without it, "restaurant days are my problem" is an impression; with it, it is a count. When students build their log, the Source column is the one they are most tempted to skip because it slows down data entry. Say out loud that skipping it would have killed Maya's entire conclusion.

Colour is doing real work here: it lets an audience see the trust problem at a glance in the chart two slides from now. Make sure students use the same six hex values so their workbooks and their decks read as one system.

The Method

Three moves did all the work

VLOOKUP — fetch the macros and the source

=VLOOKUP($C2, FoodTable, 3, FALSE)

Same lookup, pointed at the Source column (col_index 7) so every row remembers where it came from.

SUMIF — total each day by date

=SUMIF(Log!$A:$A, A2, Log!$F:$F)

AVERAGEIF — summarise across the 14 days

=AVERAGEIF(Days!$B:$B, "restaurant", Days!$C:$C)

Common student failure modes to watch before presentations: col_index off by one (usually pulling Protein instead of Calories), forgetting FALSE so VLOOKUP silently approximate-matches, and SUMIF criteria that do not exactly match the date format in the log. A fourth: hard-coding the day totals and losing the model. The last AVERAGEIF is exactly how Maya split restaurant days from home days without touching a formula later.

Honest-visual checklist on display: y-axis starts at zero (no truncation), the value axis is labelled kcal, the target is drawn as a reference line, and over-target days are coded by cause rather than just by colour. If a student's chart truncates the axis to exaggerate a difference, this slide is the counter-example. Ask: what would this chart look like if the axis started at 1,500?

Same person, same appetite. The variable that moved the number was where I ate.

This is the payoff. The three tiles are the finding, the effect size, and the control group all at once: restaurant days are the treatment, home days the control, and the gap between +35% and +1% is the whole story. Have students look for a natural control group in their own logs — weekdays vs weekends, home vs out, school lunch vs not. That is what turns a log into an argument.

The Caveat

My evidence has a hole in it — and it is on the restaurant side

Restaurant foods were mostly logged as Restaurant nutrition or Estimate/other — the two least reliable sources. Portions vary by location, and one plate is rarely one serving.

So the days that drove my conclusion are also the days I trust least.

What would change my conclusion
  • Portion data from the restaurant's own nutrition sheet, not an estimate.
  • A week of restaurant days where I weighed or measured the plate.
  • If the overage turned out to be one or two large meals, not the whole day.
This slide is the difference between a 3 and a 4 on Communication. Students often think a caveat weakens the argument; it does the opposite — it tells the audience which part of the result to trust. Acknowledge that Maya's restaurant totals could be off by a meaningful margin in either direction, and that the finding is a strong lead, not a proven law.

Next

What I would do with a second two weeks

  1. Log restaurant meals twice. Once as an estimate, once from the restaurant's published nutrition — then measure the disagreement.
  2. Split the overage by meal. Add a Meal column so I can see whether one dinner is carrying the whole restaurant day.
  3. Set a restaurant-day target, not just a daily one. The tracker already knows the day; now it should know the plan.

A model you can't act on is a scrapbook. These are moves.

The rubric does not require next steps, but a 4.0 presentation usually has them: it shows the student sees the model as a tool rather than an assignment. Note that every next step is testable — it changes the data she would collect, not just the chart she would draw.
