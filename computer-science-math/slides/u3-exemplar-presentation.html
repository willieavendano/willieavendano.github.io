My Hand, and the Month It Breaks

Camila Ortiz — a 4.0 midterm presentation

$64,000 salary · $4,467 take-home · 17 expense lines · one hazard that empties the fund in 8 months

This deck is the demonstrative exemplar for Cornerstone III, the midterm: what a 4.0 looks and sounds like. Like the Cornerstone I exemplar, it is written as Camila's finished four-minute talk, so play it straight during class — do not narrate it as a template. Slides 1–6 are the talk; everything from the divider onward is the teacher-facing debrief ("how this earned a 4") and is NOT part of the four minutes. All of Camila's numbers come from her exemplar workbook (csm-cs3-exemplar.xlsx) and are internally consistent: take-home $4,467.00, expenses $3,531.54, savings $935.46, savings rate ≈20.9%. If a student challenges a figure, every one of them traces back to that file. The apartment listing and the car listing are both illustrative; real students must link real listings.

The scenario slide is where the rubric's Model Correctness does its heaviest lifting: one change per scenario, side by side, so the audience can see cause and effect. The Break · 4 of 5

A 30% hours cut breaks me in 8 months — my four cuts buy 9 more

The break

Savings go to −$404.64 / mo. My $3,000 emergency fund is gone in 8 months.

My fix — four cuts, $225/mo
  • Eating out / cafecitos −$100
  • Entertainment −$70
  • Gym −$35
  • Subscriptions −$20

→ −$179.64 / mo, runway 17 months.

"My budget breaks — here is my fix" is an expected outcome, not a failure, and this is the slide that earns the level. Camila does three things at a 4: she computes months-to-zero by formula ($3,000 ÷ $404.64 rounds up to 8), she models a fix rather than just worrying about it (four cuts totalling $225, leaving −$179.64 and 17 months of runway), and she shows both on one honest chart, dollars from zero, axes labelled. The chart's simplifying assumption — no interest on the fund — is worth saying out loud. Common failure: reporting "the fund lasts about 8 months" without showing the division, or naming a fix without a number attached. Ask: what is the smallest cut that gets the runway past 12 months?

Recommendation · 5 of 5

Run this plan, keep the 5-year loan, and name the weak assumption

What I would actually do:

  • 5-year loan — $621.36/mo, ≈11.7% of gross, the shortest term under ~12%.
  • Protect the ≈20.9% savings rate — it is what makes the four-cut fix work.
  • Hold 4–6 months of expenses, not the $3,000 floor.

Save first, borrow short, rehearse the bad month.

One limitation I will say out loud

Income volatility. My whole plan rests on a steady paycheck. The hours-cut scenario is survivable only because the fix is already modelled — and even then it is 17 months, not forever.

Also honestly: car insurance is a published Miami average, and the apartment listing is illustrative.

The recommendation slide tests two rubric criteria at once: Communication (conclusion first, then the caveat) and Model Correctness (the numbers quoted are the modelled ones). Camila's recommendation is internally consistent with the whole talk — a short term under ~12% of gross, a savings rate she defends, and a fund above the $3,000 default. The limitation is not decoration: income volatility is the actual load-bearing assumption, and naming it tells the audience which part of the model to trust. Push students to make their limitation specific to their own model (an average, an estimate, an illustrative listing) rather than a generic "nothing is certain."

Point out that the yellow cells are inputs and the only place a raw number is allowed to live: the tax year, the standard deduction, the FICA and Florida rates, and every bracket ceiling and rate (B5:B9 and B12:B23). Everything else is a formula. There is no SUMPRODUCT here — the federal tax is built bracket by bracket and then summed. B10 pulls the gross salary by live link (='Profile & Car'!$B$9); B11 subtracts the standard deduction (=MAX(0,$B$10-$B$6)); each bracket tax clips the taxable income to that bracket and multiplies by its rate (B17 =MAX(0,MIN($B$11,$B$15)-$B$12)*$B$16); and the selected cell B24 is the sum of the four bracket taxes, =$B$14+$B$17+$B$20+$B$23 = $5,500.00. B25 is =$B$10*$B$7 (FICA), B26 is =$B$10*$B$8 (Florida, $0.00), B27 is =$B$10-$B$24-$B$25-$B$26, and B28 rounds the month, =ROUND($B$27/12,2) = $4,467.00. The D column is the audit trail: every computed cell shows its formula, every input its source. Common failure to look for: brackets typed as literal rates inside one formula (0.1*(x)+0.02*(x-12400)…) — that passes today's check but fails Formula Honesty, and it cannot show the bracket-by-bracket build.

The scenarios tab is where a student proves the model is live rather than a snapshot. It has two regions. Rows 5–17 are the labelled inputs — one change per case (hurricane $1,150, rent +8%, hours cut 30%, a one-time $1,000 bonus) plus the four modelled cuts and their remainders; the yellow cells are the only raw numbers, and B17 totals the cuts (=SUM($B$9,$B$11,$B$13,$B$15) = $225). (3) Months-to-zero is a formula, not a typed 8: the selected cell E26 is =IF('Budget'!$B$31+E25<=0,1,IF(E23<0,1+ROUNDUP(('Budget'!$B$31+E25)/-E23,0),"Not depleted")) — if the fund is already gone it is month 1; otherwise, if savings are negative, it is one plus the fund divided by the monthly drain, rounded up; otherwise it never depletes. Use this slide as the shared vocabulary when you hand back work. Two of these are the ones students underestimate. Formula Honesty is not "did you use a formula" but "could a stranger read this workbook and tell which numbers are facts and which are choices" — hence the input cells and the ≈ marks. Sources & Assumptions is not "did you cite something" but "does every single line have a provenance, including the embarrassing ones marked assumption." The grade is the average of these criteria to one decimal, so a brilliant model with a sloppy sources column lands between a 3 and a 4, not at a 4.

Teacher Debrief · 5 of 6 — Live defense

“I'm raising your rent $200” — what moves, and which formula moves it

NumberBeforeAfter
My rent share$1,300.00$1,500.00

Savings rate≈20.9%≈16.5%
Rent share of gross≈24.4%≈28.1%

A $200 bump is ≈15.4% of my rent — bigger than the modelled 8% renewal (+$104).

Which cells move, and which formulas move them
  1. One input: listed rent, Housing B7 ($2,600 → $3,000).
  2. My share is a formula: Housing B14 =$B$7/$B$13 → $1,500; Housing B15 → ≈28.1%.
  3. Budget B5 =ROUND('Housing'!$B$14,2) follows, so the Fixed SUMIF B22 rises $200.
  4. Total Budget B24 =SUM(B5:B21) rises, so Savings B26 falls by exactly $200.
  5. Rate Budget B27 and every months-to-zero cell (Scenarios 22–26) re-solve on their own.

Nothing is retyped — I move one cell; the links follow.

This is the move the Live Defense criterion is built around. Camila does not read the answer off the slide; she says which cell she changed, then traces the ripple. The changed input is the listed rent, Housing!B7 ($2,600 → $3,000) — not the rent share, which is itself a formula (Housing!B14 =$B$7/$B$13, with B13 = people sharing). That pushes B14 to $1,500 and B15 (share of gross) to ≈28.1%; Budget!B5 follows by link (=ROUND('Housing'!$B$14,2)), so the Fixed SUMIF Budget!B22 rises $200, the total Budget!B24 (=SUM(B5:B21)) rises, savings Budget!B26 (=ROUND($B$25-$B$24,2)) falls by exactly $200, the rate Budget!B27 falls to ≈16.5%, and every months-to-zero cell in Scenarios rows 22–26 re-solves. Watch for the trap answer — a student who adds $200 to the savings number by hand instead of to the listed-rent input, or who "changes the rent share" and forgets it is a formula. Another trap: forgetting that a $200 rise on $1,300 is ≈15.4%, a much bigger shock than the 8% renewal she modelled, so the honest answer names that difference. If a student predicts the new savings rate to two decimals (16.46%), even better: it means the formula is theirs.

Teacher Debrief · 6 of 6 — Watch-fors

Common ways a strong talk still loses a level

  • Typed totals. A number where a SUMIF should be — Formula Honesty drops, and the live defense exposes it.
  • A truncated chart axis. Dollars that do not start at zero exaggerate the difference — Communication.
  • A missing provenance. Even one line with no source and no "assumption" label — Sources & Assumptions.
  • Scenarios that move two things. A rent hike and a spending cut in the same case — Model Correctness.
  • Months-to-zero guessed. A typed 8 that does not change when the drain changes — Model Correctness.
  • Defending from the slide. Reciting the number instead of naming the cell and the formula — Live Defense.

A 4 is not a harder spreadsheet. It is one that answers a question you did not script.

Read these as a checklist, not a scold — each one is a single fix away from a level. The fastest diagnostic is the live defense: pick one input, change it in the student's own workbook, and watch what happens. If the totals move, the model is real; if a number stays frozen, that cell was typed and the interview just found it. The last line is the one to leave students with: a 4 is a model that survives a question it was not built to answer.
