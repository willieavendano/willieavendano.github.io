Expected Value & the House Edge

Concept

Expected value · simulation · the law of large numbers

Two-week interlude between the budget cornerstone and stocks. Frame it that way from the first minute: this is not a detour into gambling, it is the unit where randomness stops being scary and becomes something you can compute with. Cornerstone IV's Monte Carlo work is unusable without it.

The question

Why does a casino let you win?

The setup · 1 of 6

Nobody is cheating

  • The wheel is fair. The dice are fair. The deck is honest.
  • People walk out ahead every single night — that part is real.
  • The house still makes a predictable profit, budgeted a year in advance.

All three of those are true at the same time. That is the thing worth explaining.

Resist explaining yet. Let the tension sit — students almost always assume the game must be rigged, and the reveal lands harder if they say that out loud first. Ask: if it is fair, where does the profit come from?

Expected value · 2 of 6

What one play is worth

Multiply each outcome by how often it happens, and add.

\( EV = p_{\text{win}}\,(\text{what you gain}) + p_{\text{lose}}\,(\text{what you lose}) \)

Roulette, betting red on $1. American wheels have 38 pockets: 18 red, 18 black, 2 green.

\( EV = \tfrac{18}{38}(+1) + \tfrac{20}{38}(-1) = -\tfrac{2}{38} \approx -0.0526 \)

Every $1 on red is worth about 94.7¢ the moment it leaves your hand.

Walk the arithmetic slowly. The single most common error is forgetting the two green pockets — students compute 18/36 and get zero, then wonder where the business is. That mistake is worth making publicly: the whole industry lives in those two pockets.

Expected value · 3 of 6

You are paid as if the greens did not exist

That gap is the house edge. It is disclosed, printed, and unchanging.

This is the deck's central image. If a student remembers one slide in two weeks, make it this one. Same structure appears in insurance premiums, payment processing fees, and the spread on every trade they will place in Cornerstone IV.

Turn and talk, 90 seconds: those two roulette bets are mathematically the same bet, so why does one feel thrilling and the other feel steady? Answer is variance, which is the next slide. Do not give it away — let a student say "because you almost never win the single number."

Variance · 5 of 6

Same edge, different night

  • Red/black. You win a little, often. The bankroll drifts down slowly.
  • Single number. You lose 37 times, then win 35 at once. Same expected value, violent path.
  • Expected value tells you the destination. Variance tells you the ride.

Nobody has ever been ruined by an expected value. People are ruined by variance.

Bridge to finance directly: a bond and a small-cap stock can have similar expected returns and completely different survival odds for someone who needs the money in March. Cornerstone IV asks them to report percentiles for exactly this reason.

The mechanism

So how does the house get paid?

The law of large numbers · 6 of 6

Patience, and nothing else

  • Over 10 plays, almost anything can happen. You might be up 60%.
  • Over 1,000, the average starts to look like the expected value.
  • Over 100,000, it is the expected value, to a fraction of a cent.

The casino does not need to beat you. It needs enough plays.

Say the punchline plainly: you are the small sample and the house is the large one. That asymmetry is the entire business. Then stop talking and open the simulator — the next slide is the point of the lesson and it has to be seen, not described.

Do this now · 8 minutes

Watch it converge

  • Open tools/casino-simulator.html. Choose roulette red/black.
  • Run 10 plays. Write down the average per $1. Run it again. And again.
  • Now run 100,000. Run that again. And again.
  • Which number moved, and which one refused to?

Then switch to the single-number bet at 100,000 and compare the shape of the line.

Circulate and ask individuals to read their 10-play number out loud — the spread across the room is the demonstration. Then have them all read their 100,000-play number: everyone will be within a hair of −0.0526. That contrast is the lesson and no deck can deliver it.

Recap

What you now know

  • Expected value is what one play is worth on average — computed, never guessed.
  • House edge is negative expected value, stated politely.
  • Variance is the ride; expected value is the destination.
  • The law of large numbers is why the destination always arrives — eventually.
Point forward explicitly: in Cornerstone IV they run a thousand simulated futures for a real portfolio. It works for exactly the reason they just watched on screen.
