The Grid · 1 of 3

A spreadsheet is a coordinate system

You've used (x, y) coordinates before. A spreadsheet cell address is the exact same idea, just with a letter instead of a number for one axis:

A
B
C
1
A1
B1
C1
2
A2
B2
C2
3
A3
B3
C3

Column letter, then row number — B2 means "column B, row 2." Literally x, then y.

This is the single most important framing move of the workshop: A1 notation is not a new idea, it's coordinate geometry with a letter swapped in for the first axis. Students who've never used a spreadsheet but have used a coordinate plane already know this.

Reading and writing addresses

top-left corner of the grid
D12
column D, row 12
4th column, 12th row down
B2:B8

every cell from B2 through B8

A range is just two addresses with a colon between them — "everything from here to there."

You try · 3 minutes

Name that cell

Open a blank spreadsheet
  1. Click into the cell at column C, row 5. What does the name box say?
  2. Type your name into A1, your age into A2, and your favorite number into A3.
  3. What range covers all three cells you just filled?
Answers: 1) C5, 2) whatever they typed, 3) A1:A3. This is the first hands-on-keyboard moment of the workshop — walk the room and confirm everyone actually has a spreadsheet open before moving on.

Formulas · 1 of 4

Every formula starts with =

Type a number into a cell, it's just a number. Type = first, and Excel knows "this is a machine to run, not text to store."

42
a value
Excel stores exactly this — the number 42, nothing more
=20+22
a formula
Excel runs the math and displays 42

The cell shows 42 either way — but only one of them is actually a function machine.

Formulas · 2 of 4

The big idea: reference, don't retype

You could type a value straight into a formula — =20+22 — but that only works once. The move that makes a spreadsheet powerful is pointing at other cells instead:

Rule

=B2+B3 instead of =12+8. Change what's typed in B2, and every formula that references it updates on its own — nothing you have to retype by hand.

A reference is a live pointer. A typed-in number is frozen forever.

This is the single biggest mental shift for students new to spreadsheets — coming from a calculator mindset, typing the raw numbers feels natural, but it's exactly the habit to break today. Every example from here forward should visibly use cell references, never hardcoded numbers.

Formulas · 3 of 4

Worked example: why hardcoding breaks

AB
1Hourly wage15
2Hours worked8
3Paycheck=B1*B2

Give yourself a raise — change B1 to 17. =B1*B2 instantly shows $136. A formula written as =15*8 would still say $120, silently wrong, forever.

You try · 4 minutes

Fix the hardcoded formula

In your spreadsheet
  1. In A1 type Price, in B1 type 19.99.
  2. In A2 type Quantity, in B2 type 3.
  3. In B3, write a formula for total cost using cell references, not typed numbers.
  4. Now change B2 to 5. Did B3 update on its own?
Expected formula: =B1*B2. At quantity 3 it reads $59.97; after changing B2 to 5 it should update on its own to $99.95 with no further typing. Confirm B3 actually updates live — if it doesn't, they typed the number instead of the reference. That's the exact mistake this activity is designed to surface.

Fill & References

$ locks it. Nothing locks, it slides.

One formula, dragged down a column, needs to know what should change and what shouldn't.

Fill · 1 of 3

Relative vs. absolute references

Relative — B2

  • Shifts as you drag/fill the formula
  • The default for every reference you type

Absolute — $B$2

  • Frozen in place no matter where you drag it
  • Use it for a value every row needs, like a tax rate

The dollar sign locks the column, the row, or both — wherever you put it.

Fill · 2 of 3

Worked example: fill-down that breaks (and one that doesn't)

ABC
1Tax rate0.07
2Item price10=B2*$B$1
3Item price25=B3*$B$1
4Item price40=B4*$B$1

Fill C2 down to C3 and C4: B2 slides to B3, B4 — each row's own price. $B$1 stays locked on the tax rate every time.

Walk this one live if possible — have students type =B2*B1 without the dollar signs first, fill down, and watch row 3 and 4 come out wrong (or blank) because B1 slid to B2 and B3. Then fix it with $B$1 and fill again so they see the contrast directly, not just read about it.

Fill · 3 of 3

Autofill isn't just formulas — it detects patterns

Mon
a weekday
drag → Tue, Wed, Thu…
1, 2
two numbers
drag → 3, 4, 5… (Excel detects the step)
Jan
a month
drag → Feb, Mar, Apr…

Give Excel two data points and it guesses the pattern — always check what it guessed.

You try · 3 minutes

Predict the fill

Before you touch the keyboard
  1. If A1 = 5 and A2 = 10, what will A3 and A4 autofill to?
  2. If B1 = =C1*2 and you fill it down to B2, what does B2's formula say?

Now test both in your spreadsheet — were you right?

Answers: A3=15, A4=20 (step of 5 detected). B2 becomes =C2*2 — the reference slides down one row because it's relative, not absolute. Have students predict before testing; this is where the relative/absolute idea from earlier gets reinforced through pattern-detection instead of a formula.

Charts · 2 of 2

Preview: charts can lie

A chart is just a picture of your data — and pictures can be staged. Watch for these before you ever trust one, including your own:

  • An axis that doesn't start at zero can make a tiny difference look enormous
  • A stretched or squished axis can exaggerate or hide a trend

"Honest charts" is a running theme all year — the first real practice comes with the Tracker in Unit 1.

Keep this brief today — it's a preview, not the lesson. The Food & Macro Tracker (Unit 1) is where chart honesty actually gets graded, so plant the flag here and move on rather than over-teaching axis manipulation right now.
