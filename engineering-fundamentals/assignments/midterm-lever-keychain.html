You will design a keychain bottle opener as a gift for one real person in your life, model it in Tinkercad, 3D-print it, test it, improve it, and present it. A bottle opener is a lever, so this project is also how you show what you learned in Unit 2: where the fulcrum, load and effort are, and how the lengths of the arms decide how much the lever multiplies your force.

The Challenge

A good gift fits the person. A good tool does its job. Your keychain has to do both. It should feel like it was made for this person, because you found out what matters to them, and it has to actually open a bottle without snapping, because you designed the lever on purpose.

  • Solo project: you interview, design, model, test and present your own opener. Classmates can give feedback, but your sketches, model and data must be your own.
  • A real client: pick one real person who opens bottled drinks like sparkling water, soda or root beer: a parent, grandparent, sibling, coach or neighbor. Interview them, ideally in person over Thanksgiving break.
  • Software: Tinkercad, which you learn on Friday, November 20. No experience is expected. The model is simple shapes, holes and text.
  • Two versions: you print v1, test it, measure what went wrong, and print an improved v2. The change from v1 to v2 is the most important evidence in your presentation.
The real test: does it ACTUALLY open a bottle? On December 17 you will open a capped glass bottle with your printed opener live, in front of the class, in 3 tries or fewer. A beautiful keychain that can't lift a cap is not a working solution. If v1 bends, slips off the cap or snaps, that's engineering: figure out exactly why (too thin? tooth too short? arm too short?), change the model, and test v2. If v2 still fails, present the evidence: what you measured, what you changed and what you'd change next. That earns real credit. Pretending it works earns none.

How a Bottle Opener Works: It's a Lever

When you lift the handle of a bottle opener, the opener pivots on the top of the cap (the far side, away from your hand). The tooth hooks under the near edge of the cap and pulls it up. Your hand is the effort, at the end of the handle.

IMA = effort arm ÷ load arm   (both measured from the fulcrum, in mm)
AMA = load force ÷ effort force   (both in N)
efficiency = AMA ÷ IMA × 100%

What this means for your design. The load arm is set by the cap: it's roughly the distance across the cap, so you can't make it much shorter. To raise IMA you make the effort arm longer, but criterion 3 caps the length at 90 mm. That tradeoff between size and mechanical advantage is the heart of your design. A longer handle also bends more, so thickness matters too.

In Tinkercad: drop the Ruler on the workplane at your fulcrum point. Read the distance to the tip of the tooth (load arm) and to the middle of where your thumb or fingers push (effort arm). Screenshot it.

Measurement Worksheet

A 3D printer does not print exactly what you model. Plastic shrinks, the first layers squish, and holes come out small. Your opener only works if you measure the real things it must fit, design to those numbers, and then check the print against the model.

How to use it. A: measure each real-world feature three times with calipers and record the average (mm). B: the value you used in Tinkercad. C: measure the same feature on your print. Error = C − B. Holes usually print 0.2–0.4 mm small, so if a hole has to fit something, make it a little bigger on purpose and say so.

Design tips that save a reprint. Make the opener at least 4–5 mm thick; thin PLA bends. Round the corners where the handle meets the tooth, because sharp inside corners are where prints crack. Keep raised or cut-in text at least 0.8 mm deep and 6 mm tall, or the printer can't show it. Put the ring hole at the end of the handle, where it won't be in the way of your fingers.

Part 2 · The written check (individual, about 25 minutes)

After presentations, everyone completes a short written check on their own. It covers Unit 2 and your own project data. You may use a calculator and your own Lever and Measurement Worksheets, nothing else. Expect to:

  • identify the fulcrum, load and effort, and the lever class (first, second or third), for everyday objects you haven't seen in class;

  • predict what happens to IMA and to the effort force when an arm gets longer or shorter;
  • explain, using a number from your own worksheet, one way your print or test didn't match your model, and what you would change.
