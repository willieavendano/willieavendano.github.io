Rising Water, Rising Stakes

How Miami answers the ocean — and who gets protected

Climate action · UN SDG 13 · one challenge · three solutions · five minutes

This deck is the MODEL for Project 1.1.5, not the project itself — walk students through it and name the moves as you go: story hook, organized sections with signposts, three different KINDS of solutions (machine, ecosystem, law), local evidence, an ethical question, real sources. Part 1 · 0:30 – 1:45

The Challenge

What it is · why it's a grand challenge · why it's Miami

Signposts are a delivery skill from the rubric: telling the audience where you are before each section. Three sections, three solutions, one impact slide, one close — that's the whole organizational structure students should imitate.

The Challenge · 1 of 3

Preparing for the water that's already here

The chain reaction:

  • Warmer air → warmer, expanding oceans + melting ice
  • Higher seas push into streets, drains, and wells
  • Miami: flat, low, porous limestone

Our anchor from Activity 1.1.3:

  • UN SDG 13: Climate Action
  • NAE: “Restore and improve urban infrastructure”
Why limestone matters

A seawall stops water flowing over the top — not water rising through spongy coral rock. Every solution below has to answer for that.

"Teach a bit" is the requirement — 45 seconds of clear explanation, one causal chain, one local geology fact. The limestone detail is the payoff: it shows research depth beyond a Google first hit and sets up why one solution alone won't work.

Part 2 · 1:45 – 3:45

Three Solutions

One problem, three kinds of answers: a machine, an ecosystem, and a law

The assignment requires THREE interesting solutions and says they don't have to be engineering — so the model deliberately spans product/engineering, nature/product, and policy. Variety of solution TYPE is what makes this slide section score well, not three flavors of the same idea.

Solutions · 1 of 3

Solution 1 · Engineering

Pump it, raise it, wall it

What it is:

  • Miami Beach's stormwater program: ≈$650M spent so far
  • 70+ pumps push floodwater back over the seawalls
  • Roads lifted, seawalls raised block by block

The honest trade-off:

  • Works today — but it's expensive to build and forever to maintain
  • Pumps move water, they don't remove it
Every solution slide follows the same 2-column shape: what it is + how it works on the left, honest trade-off on the right. The trade-off column is the difference between Basic and Advanced on the rubric — it shows the team evaluated the idea, not just summarized it.

Solutions · 2 of 3

Solution 2 · Product + Nature

Let the shoreline do the work

What it is:

  • Living shorelines: mangroves and oyster reefs that soak up wave energy
  • Permeable pavement: parking lots that swallow rain instead of shedding it
  • Deployable in small pieces — one lot, one canal at a time

The honest trade-off:

  • Far cheaper per piece, and it rebuilds habitat
  • Grows slowly — no match for a king tide on its own
This is the "product" lane the assignment invites: a deployable technology plus a natural system. The pairing with Solution 1 is deliberate — pumps defend hard and fast, nature defends soft and slow. Real designs use both; saying so demonstrates systems thinking.

Solutions · 3 of 3

Solution 3 · Policy

Vote the money into place

What it is:

  • Miami Forever Bond: $400M approved by Miami voters in 2017
  • Roughly half earmarked for sea-level-rise and storm resilience
  • Updated codes now require higher seawalls for new and rebuilt waterfront

The honest trade-off:

  • It's the only solution that scales city-wide — and the only one you can vote on
  • Bonds are borrowed money; deciding who pays and who gets protected first is political
The policy lane completes the assignment's "products, policy, etc." span. Note the verbs students can imitate: a machine (Solution 1), an ecosystem (Solution 2), a law (Solution 3) — three different levers on one problem.

Impacts · 3:45 – 4:30

The hard question isn't engineering

If we act:

  • Homes, roads, and the aquifer stay usable for the next generation
  • Resilience jobs and protected property values

If we don't:

  • Insurers pull out first; then the tax base follows
  • Our generation pays for the delays
The ethical wrinkle

The safest high ground is often the least wealthy — Little Haiti sits on some of Miami's highest land. If solutions raise land values, do we protect the shoreline and price people out of their own neighborhood? Who gets protected first?

Two rubric targets in one slide: "predict the impact on future generations with evidence" and the 1.1.5 requirement to consider ethical implications. Naming the unobvious impact (climate gentrification) is exactly the Advanced descriptor on the EES master rubric's D7 Systems Thinking domain.

Close · 4:30 – 5:00

The water doesn't vote. We do.

Every solution you saw today exists because someone chose to build it: an engineer designing a pump, a biologist planting mangroves, a voter checking a box in 2017.

In 20 years, Miami will be either the city that adapted first — or the case study other cities study. The difference is the people in this room.

This is the moment to slow down, look at the audience, and land the last line.

The rubric's Advanced closing: "powerful closing inspires the audience." Model the delivery move too — final sentence from memory, eye contact, pause after the last word. Have students write THEIR last sentence first and rehearse it more than any other line.
