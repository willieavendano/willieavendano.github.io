PLTW Project 1.3.7 asks you to design a 3D-printed protective case for a pair of earbuds. For your midterm, the device is your phone. Your midterm is a final presentation on Wednesday, December 16. Bring the printed case and the phone it was designed for, and show that one fits the other.

Read this first

The Design Brief and the Project Checklist are the framework for this midterm. They determine what goes in your presentation and what the rubric scores.

Each section of the checklist (Define → Generate → Develop → Construct & Test → Evaluate) becomes a section of your slides, and each rubric criterion matches one of those sections. If your design brief and checklist are complete and signed off, your presentation already has an outline.

The Challenge

Phones cost hundreds of dollars and live in pockets, backpacks and the bottom of bags. A cracked screen, scratched camera lens or bent charging port is the most common way a phone gets damaged. Use the design process to design a protective case for one real phone. Model it in Fusion and 3D-print it. Then show proof of concept: evidence that your design works and meets the criteria in your design brief.

  • Solo project: you design, model, print and present your own case. The target phone is usually your own, and it must be in class on presentation day. If a classmate has the same phone, your sketches, models and data must still be your own.
  • Two components: the case has two parts that join together (hinge, snap or slide). You model both components in Fusion, and the two parts must fit together.
  • Official resources: use the PLTW 1.3.7 activity in myPLTW for the competitor multiview drawings, Decision Matrix, Decision Matrix Rubric and 3D Printing Process resources. This packet adapts that project to a phone and to our calendar.
The real test: does it ACTUALLY fit the device? On December 16 you will put the phone into your case live, in front of the class, against a 7-second timer. A good-looking case that the phone can't go into does not count as a working solution. If your first print doesn't fit, that's engineering. Measure exactly why, fix the model, and reprint before the deadline. Before Dec 16, also record a short fit video of the phone going in: it goes on your slides, and it's your backup if the live demo goes wrong. If you still can't get a fit, present the evidence: what you measured, what you changed and what you would change next. That earns real credit. Pretending it fits earns none.

  1. Two components joined by a mechanism (hinge, snap or slide). You model both components.

  5. Fits: each interior dimension equals the measured phone dimension plus your stated clearance. The phone doesn't rattle and doesn't need force to go in.

Tolerance Worksheet

A 3D printer does not print exactly what you model. Plastic shrinks, the nozzle squishes the first layers, and holes come out small. Your case only fits if you measure the phone, design in clearance on purpose, and check the print against the model.

How to use it. A: measure each phone feature three times with calipers and record the average (mm). B: choose a clearance per side; 0.3–0.5 mm is a typical starting point for PLA. C: CAD interior = A + 2×B for widths and lengths (one clearance on each side). D: after printing, measure the same feature on the print. Error = D − C. Explain on a slide why the print differs from the model.

Save plastic with a fit ring. Before printing the full case, print just a 4–5 mm tall slice of the case wall (about 20 minutes) and test it on the phone. If the ring fits, the case will fit. This is Occam's Razor applied to testing.

Why two components? PLA is stiff and doesn't stretch. A one-piece rigid shell that wraps over the screen edge usually can't get the phone in at all. A second component that hinges, snaps or slides on is how your design gets the phone in and keeps it in. Explain your joint in your presentation.

Bonus · the iPhone Duo challenge

Instead of your own phone, design the case for Mr. Avendano's future iPhone Duo, Apple's first foldable iPhone. If you choose this, your client is Mr. Avendano and the bonus is +0.5 on your midterm score.

  • The hinge criterion becomes the whole problem. The Duo folds like a book, so your two components are the two halves of the case. They must hinge with the phone and allow it to open flat and close fully.
  • You can't put calipers on a phone you don't have. Work from Apple's published dimensions. Find them yourself on apple.com, cite the page, and check them against Apple's Accessory Design Guidelines. Reported figures are about 117.8 × 84.1 × 11.3 mm folded and 117.8 × 164.6 × 5.2 mm open. Sources disagree on the open length, so confirm every number.
  • Proof of fit: build or print a full-size dummy of the Duo from the published dimensions and fit-test your case on it, folded and open. Your tolerance worksheet column A becomes “published dimension” and needs a source.
  • Talk to Mr. Avendano by Wed, Dec 2 if you're taking this on. If his Duo has arrived by December 16, your case also gets a live fit test on the real device.

Midterm Rubric

Each criterion is scored on the house 4-level scale: 4 Advanced · 3 Proficient (the expected standard) · 2 Developing · 1 Beginning. Half levels are allowed. Your midterm score is the average of the seven criteria, to one decimal, plus any bonus. The criteria follow the checklist and are adapted from the PLTW 1.3.7 rubric (domains D2 Design Process, D9 Engineering Design, D13 Modeling, D8 Communication, D1 Engineering Mindset).
