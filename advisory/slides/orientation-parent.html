How to use your advisor

Me first, for almost everything

  • Email me first for anything non-academic. Schedules, social stuff, a rough week, a question you do not know who to ask. I route it and follow up.
  • Email the teacher first for a grade. Copy me. If it is not getting solved in a week, I step in.
  • Tell me what I cannot see. Sleep, a move, a loss, a new sibling. Context changes how I read a bad Tuesday.
  • Expect a reply within 24 hours. School days. Friday emails get a Monday reply.
The routing rule is the most useful thing parents take home. Non-academic → advisor. Grade → teacher, copy the advisor.

Tell parents to use the same four words at home. It works because it is low-stakes: a one-word answer is easy to give and hard to hide behind.

Executive function · practised here daily

The four moves

  • Write it down somewhere that isn't Veracross. A planner or a notebook. The act of writing the deadline is what makes you remember it — not the app that already knows.
  • Notes are taken, not received. Photographing the board is not notetaking. Rewriting an idea in your own words is where it sticks.
  • Start the night it is assigned. Ten minutes on the day it is given beats an hour the night before, every single time.
  • Ask before it is due, not after. See me BEFORE the deadline if something has gone wrong. I can help then. After, I can only apply the policy.
Same four moves as every course deck — on purpose. Advisory is where they get rehearsed: planner checks in the morning, start-tonight reminders on Wednesdays.

How to help at home

Four things that work

  • Ask the weather question. Sunny, cloudy, storm, hurricane? It is the same check-in we do at 8:00, and it gets more than 'fine'.
  • Protect sleep like a grade. Ninth graders who sleep eight hours do better in every class I teach. Phones out of the bedroom.
  • Let the planner be theirs. Ask to see it; do not fill it in. The habit only sticks if they own it.
  • Let a small thing go wrong. A forgotten charger in September is the cheapest lesson they will ever get.
End on the charger line — parents laugh, and it is the actual advice.

Honest boundaries

What an advisor is not

  • Not a counselor or therapist. When something is bigger than a bad week, I walk your student to the counselor myself — and I tell you.
  • Not the grade office. I can see grades and I will flag patterns, but the teacher owns the gradebook.
  • Not a substitute for the handbook. Attendance and discipline live with the dean. I am usually in the room for the conversation.
Say this plainly. Parents trust the role more when its edges are clear.
