Where We Are

From a chance process to a model

Last week we built probability rules and random-variable language. This week we turn repeated trials into two powerful models:

  • Binomial — count successes in a fixed number of trials.
  • Normal — describe a continuous, mound-shaped distribution.
  • State conditions before calculating.
  • Use technology to calculate, then explain in context.

A calculator can produce a probability. It cannot choose the model for you.

The key transition is model selection. Students often recognize a familiar formula and calculate immediately; today require a conditions check first. Emphasize that the binomial model is discrete because X counts successes, while the normal model is continuous because it describes measurements along a number line. This distinction will matter when we use z-scores next.

Act I · The Binomial Model

Does this count as binomial?

Four conditions. Every time.

Make the four conditions a gate, not a decorative acronym. Students should be able to defend the model before they touch a binomial probability command. The most common failures are treating changing probabilities as constant and ignoring dependence when sampling without replacement.

Binomial Model · 1 of 4

Four conditions, one count

B
Binary outcomes
Each trial has two categories for the question: success or failure.
I
Independent trials
The result of one trial does not change the probability on another.
N
Number fixed
There are a fixed number n of trials before the process begins.
S
Same probability
The probability of success p is constant from trial to trial.

BINS: Binary, Independent, Number fixed, Same probability.

Binomial Model · 2 of 4

Independence is a design claim

Ask what happens to the probability after one trial:

Usually independent

Spin a fair spinner 30 times. The first result does not change the next spin.

Check carefully

Sample 10 students without replacement from a class of 24. The composition changes after each draw.

When sampling without replacement, the 10% condition can justify treating trials as approximately independent: sample size ≤ 10% of the population.

The 10% condition is a practical approximation, not a magic switch that makes draws truly independent. Ask students to name the population before they apply it. A frequent failure mode is saying “without replacement is independent because the population is large” without checking the sample-to-population comparison. Make them articulate what stays approximately constant.

Binomial Model · 3 of 4

Name the random variable before the formula

A complete model statement

Let X = the number of free throws made in 12 attempts by a player whose probability of making each attempt is 0.75.

X ∼ Binomial(n = 12, p = 0.75)

  • X counts successes, not trials.
  • n is the number of trials; p is the success probability.
  • Write the context first; the notation is a compressed sentence.

Binomial Model · 4 of 4

The binomial probability formula

For exactly x successes in n independent trials:

P(X = x) = C(n, x)px(1 − p)n − x

  • C(n, x) counts where the successes can occur.
  • px gives the success part.
  • (1 − p)n − x gives the failure part.
  • Use ≤ or ≥ for cumulative events.
Students often remember the formula as a string of symbols and lose the meaning of each factor. Have them say “arrangements, successes, failures” as they point to the three pieces. In practice, a calculator or R/Python is safer for the arithmetic, but the formula is still the model evidence.

Worked Example · Setup

Free throws: one probability question

A player makes a free throw with probability 0.75. Assume attempts are independent. In 12 attempts, what is the probability of making exactly 9?

First move

Define X = number of made free throws. Then X ∼ Binomial(12, 0.75).

Before calculating: identify B, I, N, and S.

Worked Example · KDEA

P(X = 9), in context

StepWhat you write
Kn = 12; p = 0.75; independent attempts; X = made free throws
DP(X = 9), the probability of exactly 9 makes
EX ∼ Binomial(12, 0.75); P(X = x) = C(n, x)px(1 − p)n − x
AP(X = 9) = C(12, 9)(0.75)9(0.25)3 ≈ 0.259. There is an approximately 25.9% chance the player makes exactly 9 of 12 attempts.

A probability answer needs a sentence that names the event.

Model the statistical version of KDEA explicitly: K is data/design/conditions, D is the target probability, E is the named procedure and formula, and A is compute plus conclude in context. Students may write 0.259 and stop. Require the event and the population of trials in the final sentence. The displayed decimal is rounded, so use approximately, not an equals sign.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 8 minutes

Translate the wording

With a partner

A quality-control sensor correctly flags a defective item with probability 0.90. It checks 15 independent items.

  1. Define X in context and write the binomial model.
  2. Write calculator-ready notation for “at least 13 correctly flagged.”
  3. Write a one-sentence interpretation of the probability you would calculate.

Words first: “at least” tells you the direction of the inequality.

Do not reveal a numeric answer on this practice slide. Listen for “at least 13” becoming 13 through 15, not exactly 13. Also watch whether students define X as defective items instead of correctly flagged items; that changes p and reverses the event. Ask one pair to read its interpretation aloud.

Binomial Distribution · Center & Spread

Mean and SD come from the model

μ
Mean
\( \mu_X = np \)
Expected number of successes in n trials.
σ
Standard deviation
\( \sigma_X = \sqrt{np(1-p)} \)
Typical distance from the mean, in successes.

The mean is not necessarily an attainable outcome. It is a long-run center.

Separate “mean” from “most likely value.” For a count, the mean can be a decimal even though X is integer-valued. The units here are successes or attempts, not a probability. Students commonly use np for the SD or forget the failure probability 1-p; have them say the full square-root expression.

Worked Example · KDEA

What should 12 free throws look like?

StepWhat you write
Kn = 12; p = 0.75; 1 − p = 0.25
Dmean and SD of X, the number of makes
E\( \mu_X = np \); \( \sigma_X = \sqrt{np(1-p)} \)
AμX = 12(0.75) = 9
σX = √(12(0.75)(0.25)) = √2.25 = 1.50
A typical 12-attempt set is centered at 9 makes, with a spread of 1.50 makes.

The center is 9 makes; the spread is measured in makes.

Interpretation

SD is a sentence, not just a square root

For X = makes in 12 attempts:

Context template

In repeated sets of 12 free throws under the same conditions, the number made typically differs from its mean of 9 by about 1.50 makes.

  • Do not say “the SD is 1.50%.”
  • Do not say every result is exactly 1.50 from the mean.
  • Do say what is varying and what unit the spread uses.

Act II · The Normal Distribution

A smooth model for variation

Center, spread, and standardized location.

This is the normal distribution’s first appearance in the course. Keep the claim modest: it is a useful model for some quantitative distributions, not a universal shape. Today students need the empirical rule and z-scores; detailed probability calculations continue in the next meeting.

Normal Distribution · 1 of 3

The 68–95–99.7 rule

68%
Within 1 SD
Approximately 68% of observations lie between μ − σ and μ + σ.
95%
Within 2 SD
Approximately 95% lie between μ − 2σ and μ + 2σ.
99.7%
Within 3 SD
Approximately 99.7% lie between μ − 3σ and μ + 3σ.

The rule applies to a normal-shaped distribution, not to every dataset.

Have students draw the center and three tick marks before memorizing percentages. The common error is assigning 68% to one side of the mean; 68% is the full interval from one SD below to one SD above. Also state the condition: the empirical rule is about a normal distribution or a graph that is reasonably normal-shaped.

Normal Distribution · 2 of 3

A z-score is a location in SD units

z = (x − μ) / σ

  • z = 0 means the observation is at the mean.
  • Positive z means above the mean; negative z means below.
  • |z| tells how many SDs away from the mean.

Standardizing changes the scale, not the relative location.

Worked Example · KDEA

One score, standardized

Suppose quiz scores are approximately normal with mean 72 and SD 8. A score of 84 is how unusual relative to the distribution?

StepWhat you write
Kμ = 72 points; σ = 8 points; x = 84 points
Dstandardized location of 84 points
Ez = (x − μ) / σ
Az = (84 − 72) / 8 = 1.5. The score is 1.5 SDs above the mean.
This is the first place students see why z-scores are useful: the raw unit is points, but the standardized answer compares location across distributions. Watch for subtracting in the wrong order, which flips the sign. Keep the interpretation tied to the score and the distribution; “z is 1.5” alone is not a conclusion.

Practice · Individual → Pair · 6 minutes

Which observation is farther from center?

Try it without a calculator first

Distribution A has μ = 40 and σ = 5. Distribution B has μ = 200 and σ = 20.

  1. Find the z-score for x = 50 in A.
  2. Find the z-score for x = 240 in B.
  3. Which observation is farther above its distribution’s mean? Explain in SD units.

Compare z-scores, not the raw values.

Do not put the answers on the student slide. The instructional target is comparison across unlike scales. Circulate for the subtraction and denominator first, then ask students to explain why the larger raw value is not automatically the more unusual observation. This is a short Tuesday-sized activity; stop after the comparison and defer normal-area technology to Thursday.
