Where We Are

Last week: the sampling distribution. This week: the real thing.

You already know the shape, center, and spread of x̄₁ − x̄₂, and you know the conditions get checked for both groups. This week turns that into an actual interval, and into the single most important judgment call in two-sample work: are these two groups paired, or independent?

Get the paired-vs-independent call wrong, and every step after it is wrong too.

Frame the stakes accurately: unlike a units error or an algebra slip, misidentifying paired vs. independent design isn't a small deduction — it changes which formula, which parameter, and which degrees of freedom apply. This is why 4.8 gets its own extended block today instead of a single slide.

4.7

Confidence Interval for a Difference in Means

Same KDEA. Two groups instead of one.

4.7 · The Formula

\( (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t^* \cdot \sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2} \)

Same KDEA mapping as every interval this unit — the only change is what goes in D and E:

StepWhat changes for two samples
Kdata and conditions for BOTH groups — Random, Independent (10% each, and independent of each other), Normal/Large Sample (each group)
Dμ₁ − μ₂, the true difference between the two population means
Etwo-sample t-interval; df from technology (Welch)
Acompute AND conclude in context — state which group the interval favors, if either
This is a genuinely light lift conceptually if last week's sampling-distribution content landed — the formula is just last week's spread formula with a t* multiplier bolted on, structurally identical to how last week's one-sample interval used last-last-week's spread formula.

Worked Example · Setup

Study hours: Stats vs. Physics

A researcher randomly selects n₁ = 32 students from all AP Statistics sections and, separately, n₂ = 30 students from all AP Physics sections, and asks each student their average weekly study hours. Stats: x̄₁ = 6.4 hr, s₁ = 1.8 hr. Physics: x̄₂ = 7.1 hr, s₂ = 2.0 hr.

Build a 95% confidence interval for μ₁ − μ₂ (Stats − Physics).

Two separate, unrelated groups of students — worth pointing out explicitly that this is the independent case, since the paired-vs-independent framing is coming up in a few slides and this example is the "independent" anchor students will refer back to.

Worked Example · KDEA

95% CI for μ₁ − μ₂

StepWhat you write
Kn₁=32, x̄₁=6.4, s₁=1.8; n₂=30, x̄₂=7.1, s₂=2.0. Random: both randomly selected. Independent: both groups <10% of their populations, and the two groups share no students. Normal/Large Sample: both n ≥ 30.
Dμ₁ − μ₂ = true difference in mean weekly study hours (Stats − Physics)
Etwo-sample t-interval, df ≈ 59.7 (technology, Welch)
ASE = √(1.8²/32 + 2.0²/30) ≈ 0.484
t* ≈ 2.000 (df ≈ 60, 95% confidence)
(6.4 − 7.1) ± 2.000(0.484) = −0.7 ± 0.97
CI: (−1.67, 0.27)

We are 95% confident the true difference in mean weekly study hours is between −1.67 and 0.27 hours.

StatKey's bootstrap "CI for Difference in Means" tool builds this same interval by resampling instead of the t-distribution — worth a quick mention as a way to sanity-check the shape assumption when conditions feel shaky, without replacing the by-hand t-procedure on assessments.

4.7 · Zero Is the Benchmark

Does the interval contain zero?

(−1.67, 0.27) spans zero — plausible values of μ₁ − μ₂ include zero.

The move

Zero inside the interval means "no difference" is a plausible value — we do NOT have convincing statistical evidence that mean study hours differ between the two courses.

If the interval were entirely above or entirely below zero, that WOULD be convincing evidence of a real difference.

This zero-benchmark move is the two-sample version of last week's "does the interval contain the claimed value" logic — same idea, the claimed value for a difference is always zero (no difference). Flag the callback explicitly; it reduces this to a pattern students already trust.

4.8

Paired vs. Two-Sample

The one design question that decides everything else.

4.8 · Paired Data

Paired: each observation has a natural match

Data is paired when every observation in one group is naturally linked to exactly one observation in the other — usually the same subject, measured twice, or matched units.

Same subject twice
Resting heart rate before and after a training program — each person contributes one "before" and one "after."
Matched pairs
Twins, siblings, or units matched on a lurking variable, then one of each pair assigned to each condition.

The fix: compute the differences, then run a ONE-sample t-procedure on those differences.

The "compute the differences, then it's a one-sample problem" move is the entire trick of paired data — it converts a two-variable problem back into last week's one-sample machinery. Students who internalize this stop being intimidated by paired problems; they just see one extra step (subtract) before a procedure they already know cold.

4.8 · Side by Side

Paired vs. independent two-sample

FeaturePairedIndependent Two-Sample
Designsame subjects/matched units, measured twicedifferent, unrelated subjects in each group
Parameterμd — mean of the differencesμ₁ − μ₂
What you computeONE list: di = x₁i − x₂iTWO lists: x̄₁, s₁ and x̄₂, s₂
Procedureone-sample t on the differences, df = npairs − 1two-sample t, df from technology

Same subjects appearing in both columns of your data table — that's your signal to pair.

"Same subjects appearing in both columns" is the fastest diagnostic to teach — if a student can point to one row of raw data and see the same name or ID twice, it's paired. This is more reliable than trying to reason abstractly about "relatedness."

Quick Check · Turn & Talk · 6 minutes

Paired, or independent?

With your table — classify each, and say why
  1. 18 runners' 5K times, measured before and after 6 weeks of training.
  2. Reading scores from 25 randomly chosen 3rd graders and a different 22 randomly chosen 5th graders.
  3. 20 pairs of shoes, each pair tested with two different sole materials (one material per shoe in the pair).
Answers: (1) paired — same 18 runners, two measurements each. (2) independent — different, unrelated students in each group. (3) paired — each pair of shoes is a matched unit, even though the exact subject (foot) differs; matching on the pair is what makes it paired, not literal repetition on the same object. Scenario 3 is the one groups get wrong most often — spend extra time there.

Setting Up Tests for Two Means

Hypotheses and procedure only — carrying them out is next week.

4.9 · Paired Setup

Setting up a paired test

A coach wants to know if a 6-week training program changes runners' 5K times. This is the paired scenario from the quick check — here's the setup, stopping before computation:

StepWhat you write
Dμd = true mean difference in 5K time (before − after). H₀: μd = 0, Ha: μd > 0 (expecting times to drop)
Eone-sample t-test on the differences, df = 17 (18 pairs − 1)

4.9 · Two-Sample Setup

Setting up a two-sample test

A teacher wants to know if two different review methods produce different quiz scores between two unrelated groups of students — independent, not paired:

StepWhat you write
Dμ₁ − μ₂ = true difference in mean quiz score. H₀: μ₁ − μ₂ = 0, Ha: μ₁ − μ₂ ≠ 0
Etwo-sample t-test, df from technology (Welch)

Same D-and-E structure as the paired setup — only the parameter and the procedure change.

Put this side by side with the previous slide mentally for students — the parallel structure (D names the parameter and hypotheses, E names the procedure and df) is identical across paired and independent designs; only the specific parameter symbol and df source differ.

Common Traps

This week's headline error

Paired vs. two-sample misidentification

Running a two-sample procedure on paired data (or vice versa) doesn't just cost a few points — it's the wrong parameter, the wrong standard error, and the wrong df, all at once. Before writing E, always ask: does the same subject or matched unit appear in both columns?

If yes — paired, one-sample t on differences. If no — independent, two-sample t.

This is the single highest-stakes identification error in Unit 4 — worse than a z-vs-t slip, because it invalidates the entire procedure rather than just widening or narrowing an interval. Make the diagnostic question ("same subject in both columns?") something students can recite.
