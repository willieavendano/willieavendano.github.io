Why This Matters

You will never measure an entire population

You can't survey every Cushman student, every voter, every widget off an assembly line. What you can do is take a well-designed random sample and use it to make a trustworthy statement about the population it came from.

  • That statement always comes with uncertainty — inference is honest about how much
  • Unit 3 is entirely about proportions: percentages, categorical outcomes, yes/no data

Today: what a sample proportion can and can't tell you, before we build anything with it.

Estimators · 1 of 3

Two kinds of numbers: parameter and statistic

QuantityPopulation (parameter)Sample (statistic)
Proportionpp̂ (“p-hat”)
Meanμx̄ (“x-bar”)
Sample sizeN (population)n (sample)

A parameter is a fixed, usually unknown, number describing the whole population. A statistic is computed from a sample — it's what we actually get to see, and it changes from sample to sample.

This table is the whole unit's notation contract — refer back to it constantly. Emphasize: p is a fixed number (even though we don't know it), while p̂ is a random variable — it takes a different value every time you draw a new sample. That distinction is the seed of everything in 3.2.

Estimators · 2 of 3

A point estimate is a single best guess

p̂ is a point estimate of p — one number, computed from your sample, that stands in for the unknown population proportion.

Example

You survey a random sample of n = 200 students; 170 say they eat lunch in the cafeteria. p̂ = 170/200 = 0.85 is your point estimate of the true proportion of all students who eat in the cafeteria.

One number. No sense yet of how far off it might be — that's coming.

Estimators · 3 of 3

Different samples give different p̂ — and that's not an error

If you took a different random sample of 200 students, you would almost certainly get a different p̂. Maybe 0.83, maybe 0.88. This bouncing around is called sampling variability.

  • It is not a mistake, not bad measurement, not sloppy work
  • It's an unavoidable consequence of randomness — and it's predictable randomness

The goal of this week: describe exactly how p̂ bounces around, before we use it to infer anything.

Common misconception to head off now: students often think sampling variability means "someone did the sampling wrong." Push back hard — a perfectly executed random sample still produces a different p̂ every time, purely from chance. That predictability (not the individual bounce) is what makes inference possible at all.

The Sampling Distribution

What if you took every possible sample?

Imagine repeating your sample of 200 over and over, forever, and plotting every p̂ you got.

This is the conceptual hinge of the whole unit. A sampling distribution is a distribution of a statistic (not of individual data values) — students conflate the two constantly. Be explicit: we are not plotting 200 students' individual answers, we are plotting thousands of p̂ values, each one itself a summary of a full sample of 200. It's a distribution of distributions, one level up.

Sampling Distributions · 1 of 4

The sampling distribution of p̂

The sampling distribution of a statistic is the distribution of values that statistic would take across every possible sample of a given size, drawn the same way.

Key idea

It's a mathematical model, not something you actually build by hand — but it behaves in a predictable, describable way: it has a shape, a center, and a spread, exactly like any distribution you described back in Unit 1.

Sampling Distributions · 2 of 4

Simulate it before you trust the formula

Tool · StatKey

Before the formulas, watch it happen: StatKey (lock5stat.com) lets you draw thousands of random samples from a population with a known p, and builds the sampling distribution of p̂ live in front of you.

  • Set the population proportion, set n, hit “Generate 1000 Samples”
  • Watch the histogram of p̂ values pile up into a bell shape, centered near the true p
If a computer lab or demo screen is available, run this live — it lands far better simulated than described. The takeaway for students: the bell shape and the centering on p aren't a coincidence, they're what the next few slides prove algebraically. StatKey also previews the bootstrap tools we'll revisit conceptually when discussing confidence intervals next week.

Sampling Distributions · 3 of 4

Two ways an estimator can go wrong

Δ
Bias
Is the sampling distribution centered on the true parameter? An unbiased estimator's average value, over many samples, equals the parameter.
σ
Variability
How spread out are the p̂ values from sample to sample? Lower spread means a more precise estimator.

We want both: centered correctly (unbiased) AND tightly clustered (low variability). p̂ delivers the first for free; n controls the second.

The archery-target metaphor works well here if you want a visual aid on the whiteboard: bias is missing the bullseye on average; variability is how scattered the arrows are around wherever they're centered. p̂ is always centered on the bullseye (unbiased) — the only thing we control is grouping the arrows tighter, which is exactly what increasing n does.

Sampling Distributions · 4 of 4

p̂ is an unbiased estimator of p

Mean of the sampling distribution

mean of p̂ = p

Averaged over every possible sample, p̂ lands exactly on the true population proportion. No systematic pull too high or too low — this holds as long as the sample is random.

This is why we're allowed to use a single p̂ as a stand-in for the unknown p.

The Spread · 1 of 2

How much does p̂ bounce around?

Standard deviation of the sampling distribution of p̂

SD(p̂) = √[ p(1−p) / n ]

This formula uses the true population proportion p — it describes the theoretical spread before you've collected any data.

  • Bigger n → smaller SD(p̂) — larger samples bounce around less
  • The relationship isn't linear: quadrupling n only halves the SD (it's a square root)
The √n relationship is worth pausing on — students often assume doubling sample size halves the spread. It doesn't; you need to quadruple n to cut SD(p̂) in half. This becomes very relevant later when we discuss margin of error and sample-size planning for confidence intervals.

The Spread · 2 of 2

When is the shape approximately normal?

The sampling distribution of p̂ is approximately normal when the Large Counts condition is met:

Large Counts condition

np ≥ 10  and  n(1−p) ≥ 10

Both the expected number of “successes” and the expected number of “failures” have to be at least 10. Skip too small on either side and the sampling distribution gets skewed — the normal approximation breaks down.

This condition will reappear, unchanged, on every KDEA table for the rest of Unit 3.

Flag this explicitly as one of the three recurring conditions (Random, 10%, Large Counts) that will anchor every single procedure through chi-square. Students who memorize this condition now save themselves from re-deriving it in week 3 under test-day pressure. The most common failure mode later in the unit is skipping condition-checking altogether — start the habit today.

Same Protocol, New Data

KDEA still works. The letters just mean something new.

The four-step protocol from Physics-style problem solving is the same house move here — K, D, E, A, never renamed, never reordered.

If students have taken a Cushman course that uses KDEA before, lean on that transfer explicitly — this is the exact same discipline, just applied to inference instead of dimensional analysis. If this is their first exposure, frame it as "the way to solve ANY problem," same as it's taught everywhere else in the building.

KDEA for Inference

What each letter means for a proportions problem

StepWhat you write, for inference
Kthe data AND the conditions, checked explicitly: Random, 10%, Large Counts
Dthe parameter or claim — in words, in context, with correct notation
Ethe procedure — which formula (interval or test), written out
Acompute AND conclude — the arithmetic, then a sentence that answers the question, in context

A correct number with no conditions checked and no context sentence is an incomplete KDEA — every time.

Worked Example · KDEA

Describe the sampling distribution of p̂

District records show 85% of all Cushman alumni would recommend the school (p = 0.85). We plan to survey a random sample of n = 200 alumni.

StepWhat you write
Kp = 0.85, n = 200, random sample. Random ✓ (stated); 10%: 200 alumni is far less than 10% of all alumni ✓; Large Counts: np = 170 ≥ 10, n(1−p) = 30 ≥ 10 ✓
Dthe shape, center, and spread of the sampling distribution of p̂ for samples of size 200
Emean of p̂ = p; SD(p̂) = √[p(1−p)/n]; shape is approximately normal since Large Counts is met
Amean = 0.85; SD = √(0.85 × 0.15 / 200) ≈ 0.0253. The sampling distribution of p̂ is approximately normal, centered at 0.85, with standard deviation ≈ 0.0253.
Walk the conditions row slowly — this is the first time students write all three conditions in one place, and the habit needs to stick before intervals and tests raise the stakes. Point out the A row has two parts: the numbers (mean, SD) AND a full descriptive sentence — a bare "0.0253" with no sentence is not a complete conclusion, same standard as physics's "units travel with the math."

Practice · Turn & Talk · 10 minutes

Your turn: check the conditions and describe p̂

With your partner

A manufacturer claims 92% of widgets pass inspection (p = 0.92). For random samples of n = 150 widgets:

  1. Is the Large Counts condition met? Show the arithmetic.
  2. What is the mean of the sampling distribution of p̂?
  3. What is the standard deviation? Round sensibly, and use ≈.
Answers: np = 138 ≥ 10, n(1−p) = 12 ≥ 10, condition met. Mean = 0.92. SD = √(0.92×0.08/150) ≈ √(0.00049) ≈ 0.0221. Watch for students who compute n(1−p) as n − p instead of n×(1−p) — a very common arithmetic slip the first time they see this formula.

Early Traps

Three habits to build now, not in week 4

p
p vs p̂
p is the fixed, usually unknown population value. p̂ changes every sample. Mixing them up breaks every formula downstream.
√
Skipping conditions
Large Counts isn't optional decoration — without it, “approximately normal” is a claim you haven't earned.
n
Linear thinking
Doubling n does not halve SD(p̂) — it's a square root relationship.
These three are the errors that resurface most often once intervals and tests raise the stakes in weeks 2–4. Naming them now, while the material is still low-pressure, is cheaper than fixing them under test conditions later.
