Two Categorical Variables

Week 1

Two-way tables · joint, marginal & conditional frequencies · simulation

This is the first week of Unit 2, so make the bridge from Unit 1 explicit: students already know how to describe one variable; now we ask how two categorical variables behave together. The week has a short Monday, a 72-minute Tuesday, and a full Thursday. Preview that tables are the language and simulation is the evidence engine.

Unit 2 · 1 of 6

One variable describes. Two variables compare.

A single categorical variable answers what is common? Two categorical variables let us ask:

1
Who is in each group?
Counts and percentages for combinations of categories.
2
How do groups differ?
Compare conditional relative frequencies using a shared denominator.
3
Could chance explain it?
Use simulation to estimate how unusual an observed pattern is.

Today: read the table, name the denominator, model chance.

Students often jump straight to a percentage before saying what comparison they want. Keep asking, “Compared with what?” and “Out of which group?” The shared denominator is the conceptual hinge of the entire week; students who skip it will report a mathematically valid number that answers the wrong question.

Act I · Read the table

Every percentage has a denominator.

Two-way tables keep the denominator visible.

Pause here and make the phrase “denominator visible” a class refrain. The table is not just a storage device for counts; it is a map of which population each percentage describes.

Two-way tables · 1 of 4

A two-way table counts two categorical variables together

Suppose 100 students report whether they use a study plan and whether they passed a quiz:

Study planPassedDid not passRow total
Yes36440
No303060
Column total6634100

Joint cell: 36 students both used a study plan and passed.

Marginal total: 40 students used a study plan, regardless of result.

Name the variables and their roles: study plan is the explanatory variable for this story, quiz result is the response variable. Do not imply causation from this observational table. The most common student failure is calling every interior cell a “marginal”; reserve marginal for totals on the edges.

Two-way tables · 2 of 4

Three frequencies, three questions

36
Joint frequency
A cell count: used a study plan and passed.
40
Marginal frequency
A row or column total: used a study plan, regardless of result.
66
Marginal frequency
A column total: passed, regardless of study-plan status.

The word and points to a joint cell. The word or points toward a total.

Translate the sentence before touching the calculator.

Two-way tables · 3 of 4

Joint relative frequency: out of everyone

Divide a cell by the grand total when the question describes the entire group.

Worked example

What proportion of all 100 students both used a study plan and passed?

StepWhat you write
K36 students are in the joint cell; 100 students are in the sample.
DJoint relative frequency, in proportion of all students.
Ejoint relative frequency = joint frequency ÷ grand total
A36 ÷ 100 = 0.36, so 36% of these students both used a study plan and passed.
Model the context sentence at the end. “0.36” is not the final communication; “36% of these students…” is. A frequent error is dividing 36 by 40 because students notice the row first. Ask them to underline “all 100 students” in the prompt before selecting the denominator.

Two-way tables · 4 of 4

Marginal relative frequency: out of a row or column

Row marginal

Proportion who used a study plan:

40 ÷ 100 = 0.40 → 40%

Column marginal

Proportion who passed:

66 ÷ 100 = 0.66 → 66%

Marginal = one variable at a time. Joint = both variables together.

Conditional relative frequency · Worked example

Conditional means “within a named group”

What proportion of students who used a study plan passed?

StepWhat you write
K36 plan-users passed; 40 plan-users are in the row.
DPass proportion among students who used a study plan.
Econditional relative frequency = target cell ÷ conditioning-group total
A36 ÷ 40 = 0.90, so 90% of students who used a study plan passed the quiz.

The denominator is the group named after “among” or “given.”

This is the first deliberate K-D-E-A mapping for statistics: K is data/design/conditions, D is the parameter, probability, or claim sought, E is the procedure or formula, and A is compute plus conclude in context. Emphasize that a context-free answer is like an answer without units.

Compare · Turn & Talk · 7 minutes

Reverse the condition, change the denominator

90%
Among plan-users
36 ÷ 40: passed, given study plan.
54.5%
Among passers
36 ÷ 66 ≈ 0.545: used a study plan, given passed.
With a partner

Explain in one sentence why these are different questions. Then identify the denominator for: “Among students who did not pass, what proportion used a study plan?”

Do not let students turn this into a memorized “row versus column” rule. The language controls the denominator, and the orientation of the table is arbitrary. Listen for the correct distinction: the first percentage starts with plan-users; the second starts with passers.

Act II · Model chance

A table describes the sample. Simulation asks, “Could chance do this?”

Keep the observed pattern fixed. Randomize the part chance could change.

This is the transition from description to inference-like reasoning, without jumping ahead to formal probability rules. Students should understand that a simulation is not “making up data”; it is a model of a chance process under a stated assumption.

Simulation · 1 of 5

Probability can be estimated by repetition

1
Define success
State the outcome you will count in one repetition.
2
Repeat fairly
Use a chance device or software so each repetition follows the model.
3
Count the proportion
successes ÷ repetitions estimates the probability.

More repetitions usually make the estimate stabilize, but a simulation result is still an estimate.

Have students distinguish “probability” from “proportion of one sample.” The simulation proportion is a statistic used to estimate the chance under the model. Avoid promising that more trials makes the answer exact; it reduces random fluctuation but does not repair a bad model.

Simulation · 2 of 5

Four moves for a two-categorical simulation

01
State the chance assumption
Example: study-plan status and quiz result are unrelated in the population.
02
Build the model
Keep 40 plan labels and 60 non-plan labels; randomly shuffle the 66 pass labels across 100 students.
03
Run many repetitions
Record the simulated joint count each time; 1,000 or more repetitions gives a useful picture.
04
Compare to observed
Count simulated results at least as extreme as the observed joint count.

A simulation is only as good as the chance process it represents.

Simulation · 3 of 5 · KDEA

Observed association: could random assignment produce it?

In the study-plan table, 36 of the 40 plan-users passed. Estimate the chance of getting at least 36 passers among 40 plan-users if the variables were unrelated.

StepWhat you write
K100 students; 40 plan labels; 66 pass labels; observed plan-user pass count = 36.
DProbability of a simulated count ≥ 36, assuming no association.
ERandomly shuffle the 66 pass labels across the 100 fixed plan-status labels; repeat many times; find the proportion with count ≥ 36.
AIf the simulated proportion is small, conclude that a result at least this extreme would be unusual under the no-association model. State the conclusion in context.
The key design decision is what stays fixed: the observed margins. Students often simulate by drawing from the whole table and accidentally change the group sizes. Ask, “Which labels are being shuffled, and which labels are staying attached?” Do not supply a numerical result here; students will generate and interpret the distribution next.

Simulation · 4 of 5

StatKey makes the randomization visible

In StatKey
  1. Choose a randomization test for two categorical variables.
  2. Enter the observed table.
  3. Run many shuffles.
  4. Mark the tail at the observed statistic.
On paper, R, or Python

Write the model in words first. Then make the random mechanism reproducible with a seed and record the statistic each repetition produces.

successes ÷ repetitions is the estimate.

Software is a calculator for a model, not a substitute for the model.

The tool choice is secondary. The same logic should be visible in StatKey, a short R script, or Python. If students can click “run” without naming the statistic and tail, stop and reset. A seed helps students compare results while preserving the idea that different runs can differ slightly.

Simulation studio · Groups of 3 · 18 minutes

Run the model. Then write the conclusion.

Your task
  1. Use StatKey, R, or Python to randomize the pass labels across the fixed study-plan groups.
  2. Run at least 1,000 repetitions and display the simulated distribution.
  3. Estimate the proportion of simulated plan-user pass counts at least 36.
  4. Write a two-sentence conclusion: numerical estimate, then context.

Report: model · repetitions · statistic · estimate · context

Assign roles: model-checker, simulator, and reporter. The reporter must be able to explain what one repetition represented. Circulate for the phrase “at least 36”; students commonly use “exactly 36” and undercount the tail. Require a plot or tally so the estimate is evidence, not a mystery number.

Simulation · 5 of 5

Small simulated tail, careful conclusion

IF
The tail is small
The observed association would be unusual if the variables were unrelated.
IF
The tail is not small
The observed association is plausible under the no-association model.
ALWAYS
Keep the wording conditional
Say “under the model” or “if unrelated,” not “the model is true.”
NEVER
Claim causation from this table
An observational association does not prove that a study plan caused passing.
This slide protects the distinction between evidence and proof. Students may overclaim in either direction: “chance caused it” or “the study plan caused passing.” Bring them back to the model and the design. The simulation answers a chance question, not a causal question.
