Act I · Mixed Multiple Choice

See the structure before the numbers.

AP questions reward the model you choose, not the calculator you use.

This divider is the mental reset from unit-by-unit classwork to mixed retrieval. Tell students that the first move on a hard question is classification: data, probability, model, inference, or regression. Calculation comes after the classification.

Five Units · At a Glance

One course. Five different kinds of evidence.

U1
data
One-variable displays, summaries, sampling, and study design.
U2
probability
Random variables, distributions, expected value, and simulation.
U3
proportions + chi-square
Categorical inference: one proportion, two proportions, and chi-square independence or homogeneity.
U5
regression
Association, least-squares output, residuals, and prediction in context.
U4
means
Quantitative inference: one mean, two means, and paired means.
→

Name the variable, name the design, then choose the procedure or model.
Use this as the retrieval map. The unit order is intentionally U1, U2, U3, U5, U4 because that is the course sequence in the new CED. Do not let students collapse U3 and U4 into a generic "confidence interval" bucket: categorical versus quantitative is the first fork.

Multiple Choice · 45-second routine

Four moves before you touch the calculator

1
Name the variable
Categorical or quantitative? One variable or a relationship?
2
Name the design
Sample, experiment, paired data, two independent groups, or repeated trials?
3
Predict the direction
Before computing, decide whether the result should be positive, negative, larger, smaller, or near a benchmark.
4
Eliminate aggressively
Cross out wrong units, wrong parameter, impossible probability, and conclusions that overreach.

Classification first. Arithmetic second.

Mixed MC · Turn & Talk · 6 minutes

Practice set: classify each question

For each prompt, write only the first move
  1. A random sample estimates the percentage of students who prefer digital notes.
  2. Two independent groups compare mean commute times.
  3. A survey asks whether preferred social platform differs across three grade levels.
  4. A study reports that hours of sleep and reaction time have a moderate negative association.

Say: variable · parameter · design · procedure.

Do not collect final letters yet. Circulate for the language students use. A strong response names the variable type and target parameter before naming a test. Listen for "two means" when the design is actually paired, and for "correlation proves" when the prompt only gives observational association.

Highest-Value Review Asset · Procedure Chooser

Which procedure when?

Question asks aboutData / designProcedure or model
one population proportionone categorical sample; success / failureone-proportion z interval or test
difference in two proportionstwo independent categorical samplestwo-proportion z interval or test
association between two categorical variablesone random sample classified two wayschi-square test of independence or homogeneity
one population meanone quantitative sampleone-sample t interval or test
difference in two meanstwo independent quantitative samplestwo-sample t interval or test
mean of paired differencesmatched pairs or before/afterpaired t interval or test
linear association / predictiontwo quantitative variablesleast-squares regression analysis
Spend the most time here. The critical distinction is not "z versus t" first; it is categorical versus quantitative, then one versus two, then independent versus paired. Point to the chi-square row and say explicitly: this is not a goodness-of-fit procedure. The new CED review should stay with independence or homogeneity.

Procedure Chooser · Conditions

After the name comes the conditions

Randomness / independence
  • random sample or random assignment?
  • 10% condition when sampling without replacement?
  • independent groups or paired observations?
Normality / counts
  • large counts for a proportion procedure?
  • sample size / shape for a t procedure?
  • expected counts for chi-square?

A named procedure without conditions is unfinished reasoning.

Mixed MC · Probability

Probability: protect the sample space

Practice prompt

A game has three rounds. In each round, the probability of success is 0.70, and the rounds are independent. Let X be the number of successful rounds.

  1. Identify the distribution of X and its parameters.
  2. Write the expression for P(X = 2).
  3. Explain why P(X = 2) is not the same as 0.702.

Model first: fixed trials, two outcomes, constant p, independence.

This is a model-recognition check, not a calculator race. The common failure is using p raised to a power and forgetting the number of arrangements. Ask students to explain the binomial coefficient in words: how many placements of the two successes are possible?

Mixed MC · Regression

Regression language must stay attached to context

OutputAcceptable interpretation frame
slope bFor each additional 1 [x-unit], predicted [y] changes by b [y-units], on average.
rDirection and strength of a linear association between x and y.
r2Percent of variation in the observed response explained by the linear model with this explanatory variable.
residualobserved y minus predicted y for a particular case; units are y-units.

Never turn an observational regression into a causal sentence.

Keep the phrase "with this explanatory variable" in the r-squared interpretation; it prevents students from treating r-squared as a universal explanation. Also flag that the regression unit is U5 in this course, even though it appears before U4 in the calendar sequence.

Act II · FRQ Practice

Ten points are ten chances to be precise.

Earn the point the rubric is looking for.

Transition from selecting an answer to constructing evidence. The new 10-point FRQ format is not a demand for longer writing; it is a demand for visible statistical reasoning. Students should write one precise sentence where one precise sentence earns the point.

FRQ Anatomy · The 10-point format

What a complete response makes visible

K
data + design
Identify the sample, variables, randomization, and conditions that matter.
D
target
Name the parameter, probability, prediction, or claim being asked for.
E
procedure / formula
Name the procedure and show the statistic, formula, or model that connects data to target.
A
compute + conclude
Calculate accurately, then answer the question in context with direction and units.

A correct number is evidence. A context sentence is the claim.

FRQ Rubric Anatomy · Point by point

What earns points?

Rubric targetEvidence the reader can locateCommon miss
identify / statecorrect statistic, parameter, procedure, or design featurevague label: “do a test”
justify conditionscondition named with evidence from the prompt or data“conditions are met” with no support
calculatework, value, and appropriate roundingcalculator output with no statistic shown
interpretsentence names the population / variable / unitsgeneric “there is enough evidence”
concludeanswer matches the claim and direction askedcausal language without random assignment
This table is deliberately not a secure College Board rubric or answer key. It is the classroom anatomy of a point. Coach students to make the evidence easy to find: one claim per sentence, parameter named, context repeated. Readers should not have to infer what a number means.

FRQ Practice · Conditions

Conditions need evidence, not slogans

Weak

“The normal condition is met.”

“The samples are independent.”

Stronger

“The sample size is large enough for the one-proportion procedure because there are at least 10 expected successes and failures.”

“The groups are independent because each student appears in only one group.”

Name the condition. Point to the evidence.

FRQ Practice · Worked KDEA

One-proportion interval: from prompt to conclusion

A random sample of 120 students includes 78 who favor a later school start. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the population proportion.

StepWhat you write
Kn = 120; x = 78; p̂ = 78/120 = 0.65; random sample.
Dp = population proportion of students who favor a later start.
EOne-proportion z interval; check random, 10% if relevant, and large counts.
A\( \hat{p} \pm z^* \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n} \); report the interval and conclude: we are 95% confident the population proportion is between the two bounds.

The conclusion must name the population and the variable.

This is a worked mapping, not an answer key: do not reveal a numerical interval. The point is the structure students can reproduce. Have them identify exactly where the point for the population parameter lives and why “95% of students are in the interval” is the wrong interpretation.

FRQ Practice · Write · 12 minutes

Paired means: write the response, not just the test name

Prompt

The same 18 students complete a reaction-time task before and after a short training routine. The mean of the paired differences is reported, along with a standard deviation. Is there convincing evidence that the routine changes mean reaction time?

  1. Define the paired difference in context.
  2. State the appropriate inference procedure and conditions to check.
  3. Write a conclusion template that answers “changes,” not merely “is different.”

Pair the data first. Then choose the t procedure.

Watch for students who call this a two-sample t procedure because there are two columns. The unit of analysis is one difference per student. Also listen for conclusions that say “the training works”; the prompt asks about change in mean reaction time, and without random assignment the conclusion should not claim causation.

FRQ Rubric Anatomy · Common zero-point answers

These sound statistical. They do not earn the point.

0
wrong parameter
“The mean is 0.62” when the question asks for a population proportion.
0
no context
“We are 95% confident the interval is 0.54 to 0.71.” Confident about what?
0
condition slogan
“The conditions are met” without naming the condition or using evidence.
0
causal overreach
“The treatment caused the improvement” from an observational study.
0
calculator only
A number with no procedure, statistic, or interpretation.
0
wrong direction
A conclusion about “increases” when the evidence and question concern a decrease.
Keep the tone diagnostic, not punitive. Ask students to repair one zero-point sentence orally. The most valuable correction is usually adding the population and measured variable to a conclusion; context is where students can make a precise, fast improvement without more computation.

Mixed Practice · 18 minutes

One mini-set, two modes of thinking

Multiple choice mode
  • classify the variable
  • name the parameter
  • eliminate before computing
  • mark one reason for your choice
FRQ mode
  • show K / D / E / A
  • justify conditions with evidence
  • round with ≈
  • conclude in context

Same statistics. Different evidence trail.
