4.1

Sampling Distributions for Means

Same three questions as every sampling distribution: what shape, what center, what spread.

4.1 · Sampling Distributions

The sampling distribution of x̄

Take every possible random sample of size n, compute x̄ for each one, and plot all of those x̄'s. That plot is the sampling distribution:

Shape
Approximately normal if the population is normal, OR if n is large enough — the Central Limit Theorem.
Center
μx̄ = μ — no bias. The sampling distribution is centered exactly at the true population mean.
Spread
σx̄ = σ / √n — spread shrinks as n grows. More data, more precision.
The CLT claim is easy to over-state — be precise: if the population itself is normal, x̄ is normal for ANY n. If the population is not normal, x̄ only becomes approximately normal as n grows (the usual guideline is n ≥ 30, more if the population is heavily skewed). This exact distinction is what the Normal/Large Sample condition on the next slide is checking.

4.1 · Conditions

Three conditions, checked explicitly — every time

Random
Data comes from a random sample or a randomized experiment. No random selection, no inference.
Independent
10% condition: if sampling without replacement, n is at most 10% of the population.
Normal / Large Sample
Population is stated as normal, OR n ≥ 30, OR (for small n) the sample data show no strong skew and no outliers.

Write all three, by name, every single time — a KDEA "K" row isn't finished until conditions are checked.

Common failure mode #1 of the whole unit: students state conditions as a memorized phrase without actually checking them against the problem's numbers. "n = 12, so I'd need to look at a dotplot/boxplot of the sample for skew and outliers" is a checked condition. "Normal because CLT" with no reasoning is not — flag this hard, it costs real points on the exam's scoring guidelines.

Quick Check · Turn & Talk · 5 minutes

Are the conditions met?

With your table

A city has 40,000 registered voters. A pollster wants to estimate the mean number of minutes residents commute to work.

  1. They survey a random sample of 25 residents. Is the 10% condition met?
  2. The sample of 25 shows one very long commute (90 min) with the rest clustered under 30 min. Is the Normal/Large Sample condition met?
  3. What would you need to see instead for condition 2 to pass with n = 25?
Answers to guide circulation: (1) yes, 25 is nowhere near 10% of 40,000. (2) no — an outlier at n = 25 (well under 30) is disqualifying without a stated-normal population; the skew/outlier check genuinely fails here, and that's the point of using a "trap" example instead of a clean one. (3) a dotplot with no outliers and roughly symmetric shape, or a much larger sample (n ≥ 30). This is deliberately the case where the condition legitimately does NOT hold — resist the urge to let students talk themselves into "it's probably fine."

4.2

The t-Distribution

Why we almost never actually use z for a mean.

4.2 · The t-Distribution

Why t, not z?

The z-procedures from Unit 3 assumed we knew σ. For a mean, we almost never do — we estimate it with the sample standard deviation s. That substitution has a cost:

σ unknown
Real studies don't hand you the population standard deviation — you estimate it from your own sample, with s.
Extra uncertainty
Estimating σ with s adds variability that z doesn't account for — so the interval needs to be a little wider to stay honest.
Heavier tails
The t-distribution has more area in the tails than the standard normal — especially with small n.
df = n − 1
The t-distribution's shape depends on degrees of freedom. More data → more df → a shape closer to normal.
This is the single most tested confusion in Unit 4: "use z because n is large" or "use z because the problem gives a nice round number." The actual rule has nothing to do with n's size — it's entirely about whether σ is known (use z, extremely rare on this exam) or estimated with s (use t, essentially every problem you'll see this unit and on the real exam).

Find t* with invT on a graphing calculator, statistical software, or a t-table — never look it up from memory. StatKey's t-distribution tool visualizes exactly this convergence if you want to see the curves overlap live.

The table is the payoff of the "heavier tails" tile from the previous slide — made concrete. With only 5 df, t* is noticeably larger than z*, which produces a wider (more honest) interval. By df = 100 the two are nearly indistinguishable. Mention that AP Classroom / the digital exam expects technology (invT or similar) rather than a printed table lookup — normalize that now.

4.3

Confidence Intervals for a Mean

Same KDEA protocol. New E. New distribution.

4.3 · The Formula

x̄ ± t* · (s / √n)

The same KDEA protocol from every unit this year, mapped onto inference:

StepWhat it means for inference
Kthe data, the design, AND the three conditions — checked explicitly, not just named
Dthe parameter or claim — here, the population mean μ
Ethe procedure: a t-interval, the formula, and its degrees of freedom
Acompute the interval AND conclude in context — "convincing statistical evidence that…," never "prove"

Same four letters as a kinematics problem. The E and the units just changed.

This is the slide that formally introduces the stats-flavored KDEA mapping — spend real time here since every worked example the rest of Unit 4 will be laid out in exactly this table format. The A row deserves special emphasis: a numeric interval with no context sentence is an unfinished answer, identical to a kinematics answer with no unit.

Worked Example · Setup

Metrorail wait times

Miami-Dade Transit claims the average wait time for a Metrorail train is about 6 minutes. A transit researcher takes a random sample of n = 40 riders and records each one's self-reported wait time: x̄ = 6.8 min, s = 2.4 min.

Construct a 90% confidence interval for the true mean wait time.

Riders are the population here, sampled with replacement in the sense that the transit system's rider population is enormous relative to n = 40 — the 10% condition is trivially met and worth having a student say why out loud rather than skipping past it.

Worked Example · KDEA

90% CI for mean Metrorail wait time

StepWhat you write
Kn = 40, x̄ = 6.8 min, s = 2.4 min. Random: stated random sample. Independent: 40 riders is nowhere near 10% of all riders. Normal/Large Sample: n = 40 ≥ 30, CLT applies.
Dμ = the true mean Metrorail wait time, in minutes
Eone-sample t-interval: x̄ ± t* · (s / √n), df = 39
At* ≈ 1.685 (df = 39, invT, 90% confidence)
ME ≈ 1.685 × (2.4 / √40) ≈ 1.685 × 0.3795 ≈ 0.64 min
CI: 6.8 ± 0.64 ≈ (6.16, 7.44) minutes

We are 90% confident the true mean Metrorail wait time is between about 6.2 and 7.4 minutes.

Round only at the very end, not mid-calculation — the ≈ symbols reflect honest rounding, not an exact equality. Walk the conditions row of K out loud again; this is the row students most often skip when they're in a hurry, and it's worth full credit weight on the actual exam rubric.

4.3 · Interpretation

Say it exactly this way

AP-scorable interpretation

“We are 90% confident that the interval from 6.16 to 7.44 minutes captures the true mean Metrorail wait time.”

Never say:

  • “90% chance μ is inside” — μ isn't random; the interval is
  • “90% of wait times fall here” — that's data, not the parameter

Always include:

  • The confidence level (90%)
  • The actual interval, in context, with units
The "90% of wait times" misreading is the most common wrong interpretation on real AP responses — it confuses the confidence interval (about the unknown parameter μ) with the distribution of individual data values. Drill the exact sentence structure; scoring guidelines are unforgiving about this exact distinction.

4.3 · Justifying Claims

Use the interval to evaluate a claim

Transit's claim: the mean wait time is 6 minutes. Our interval: (6.16, 7.44) minutes.

The move

6 minutes is not inside the interval — every plausible value of μ the interval suggests is above 6. That's convincing statistical evidence the true mean wait time is greater than the claimed 6 minutes.

If the claimed value falls inside the interval, you do NOT have convincing evidence the claim is wrong.

This "does the claimed value fall inside the interval?" move is the exact same logic students used for proportions in Unit 3 — flag the callback. It also previews next week: this comparison-to-a- claim idea is what a hypothesis test formalizes with a p-value instead of eyeballing the interval.

Build your own interval

Common Traps

Two errors to watch for, starting today

z vs. t

The decision is about whether σ is known, not about how large n is. On this exam, σ is almost never given — default to t.

Skipped conditions

"Random, Normal, Independent" written with no reasoning attached earns no credit. Each condition needs a sentence that touches the actual numbers in the problem.

These are the two failure modes to flag every single time a student runs a KDEA table this unit — say them out loud enough this week that by Week 2 they're self-correcting before you have to.
