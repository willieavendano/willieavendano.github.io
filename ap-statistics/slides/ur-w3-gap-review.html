Where We Are

Review is now a triage problem.

All five AP Statistics units are on the table. We are not trying to reteach the course in order; we are deciding which missing move costs the most points.

  • Evidence — Bluebook practice-exam patterns, not vibes
  • Priority — frequent, fixable, transferable gaps
  • Practice — one clean move, then a new context
  • Transfer — explain the choice, not just the number

Today: find the gap. This week: close it.

Establish the distinction between a content gap and a performance gap. A student may know the one-proportion interval formula but still lose the point by skipping conditions or context. Those are different fixes, and the triage protocol is designed to separate them.

Common Test Traps

Most lost points are not hard math.

They are mismatches between the question and the response.

These traps are high-frequency because they survive even when students know the procedure. Keep returning to the question's verb: calculate, interpret, justify, or conclude. The verb tells the student what kind of response the grader can award.

Common Test Traps · 1 of 4

Trap #1: computing is not interpreting

Compute

Find the sample proportion, test statistic, interval endpoint, or probability.

number + work + correct rounding

Interpret

Explain what that number says about the variable, population, or chance process.

statistic + object + context

If the prompt says “interpret,” a bare number is unfinished.

Ask students to circle the verb in a prompt before they write. A common failure is giving a fully correct computation when the point is an interpretation of a confidence interval or a p-value. The response must name the object: population proportion, difference in means, or chance under the null model.

Common Test Traps · 2 of 4

Trap #2: conditions are part of the procedure.

Do not treat conditions as a ritual paragraph pasted after the math. Each check explains why the method is allowed.

D
Design
Random sample, random assignment, or a stated scope of inference.
I
Independence
10% condition when sampling without replacement, or a randomized design.
N
Normal / large counts
Use the appropriate success-failure or nearly normal check for the procedure.

Name the check. Name why it matters.

Keep the conditions tied to the method: a random sample supports generalization; random assignment supports cause-and-effect; the 10% condition supports independence; large counts support a normal approximation. Students often list all three but fail to connect the check to the conclusion.

Common Test Traps · 3 of 4

Trap #3: the conclusion must return to context.

The context loop
  1. Name the population, treatment, or random process.
  2. Use the statistic or interval in words, not as a floating symbol.
  3. Answer the original question with the correct strength of claim.
Too weak
“The interval is 0.42 to 0.58.” No population or variable.
Too strong
“The treatment causes improvement.” No random assignment, no cause claim.
This is where students overclaim: they turn an observational comparison into causation, or they report an interval without saying what population parameter it describes. Have them finish every conclusion with the original noun phrase from the prompt.

Common Test Traps · 4 of 4

Trap #4: probability notation changes the question.

WordsNotationTranslation
A and BP(A ∩ B)Both events happen.
A or BP(A ∪ B)At least one happens; subtract overlap if needed.
A given BP(A | B)Restrict the sample space to B.
not AP(Ac)Use the complement: 1 − P(A).

Translate first. Calculate second.

Make students say the conditional phrase out loud: "probability of A, given B." The vertical bar is not division by default; it signals a restricted sample space. Also flag that “or” is inclusive unless the context explicitly makes the events disjoint.

Worked Problem · Setup

A confidence interval is a sentence.

A random sample of 400 registered voters finds 220 who support a transportation proposal. Construct and interpret a 95% confidence interval for the population proportion.

Before the calculator
  1. What is the parameter?
  2. Why is a one-proportion interval appropriate?
  3. Which checks must appear before the interval?

Use KDEA: data/design/conditions · target · procedure · compute and conclude

This is a worked model, not an answer key. Ask students to predict the four KDEA rows before displaying the next slide. The stats version of K is data, design, and conditions; D is the parameter or claim sought; E is the interval procedure; A includes the computation and the contextual interpretation.

Worked Problem · KDEA

One-proportion interval, mapped to KDEA

StepWhat you write
KRandom sample; n = 400, x = 220, p̂ = 220/400 = 0.55. 10% condition is reasonable; n p̂ = 220 and n(1 − p̂) = 180.
DEstimate the population proportion p of registered voters who support the proposal.
EOne-proportion z interval: p̂ ± z*, with z* = 1.96.
A0.55 ± 1.96√[(0.55)(0.45)/400] = 0.55 ± 0.0488 ≈ (0.501, 0.599). We are 95% confident that the interval from 0.501 to 0.599 captures p for registered voters.

Notice the conclusion names the population and parameter.

Worked Problem · Setup

Probability notation: let the words control the algebra.

In a school survey, P(A) = 0.40, P(B) = 0.25, and P(A ∩ B) = 0.10. Here, A is “takes Statistics” and B is “takes Computer Science.”

Question 1

Find the probability a student takes Statistics given that the student takes Computer Science.

Question 2

Are A and B independent? Justify with a probability comparison.

Same letters. Different order. Read the conditioning bar.

Worked Problem · KDEA

Conditional probability, mapped to KDEA

StepWhat you write
KP(A) = 0.40, P(B) = 0.25, P(A ∩ B) = 0.10.
DFind P(A | B); decide whether A and B are independent.
EP(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B). Independence requires P(A | B) = P(A).
AP(A | B) = 0.10 / 0.25 = 0.40. Since P(A | B) = P(A) = 0.40, A and B are independent in this model.

A conditional probability is a new sample space, not a decorative symbol.

This example is intentionally exact: no rounding hides the reasoning. Ask what would change if P(A | B) were 0.35. Students should see that the independence test is a comparison of probabilities, not a feeling that the events “seem unrelated.”

Triage Studio · 20 minutes

Build your personal review queue.

With your practice-exam record
  1. Choose three missed or uncertain items.
  2. Tag each: compute, interpret, conditions, context, notation, or time management.
  3. Write the single move that would have earned the point.
  4. Rank by frequency × transfer × fixability.

Then choose one item to explain to a partner without showing the original prompt.

Your queue should fit on one index card.

Circulate for specificity. “Review Unit 4” is too broad; “state the random-assignment condition before claiming causation” is actionable. If a student has no practice-exam record available, use a recent class response or ask them to identify a trap from today's four.

Rapid Reference

Four questions to ask before you submit.

1
What did the prompt ask?
Compute, interpret, justify, compare, or conclude? Match the verb.
2
Why is this method allowed?
State the design and the relevant independence / large-count condition.
3
What does the result describe?
Return to the population, variable, treatment, or chance process.
4
Did my notation say the same thing?
Check bars, complements, unions, intersections, subscripts, and units.

Last line of every response: answer the original question.

Act II

Read the question. Run the move.

Calm is a testing strategy.

The exam window is in progress, so do not make this a generic motivational speech. Students need a repeatable sequence that reduces decision load: read the task, mark the evidence, choose the method, and finish in context.

Exam-Day Readiness · 1 of 2

A calm checklist for the digital exam.

  • Arrive with the Bluebook workflow familiar: sign in, directions, calculator plan.
  • Use scratch work for symbols, conditions, and the first line of algebra.
  • Mark a problem only after you have named what it is asking.
  • For FRQs, leave a visible trail: K, D, E, A.
  • Use ≈ for rounded values and = for exact identities.
  • Reserve the final minute to check context and notation.

One breath. One prompt. One next move.

Keep the digital details limited to the workflow students can control. The point is not to predict secure questions; it is to remove avoidable friction. Remind them that Bluebook, StatKey, R, and Python are course tools, but the exam-day tool set is whatever the official directions permit.
