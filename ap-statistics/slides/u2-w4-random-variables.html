Act I · The Object

Meet X.

A random variable records a numerical outcome.

Use this divider to slow the room down before notation arrives. X is not “a random number” and it is not the probability itself. It is a rule that assigns a number to each outcome of a random process.

KDEA · The Stats Mapping

Same protocol. New kinds of answers.

K
Know
Data, design, and conditions given in the problem.
D
Don't know
The parameter, probability, or claim being sought.
E
Equations
The statistical procedure or formula that connects K to D.
A
Algebra
Compute and conclude in context. The context is part of A.

In AP Statistics, an answer without context is like an answer without units.

This is the bridge from the house protocol to Statistics. Do not let K become a list of numbers detached from design or conditions: include what was observed and under what random process. D is not merely “solve for x”; name the parameter, probability, or claim. E is the formula or procedure. A includes both the computation and the context conclusion. Students frequently stop at the number, so say explicitly that context is part of Algebra in this course.

Random Variables · 1 of 3

A random variable translates outcomes into numbers.

Let X be the number of customers who order iced coffee during the first ten minutes after the café opens.

X
Variable
The numerical quantity being recorded.
0, 1, 2, …
Values
Possible counts from the random process.
P(X = x)
Probability
The chance that X takes a particular value.

The lowercase x names one possible value; uppercase X names the variable.

Random Variables · 2 of 3

Discrete means the possible values can be listed.

Discrete

A count: X = number of late buses. The values are separate, countable outcomes.

Not a count

A measurement such as exact waiting time can take any value in an interval. We are focusing on discrete X today.

Always name the unit of observation: one game, one customer, one randomly selected student, or one day.

Random Variables · 3 of 3

A probability distribution pairs each value with its chance.

RequirementWhat it means
0 ≤ P(X = x) ≤ 1No probability is negative or greater than 1.
Σ P(X = x) = 1The listed outcomes cover the entire sample space.
One value, one probabilityEach possible x has exactly one probability attached to it.

Before calculating a parameter, check that the table is actually a distribution.

Students often check only that the probabilities add to 1. Require both checks: every probability must be between 0 and 1, and the total must be 1. Also ask what is missing when the total is less than 1: an unlisted outcome, not a “bad” random variable. The sigma notation is a compact way to say “add all the probabilities.”

Distribution Check

One table can tell the whole probability story.

x = goals in one game0123
P(X = x)0.200.350.300.15
Check 1

Each probability is between 0 and 1.

Check 2

0.20 + 0.35 + 0.30 + 0.15 = 1

Interpret one entry: P(X = 2) = 0.30 means a 30% chance of exactly two goals in one game.

Parameters · 1 of 3

Mean is the long-run balance point.

For a discrete random variable, the mean or expected value is a weighted average:

μX = E(X) = Σ x · P(X = x)

  • Each possible value is weighted by how likely it is.
  • The mean does not have to be a possible value of X.
  • Interpret it over many repetitions of the same random process.
Emphasize that expected value is not the most likely outcome and not a promise about the next observation. In the goals table, a mean of 1.4 goals would not mean a game can end with 1.4 goals. It means the average number of goals would approach 1.4 over many comparable games. Students who simply average the x-values are ignoring the probabilities; ask them which values deserve more weight.

Parameters · 2 of 3

Standard deviation measures typical distance from the mean.

For a discrete random variable:

Variance

\( \sigma_X^2 = \sum (x - \mu_X)^2 \cdot P(X = x) \)

Standard deviation

\( \sigma_X = \sqrt{\sigma_X^2} \)

σX uses the same units as X. A larger σX means outcomes are typically farther from μX.

Do not let students read standard deviation as “the maximum distance” or “the distance every score has.” It is a typical distance, based on squared deviations and weighted by probability. The units point is useful: variance has squared units, but standard deviation returns to the original unit, which is why context statements use sigma rather than sigma squared.

Worked Example · Setup

How many goals should we expect?

For one randomly selected game, let X be the number of goals scored. The distribution is:

x0123
P(X = x)0.200.350.300.15

Find μX, σX, and interpret both in context.

This is a fully worked example, so the answer belongs on the next slide. Have students predict the rough scale before calculating: the center should be between 1 and 2, and the spread should be less than 2 goals. That estimate becomes a reasonableness check. Keep the unit of observation visible: one randomly selected game.

Worked Example · KDEA

Build the answer from the distribution.

StepWhat you write
Kx = 0, 1, 2, 3; probabilities 0.20, 0.35, 0.30, 0.15; one game
DμX and σX, with units of goals
E\( \mu_X = \sum x\,P(X=x) \); \( \sigma_X = \sqrt{\sum (x-\mu_X)^2 P(X=x)} \)
AμX = 0(0.20)+1(0.35)+2(0.30)+3(0.15) = 1.40 goals
σX = √0.94 ≈ 0.97 goals; in context: over many games, the mean is 1.40 goals and a typical game is about 0.97 goals from that mean.
Model the Stats mapping explicitly: K is the data and design context given; D is the parameter sought; E is the procedure or formula; A is computation plus a conclusion in context. The most common failure is stopping after 1.40 and 0.97. Require the sentence that names the random process, the parameter, and the units. Here 1.40 is exact for the stated distribution; 0.97 is rounded, so the symbol is ≈, not =.

With your partner

  2. Use KDEA to find μX and σX.

Circulate for structure, not just arithmetic. Look first for the distribution check, then for a clear K row that identifies one randomly selected day. Students often write “mean = 1.7” with no object attached; prompt, “1.7 what?” For the standard deviation, listen for “typically” or “about,” not “always.” Do not display or announce an answer key during this practice slide.

Act II · The Parameters

Center. Spread. Context.

The parameter is useful only when the random process is still attached.

This act break marks the shift from “what is the object?” to “what can we say about its behavior?” Re-anchor students before the practice and assessment language: every numerical result needs a sentence about the population of repeated trials represented by the distribution.

Read the Parameters

Two numbers answer two different questions.

ParameterQuestionContext language
μXWhere is the distribution centered?In the long run, the average is …
σXHow far do outcomes typically fall from center?A typical outcome is about … from the mean.

Same random process. Different question. Different sentence.

Common Test Traps

Three ways a correct calculation becomes a weak answer.

1
Unweighted average
Averaging the x-values ignores that outcomes have different probabilities.
2
Wrong parameter
Reporting variance when the question asks for standard deviation changes the units.
3
No context
A bare number does not identify what was measured or what the number means.
Ask students to diagnose the error before fixing it. “I averaged the x-values” is a weighting error; “I used sigma squared” is a parameter and units error; “I wrote 1.4” is a communication error. This diagnostic language is more durable than simply handing back a corrected number.
