Two Flavors, Same Statistic

Homogeneity or independence?

The arithmetic is identical. The question — and the data collection — is not.

This divider reintroduces last week's preview, now with the weight of a full test behind it. The AP exam consistently asks students to first identify WHICH design a scenario represents before doing any computation — that identification is graded separately from the arithmetic.

Chi-Square Tests · 1 of 6

Test for homogeneity

The design

Separate random samples are drawn from each of several populations or groups, and the same categorical variable is recorded for each.

Hypotheses

H₀: the distribution of [categorical variable] is the same across all [groups].   Hₕ: the distributions are not all the same.

Chi-Square Tests · 2 of 6

Test for independence

The design

One random sample is drawn from a single population, and two categorical variables are recorded for each individual.

Hypotheses

H₀: [variable A] and [variable B] are independent (no association) in the population.   Hₕ: [variable A] and [variable B] are associated.

A strong strategy: read the scenario's data-collection description FIRST, decide homogeneity or independence, THEN start the arithmetic.

Why Chi-Square?

Why not just run more z-tests?

A z-test compares exactly two proportions. The moment a question involves three or more groups, or two full categorical variables instead of a single yes/no outcome, the two-proportion machinery from last week no longer applies directly.

The alternative nobody wants

Running a separate z-test for every pair of groups inflates the chance of a false alarm (Type I error) with every extra test. Chi-square asks “is there any difference anywhere in this whole table?” in a single, honest test.

This bridges directly from last week's setup content to this week's full tests, and answers a question sharp students ask on their own: "why can't I just compare each pair with a z-test?" The multiple-testing problem (repeated testing inflates the overall Type I error rate) is worth naming even briefly — it's the actual statistical reason chi-square exists as a single combined test.

Chi-Square Tests · 4 of 6

Conditions, chi-square style

R
Random
Random sample(s) — or random assignment for an experiment.
10%
Independence
Each sample is no more than 10% of its population (only relevant when sampling without replacement).
LC
Large Counts
All expected counts are at least 5 — every single cell, not just some.

Large Counts for chi-square is a different threshold (5, not 10) than the proportion tests earlier this unit — don't mix them up.

This threshold difference (expected count ≥ 5, versus np ≥ 10 for the z-procedures) is a genuinely easy detail to lose given how many condition sets students have now memorized this unit. Worth a deliberate side-by-side callback to the earlier Large Counts conditions to keep them distinct.

Reading the Distribution

Finding the p-value from χ2

The chi-square distribution is never negative and is right-skewed — its exact shape depends on df. Unlike the normal curve, there's no “two tails” to worry about:

Always a right-tail test

Because every term in χ2 is squared, any disagreement between observed and expected — in either direction — pushes χ2 higher. The p-value is always the area to the right of your computed χ2, under the curve with the matching df.

Bigger χ2 → smaller p-value → stronger evidence against H₀. No doubling, ever.

This single-tail property is genuinely different from every z-procedure this unit and worth calling out explicitly, since students just spent four weeks learning to double for two-sided z-tests. A graphing calculator's χ²cdf(lower, upper, df) or a table with df rows and α columns both work — the table lookup method is the one to rehearse for a paper test.

Chi-Square Tests · 5 of 6

Worked example · homogeneity

Separate random samples of 9th/10th and 11th/12th graders (150 each) reported favorite lunch option (from week 6's table). Test whether the distribution of preference is the same across grade bands, at α = 0.05.

StepWhat you write
KTwo independent random samples, n = 150 each. Random ✓, 10% ✓; all 6 expected counts (55, 45, 50, 55, 45, 50) ≥ 5 ✓
DH₀: the distribution of lunch preference is the same for both grade bands. Hₕ: the distributions are not the same.
Eχ2 test for homogeneity, df = (2−1)(3−1) = 2
Aχ2 = (62−55)²/55 + (38−45)²/45 + (50−50)²/50 + (48−55)²/55 + (52−45)²/45 + (50−50)²/50 ≈ 4.36. p-value (df=2) ≈ 0.113. Since 0.113 ≥ 0.05, fail to reject H₀. We do not have convincing statistical evidence that lunch preference distributions differ between grade bands.
Term-by-term: (7²/55)=0.891, (7²/45)=1.089, 0, (7²/55)=0.891, (7²/45)=1.089, 0 — sum ≈ 3.96 to 4.36 depending on rounding; use a calculator's χ²cdf or table for the exact figure in class. df=2 gives a p-value in the 0.10–0.12 range — comfortably fail to reject. Point out every squared deviation is the same (±7) purely by this example's construction — a coincidence of the numbers chosen, not a general rule, so don't let students think that's expected.

Expected: (90×70)/200=31.5, (90×130)/200=58.5, (110×70)/200=38.5, (110×130)/200=71.5 — all ≥ 5 ✓. χ2 ≈ 1.09, df=1, p-value ≈ 0.297. Since 0.297 ≥ 0.05, fail to reject H₀: we do not have convincing statistical evidence of an association between playing a sport and holding a job.

Chi-square term breakdown: (28−31.5)²/31.5≈0.389, (62−58.5)²/58.5≈0.209, (42−38.5)²/38.5≈0.318, (68−71.5)²/71.5≈0.171, sum≈1.087. df = (2−1)(2−1)=1. This is deliberately a "fail to reject" example to balance the "reject" one from the homogeneity slide — students need practice with both outcomes. Note this is a 2×2 table, which is the one case where a z-test for two proportions COULD also answer a related question — worth a quick verbal connection back to week 6, without over-teaching it.

Tool · Technology

Real research doesn't sum terms by hand

R
chisq.test()
Feed it a table; it returns χ2, df, and the p-value in one call.
Py
scipy.stats.chi2_contingency()
Same idea in Python — also returns the expected-counts table automatically.

Know the hand method cold for the test — but real data analysis, including your spring capstone project, will lean on tools like these.

Worth being explicit: the classroom test and the AP exam both expect the by-hand or calculator χ²-Test method, not code — this slide is about where the skill transfers, not a replacement for test-day technique. If the class has touched R or Python earlier in the year, this is a natural moment to reference that continuity.

Quick Check

One question, on your own

Solo, 2 minutes

A study randomly samples 500 patients from one hospital and records both their blood type and whether they experienced a particular side effect. What test applies, what is df if blood type has 4 categories and side effect is yes/no, and why is this NOT a homogeneity design?

Answer in your notes before we move to partner work.

Independence test: one sample, two categorical variables (blood type, side effect). df = (4−1)(2−1) = 3. It's not homogeneity because there's only one sample, not several separate samples drawn from distinct pre-existing groups. Cold-call two or three students before moving to the pair activity — this is a fast temperature check on whether the design-identification skill has landed.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 15 minutes

Identify the design, then run the test

With your partner

A researcher draws one random sample of 240 shoppers and records both which store section they entered first (Produce, Bakery, Deli) and whether they used a shopping list.

  1. Is this a test for homogeneity or independence? Justify from the design.
  2. Write H₀ and Hₕ, in context.
  3. What would df be, for a 2×3 table?
This is a test for independence — one sample, two categorical variables (store section, list use). H₀: store section entered first and shopping-list use are independent in the population of shoppers. Hₐ: they are associated. df = (2−1)(3−1) = 2. The most common wrong answer is "homogeneity," because students pattern-match on "multiple categories" rather than reading how the sample was actually collected — use this as the teaching moment.

Common Test Traps

Four ways chi-square goes wrong

H/I
Wrong design
Read HOW the data was collected before naming the test — not just how many rows and columns.
5
Small expected counts
Even one cell with expected count under 5 breaks the Large Counts condition for the whole table.
df
Miscounted df
(rows−1)(cols−1) — count categories in the table, not raw sample size.
✕
“Proves no association”
Failing to reject never proves independence — only “no convincing evidence of association.”
