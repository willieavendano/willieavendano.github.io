Act I · The Language

Probability makes uncertainty computable.

Every probability statement has an event, a sample space, and an interpretation.

Use this divider to establish the conceptual arc. Students will see symbols quickly, but the symbols only help after the event has been named. Ask for a plain-language version of any formula before accepting algebra; this catches the common failure mode of adding percentages that do not describe the same population or sample space.

Probability · 1 of 4

Three useful interpretations of the same number

1
Long-run relative frequency
If the random process is repeated many times under the same conditions, the proportion of successes tends to stabilize near P(A).
2
Personal belief
A probability can express how plausible an event seems, given the information available.
3
Area or proportion
For a model, probability can be the fraction of equally likely outcomes or the area of a region.
The AP course uses these interpretations in different settings. Emphasize that a 0.30 chance does not mean the event happens every third trial; it means the long-run pattern is about 30% under the stated mechanism. Students commonly confuse a probability model with a guarantee about the next individual outcome.

Probability · 2 of 4

Start with the sample space

The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes. An event is a collection of outcomes we care about.

SituationSample spaceEvent A
One fair die roll{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}roll an even number: {2, 4, 6}
One student selectedall students in the classstudent rides the bus
One manufactured partall parts produced todaypart passes inspection

A probability is always about an event inside a specified sample space.

Probability · 3 of 4

The basic rules keep probability honest

0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1
Bounds
Probabilities are proportions. They cannot be negative or exceed 1.
P(S) = 1
Sample space
Something in the complete sample space must happen.
P(∅) = 0
Impossible event
The empty event contains no possible outcomes.
ΣP(outcome) = 1
Disjoint outcomes
When outcomes cannot happen together, their probabilities add to the whole.
Keep the rules connected to the sample space rather than presenting them as a memorization list. A useful quick check is to ask whether a proposed answer passes the bounds rule. Students often report 1.14 after adding percentages from overlapping categories; that is a signal to inspect the event definitions, not to round the result back down.

Probability · 4 of 4

The complement is everything outside the event.

If A is an event, its complement Ac is “not A.” The event and its complement partition the sample space.

Complement rule

P(Ac) = 1 − P(A)    and    P(A) = 1 − P(Ac)

  • A: a randomly selected part fails
  • Ac: a randomly selected part does not fail
  • Use the complement when “at least one” is awkward.
  • Translate the final result back to the original event.
Insist that “not A” is not always “the opposite word.” If A is “student earns an A,” A-complement is every grade other than A. The most common failure is calculating P(A-complement) and reporting it as P(A), so have students say the event aloud in the last line.

Worked Example · Setup

When “at least one” suggests a complement

A quality-control process finds that 8% of parts fail inspection. For one randomly selected part, what is the probability that it passes inspection?

KDEA prompt
  1. K — identify the observed or modeled probability.
  2. D — name the event and its probability.
  3. E — choose a rule before computing.
  4. A — compute, then conclude in context.

In statistics, context is part of the answer.

Worked Example · KDEA

Pass inspection: a complete KDEA response

StepWhat you write
KP(fails) = 0.08; pass and fail are complements.
DP(passes), the probability a randomly selected part passes inspection.
EComplement rule: P(passes) = 1 − P(fails)
AP(passes) = 1 − 0.08 = 0.92. There is a 0.92 probability that a randomly selected part passes inspection.

The last sentence names the object, the population process, and the probability.

Model the distinction between an exact identity and a rounded result. Here 0.92 is exact given 0.08, so an equals sign is appropriate. In a simulation or estimated probability, use ≈ instead. Call out the final context sentence: “0.92” alone is incomplete because it does not say what the probability describes.

Addition Rule · 1 of 4

Mutually exclusive means “cannot happen together.”

A ∩ B = ∅
Set language
The intersection is empty: no outcome belongs to both events.
P(A ∩ B) = 0
Probability language
The probability of both events occurring on one trial is zero.
Die: 2 or 5
Mutually exclusive
One roll cannot show both 2 and 5.
Die: even or 4
Not mutually exclusive
A roll of 4 belongs to both events.
Students often hear “or” and automatically add. Make them test the intersection first. Use the die examples to show that an event can be contained inside another event, so overlap is possible even when the words feel different. This distinction is the hinge for the next slide.

Addition Rule · 2 of 4

Add disjoint events; subtract overlap in general.

SituationRuleWhy
A and B are mutually exclusiveP(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)No outcome is counted twice.
A and B can overlapP(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)Subtract the overlap counted twice.

Union, A ∪ B, means A or B or both. Intersection, A ∩ B, means A and B.

Say “or, including both” every time you read A ∪ B.

Worked Example · KDEA

One die roll: an addition-rule decision

For a fair six-sided die, what is the probability of rolling a 1 or a 6?

StepWhat you write
KSix equally likely outcomes; P(1) = 1/6, P(6) = 1/6.
DP(1 ∪ 6), the probability of rolling a 1 or a 6.
E1 and 6 are mutually exclusive, so add: P(1 ∪ 6) = P(1) + P(6).
AP(1 ∪ 6) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6 = 1/3. The probability is 1/3 of one die roll.
Have students justify the rule in words before simplifying the fraction. The common failure mode is using the general addition rule mechanically and inventing an overlap term; here the intersection is empty. Also ask what would change for “even or 6”: the events overlap, so plain addition would double-count 6.

Activity · StatKey · 10 minutes

Use simulation to make “long run” visible.

StatKey
  1. Open a chance experiment with equally likely outcomes.
  2. Run many repetitions and record the relative frequency of an event.
  3. Repeat with a new random seed.
Discuss
  • What changed from run to run?
  • What stabilized as trials increased?
  • How would you explain the probability without saying “guaranteed”?

The model predicts a pattern across repetitions, not the next single result.

Keep the debrief focused on interpretation, not interface technique. Students should notice variability in short runs and stabilization in long runs. If available, ask one student to recreate the experiment in R or Python after class; the code is a second representation of the same random mechanism, not a different definition of probability.

Act II · The Protocol

Words first. Equations second.

KDEA turns a probability prompt into a defensible statistical response.

This is the transition from vocabulary to practice. Remind students that KDEA is unchanged from the house protocol: Know, Dont know, Equations, Algebra. In this course, the D line names a probability, parameter, or claim, and the A line must compute and conclude in context.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 12 minutes

Choose the rule before you calculate.

A school survey reports that 0.42 of students participate in a club and 0.18 participate in a sport. Some students do both.

With your partner
  1. Define A = “participates in a club” and B = “participates in a sport.”
  2. Explain why the events are not mutually exclusive.
  3. Write the K, D, and E lines for P(A ∪ B). What additional information is needed for A?

Do not invent an overlap. Identify what the prompt does and does not give you.

Probability · 3 of 4

Four traps that look like small mistakes

1
No event defined
A decimal appears with no sentence saying what it measures.
2
“Or” means add
The events overlap, but the overlap is counted twice.
3
Complement loses context
P(Ac) is calculated correctly, then reported as P(A).
4
Probability becomes prediction
A long-run proportion is treated as a guarantee for one trial.
