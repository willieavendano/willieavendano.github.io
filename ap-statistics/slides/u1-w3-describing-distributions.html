Topic 1.6

S · O · C · V

Shape · Outliers · Center · Variability — the four things every distribution description needs.

SOCV is this course's version of KDEA for a specific recurring task: describing a distribution. Say explicitly that it's not optional or a suggestion — a full-credit graph description on the AP exam touches all four, every time, in context.

1.6 · 1 of 4

Shape

△
Symmetric
roughly mirror-image on either side of the center
→
Skewed right
a long tail stretching toward high values (e.g., incomes)
←
Skewed left

The direction of skew is the single most-flipped fact in the unit — "skewed right" means the TAIL points right, not that most of the data is on the right. Draw it out: a right-skewed distribution actually has most of its data bunched on the LEFT with a thin tail stretching right. Say this contrast explicitly every time skew comes up.

1.6 · 2 of 4

Center

Two candidates for “the middle” — and the shape from the last slide decides which one to trust.

Mean — the balance point; pulled toward outliers and skew

Median — the middle value; resistant to outliers and skew

Roughly symmetric → mean and median agree. Skewed or outliers present → report the median.

This slide is the setup for a trap that reappears constantly: students report the mean for a skewed distribution because "mean" is the word they know best. Push them to justify their choice of center by naming the shape first — that's the actual skill being tested.

1.6 · 3 of 4

Variability

How spread out the data is — a center alone can be misleading without it.

Why it matters

Two classes can both average 80% on a test. In one, every score is 78–82. In the other, scores range from 40% to 100%. Same center, wildly different story — variability is what tells them apart.

Formal measures — range, IQR, standard deviation — get calculated next, in 1.7.

This is a conceptual bridge slide before 1.7's formulas. The two-classes example is worth drawing on the board as two rough dotplots side by side — visual proof that "same average" can hide very different realities, which is exactly why variability gets its own SOCV letter.

1.6 · 4 of 4

Outliers

A value that doesn't fit the pattern of the rest of the data. The formal test (the 1.5×IQR rule) comes with 1.7's five-number summary — for now, describe outliers you can see in a graph.

Worked example

Quiz scores: 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 8, 9, 9, 9, 9, 10, 1. That lone 1 sits far from the rest of the cluster — a visible outlier, worth naming and, if possible, explaining (a blank test? a data entry error?).

Emphasize: naming an outlier isn't enough for full credit — a strong description also considers whether it's a legitimate extreme value or a likely error, and how much it might be dragging the mean around. This previews the resistant-vs-non-resistant conversation in 1.7.

1.6 · SOCV in Context

Worked example: full SOCV description

StepWhat you write
Ka histogram of 30 students' commute times, right-skewed, peak at 10–15 min, one value at 55 min
Da complete description of the distribution
ESOCV — Shape, Outliers, Center, Variability
A“The distribution of commute times is skewed right, with a possible outlier at 55 minutes. The center is best described by the median, around 12 minutes, since the skew and outlier pull the mean higher. The variability is moderate, with most commutes falling between 8 and 20 minutes.”
Read the A row aloud slowly — this single paragraph is the model answer format for every SOCV FRQ this year. Point out it names all four letters explicitly and never uses a bare number without a unit and a sentence around it. This is "context travels with the conclusion" in its fullest form so far.

Topic 1.7

Summary Statistics

Now we calculate the numbers SOCV was describing in words.

1.7 · 1 of 5

Mean and median, calculated

Mean

x̄ = (Σx) / n

Sum every value, divide by the count. Sensitive to every single data point.

Median

Sort the data. Odd n: the middle value. Even n: average the two middle values.

Data: 4, 5, 5, 6, 8, 9, 40 → mean = 11.0, median = 6. The outlier (40) drags the mean, not the median.

This worked strip is the concrete proof behind the "median is resistant" claim from Week 3's Center slide. Compute both live if time allows — watching the mean jump while the median holds steady is more convincing than being told the fact.

1.7 · 2 of 5

Standard deviation

Roughly, the typical distance a data point sits from the mean.

Formula — you'll use technology to compute this, but know what it means

s = √( Σ(x − x̄)2 / (n − 1) )

Small s → values cluster tightly around the mean. Large s → values are spread out.

Interpretation in context: “Commute times typically differ from the mean by about 6 minutes.”

Don't grind through the formula by hand for a large dataset — the point is interpreting the output, not hand-computing it. Every SD value on the exam needs a context sentence like the chip line, not just the bare number. This is where "answer without context doesn't count" gets its sharpest test.

IQR (interquartile range) = Q3 − Q1 — the spread of the middle 50% of the data, resistant to outliers.

This five-number summary is what next week's boxplots are built from directly — say that connection out loud now so Week 4 doesn't feel like a new topic. The five tiles are deliberately the exact shape a boxplot takes: whisker, box edge, line, box edge, whisker.

1.7 · 4 of 5

The formal outlier test: 1.5×IQR

Rule

A value is a formal outlier if it falls below Q1 − 1.5(IQR) or above Q3 + 1.5(IQR).

This is the calculation that turns “that point looks weird” from Week 3's SOCV slide into a defensible, checkable claim.

Walk one full numeric example if time allows: Q1=8, Q3=20, IQR=12, so the fences are 8-18=-10 and 20+18=38 — anything below -10 or above 38 is a formal outlier. The rule matters because "eyeballing" an outlier isn't defensible on a free response; the 1.5×IQR test is.

1.7 · 5 of 5

Resistant vs. non-resistant statistics

✓
Resistant
median, IQR — barely move when an outlier is added or removed
×
Non-resistant
mean, standard deviation, range — can swing dramatically from a single outlier

The rule of thumb: skewed or outlier-heavy data → report median and IQR, not mean and SD.

This slide is the payoff that ties 1.6 and 1.7 together — the shape you identify in SOCV is literally the justification for which pair of statistics (mean/SD or median/IQR) belongs in your answer. Graders want to see that reasoning stated, not just the right numbers.

Worked Example · KDEA

Full summary statistics workup

StepWhat you write
Ktest scores: 62, 68, 71, 74, 75, 78, 80, 82, 85, 97
Dfull numerical summary — center, spread, and any outliers
Efive-number summary, IQR, 1.5×IQR rule, mean and median (technology for the arithmetic)
AMin=62, Q1=71, Med=76.5, Q3=82, Max=97, IQR=11 → upper fence = 98.5. 97 is not a formal outlier, though it's the largest value. Mean = 77.2, median = 76.5 — close together, so the distribution is roughly symmetric with no outliers.
The twist in this example is deliberate: 97 LOOKS like an outlier by eye but fails the formal 1.5×IQR test. This is exactly the kind of item that separates students who memorized "biggest number = outlier" from students who actually run the rule. Make them verify the arithmetic.

Turn and Talk · 8 minutes

Which center would you trust?

With your partner

For each scenario, would you report the mean or the median? Justify with the shape.

  1. Household incomes in a large city
  2. Heights of adult women
  3. Years of experience of teachers at a brand-new school (mostly first-year hires, a few veterans)
Answers: (1) median — income is classically right-skewed, a few very high earners drag the mean up. (2) mean — heights are roughly symmetric, mean and median agree closely. (3) median — skewed by a handful of veteran teachers among mostly first-year staff. The justification sentence matters as much as the correct choice.

Tools of the Trade

Let the computer do the arithmetic

Hand-calculating standard deviation for 40 data points is a waste of your time on a test that's checking whether you understand what it means.

R
summary(), sd()
five-number summary and standard deviation in one line
Py
pandas.describe()
mean, std, quartiles, all at once
Reinforce: the graded skill is interpretation and justification (which statistic, why, in context), not manual arithmetic. Calculators and R/Python exist so class time goes toward the reasoning, not the computation.
