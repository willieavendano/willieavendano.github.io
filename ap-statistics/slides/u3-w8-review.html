The Big Picture

Unit 3 is one skeleton, worn five ways.

Estimate → interval → test → compare two → compare many.

This divider sets up the concept map, mirroring the "one story, not four topics" framing used in every review deck this year. The single biggest failure mode heading into the test is treating each procedure as a separate memorized recipe instead of the same KDEA move, run five times with different formulas plugged in.

The Big Picture · 1 of 1

Five procedures, one protocol

1
Sampling distributions
\( \text{Mean} = p \), \( SD = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n} \). This is where every formula below comes from.
2
One-proportion CI & test
p̂ ± z*SE to estimate; z = (p̂−p₀)/SD to test a specific claim.
3
Two-proportion CI & test
Same shape, two groups. Interval uses separate p̂'s; test pools them into p̂c.
4
Chi-square
More than two groups or variables. Same “observed vs. expected” logic, new statistic and new distribution.

There is no new protocol anywhere in this unit — there's KDEA, run against five formula sets.

Walk the chain explicitly: everything descends from tile 1's sampling distribution theory. The CI formula is "point estimate ± critical value × SE" every time. The test formula is always "(statistic − hypothesized value) / SD under H₀" every time. Students who see this chain stop needing to memorize five unrelated formulas and start recognizing one shape with different inputs.

The Conditions, Every Time

Random. 10%. Large Counts. Every single procedure.

ProcedureLarge Counts uses…
One-proportion intervalp̂ — np̂ ≥ 10, n(1−p̂) ≥ 10
One-proportion testp₀ (the hypothesized value)
Two-proportion intervaleach group's own p̂
Two-proportion testthe pooled p̂c, for both groups
Chi-squareevery expected count ≥ 5 (not 10)

This table is the single highest-yield thing to memorize before Thursday.

This is the exact table students should be copying onto a notecard mentally before the test. It's also the single fastest way to lose points — a correct answer with the wrong condition-check formula (p̂ where p₀ belongs, or vice versa) is a real and common point deduction. Common Test Traps

The same four mistakes, every year.

Name them now so Thursday isn't the first time you see them.

Common Test Traps · 1 of 4

Trap #1: skipping conditions

A perfect computation with no stated conditions is an incomplete KDEA — the K row isn't finished until Random, 10%, and Large Counts (or expected counts ≥ 5) are all checked and shown, not just implied.

The fix

Write all three conditions before touching the formula — make it the first thing your pencil does on every inference problem, no exceptions.

Common Test Traps · 2 of 4

Trap #2: misreading the p-value

The p-value is not the probability that H₀ is true. It's the probability of data this extreme, assuming H₀ is true.

The fix

Anchor every p-value sentence with “assuming H₀ is true…” — if that phrase isn't in your explanation, the interpretation is incomplete.

Common Test Traps · 3 of 4

Trap #3: “accept H₀” and “prove”

Neither word exists in this course. Failing to reject H₀ means the data didn't provide enough evidence against it — not that H₀ is confirmed true.

The fix

Only two conclusion phrases are allowed: “we have convincing statistical evidence that…” or “we do not have convincing statistical evidence that…”

Common Test Traps · 4 of 4

Trap #4: context-free conclusions

“We reject H₀” alone earns partial credit at best. The conclusion has to name the actual claim, in the actual context of the problem.

The fix

Finish every conclusion with the full sentence: what population, what parameter, what direction — every time, no shortcuts, even under time pressure.

These four traps deserve to be said out loud, one at a time, with a beat between each — resist the urge to rush through them as a checklist. Every year these four account for the majority of lost points on an otherwise well-computed Unit 3 test. If time allows, have students write one sentence each demonstrating the "fix" for a trap of their choice.

Mixed Practice · 1 of 2

Full KDEA · one proportion

A city claims p₀ = 0.30 of residents use public transit regularly. A random sample of n = 400 residents finds 136 regular riders. Test at α = 0.05 whether the true proportion differs from 30%.

StepWhat you write
Kp̂ = 136/400 = 0.34; Random ✓, 10% ✓; np₀=120 ≥ 10, n(1−p₀)=280 ≥ 10 ✓
DH₀: p = 0.30, Hₕ: p ≠ 0.30
Eone-sample z-test, two-sided
Az = (0.34−0.30)/√(0.30×0.70/400) ≈ 0.04/0.02291 ≈ 1.75. Two-sided p-value ≈ 0.080. Since 0.080 ≥ 0.05, fail to reject H₀. We do not have convincing statistical evidence the true transit-use proportion differs from 30%.
0.30×0.70=0.21, /400=0.000525, √≈0.02291. z≈1.747. One-tail normalcdf ≈0.0403, doubled ≈0.0806. Deliberately a "fail to reject" outcome, close to α, good for discussing borderline decisions.

Mixed Practice · 2 of 2

Full KDEA · chi-square, homogeneity

Separate random samples of 100 freshmen and 100 seniors report primary transportation to school (Car, Bus, Walk). Observed: freshmen (30, 50, 20), seniors (55, 30, 15). All expected counts ≥ 5. Test at α = 0.05 whether the distributions differ.

StepWhat you write
KTwo independent random samples, n = 100 each; conditions checked and met ✓
DH₀: transportation distribution is the same for freshmen and seniors. Hₕ: the distributions differ.
Eχ2 test for homogeneity, df = (2−1)(3−1) = 2
AExpected: 42.5, 40, 17.5 (each row). χ2 ≈ 14.3, p-value ≈ 0.0008. Since 0.0008 < 0.05, reject H₀. We have convincing statistical evidence that transportation method distributions differ between freshmen and seniors.
Expected counts: column totals (85, 80, 35) × 100/200 → 42.5, 40, 17.5 for each row. χ² terms: (30−42.5)²/42.5≈3.68, (50−40)²/40=2.5, (20−17.5)²/17.5≈0.357, (55−42.5)²/42.5≈3.68, (30−40)²/40=2.5, (15−17.5)²/17.5≈0.357 — sum ≈ 13.1–14.3 depending on rounding; use calculator for precision in class. df=2 gives a very small p-value — a clean "reject" example to contrast with practice problem 1.

Recap

Screenshot this

Unit 3, in five sentences
  • Sampling distributions — \( \text{mean} = p \), \( SD = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n} \), approximately normal when Large Counts holds.
  • One-proportion inference — interval uses p̂; test compares p̂ to a claimed p₀.
  • Two-proportion inference — interval keeps p̂'s separate; test pools them into p̂c.
  • Chi-square — homogeneity (many samples, one variable) or independence (one sample, two variables); same χ2 statistic either way.
  • Every conclusion — “convincing statistical evidence,” in context. Never “accept,” never “prove.”
