4.4

Tests for a Mean

Formalizing "does the data support the claim?"

4.4 · Hypotheses

H₀: μ = μ₀. Ha: pick a direction.

Exactly the same structure as the proportion tests from Unit 3, with μ in place of p:

HypothesisMeaning
H₀: μ = μ₀the null — "no difference from the claimed value"
Ha: μ ≠ μ₀two-sided — "the mean is different, direction unspecified"
Ha: μ > μ₀ or < μ₀one-sided — only when the question asks about a specific direction

μ₀ is a number from the problem — the claimed or historical value being tested.

Emphasize that H_a's direction comes from the RESEARCH QUESTION, not from which way the sample happened to lean. Students sometimes look at their own x̄ and back into a one-sided test after the fact — that's backwards and it's a real AP scoring deduction (the hypotheses must be set up before looking at whether the data supports them).

4.4 · The Test Statistic

\( t = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}} \)

Same three conditions as the confidence interval, unchanged: Random, Independent (10%), Normal/Large Sample. This is the E in KDEA — the procedure that connects K to D.

Read the formula

The numerator is "how far the sample mean landed from the claim." The denominator is the standard error — "how much x̄ typically bounces around by chance." The ratio answers: how many standard errors away from the claim did we land?

df = n − 1, same as the interval.

The "how many standard errors away" framing is the intuition that carries students through every t-test they'll ever see, not just this one. It's worth pausing on this slide even though it's conceptually simple — a large |t| means the sample landed somewhere unlikely if H₀ were actually true, which is exactly what a small p-value will formalize on the next slide.

4.4 · p-values

The p-value, and how to conclude

The p-value is the probability of getting a test statistic at least as extreme as ours, if H₀ were actually true. Found with a calculator's t-test function or software — never estimated by eye.

If p-value < α

Reject H₀. There is convincing statistical evidence for Ha, stated in context.

If p-value ≥ α

Fail to reject H₀. There is NOT convincing evidence for Ha — NEVER "accept H₀."

α = 0.05 unless the problem states otherwise.

"Accept H₀" is a banned phrase in this classroom, the same as "prove" — a failed test never proves the null is true, it only means the data didn't provide enough evidence against it. This wording distinction is explicitly graded on real AP rubrics; enforce it here so it's automatic by the test.

Worked Example · Setup

The pastelito claim

A Miami bakery chain claims its pastelitos average 320 calories. Nutrition audits show pastelito calorie counts are approximately normally distributed. A food blogger samples n = 16 pastelitos from different locations: x̄ = 336 cal, s = 24 cal.

Test at α = 0.05 whether the true mean differs from the bakery's claim.

Deliberately a small n (16) paired with a stated-normal population, the other branch of the Normal/Large Sample condition from last week's large-n example — worth naming explicitly so students see both valid paths through that condition this unit.

Worked Example · KDEA

Testing μ = 320 calories

StepWhat you write
Kn = 16, x̄ = 336 cal, s = 24 cal. Random: stated random sample. Independent: 16 is nowhere near 10% of all pastelitos made. Normal/Large Sample: population stated approximately normal — condition met despite small n.
Dμ = true mean calories per pastelito. H₀: μ = 320, Ha: μ ≠ 320
Eone-sample t-test: \( t = (\bar{x} - \mu_0)/(s/\sqrt{n}) \), df = 15
At = (336 − 320) / (24/√16) = 16/6 ≈ 2.667
p-value ≈ 0.018 (two-sided, df = 15, calculator)
0.018 < 0.05 → reject H₀

Convincing statistical evidence the true mean calorie count differs from (appears higher than) the claimed 320 calories.

Walk the p-value < α comparison explicitly before stating the conclusion — students often write the correct number but skip the comparison sentence that actually justifies "reject H₀." The context clause ("differs from... the claimed 320 calories") is required; a bare "reject H₀" with no context is an unfinished answer, same rule as last week's confidence interval interpretation.

Checking Your Work

Software gives you the same answer, faster

A one-sample t-test is one line in R or Python — useful for checking your by-hand KDEA work, not a replacement for showing it on an assessment:

Example

scipy.stats.ttest_1samp(sample, popmean=320) in Python, or t.test(sample, mu = 320) in R — both return the same test statistic and p-value you computed by hand.

On the digital exam, a graphing calculator's T-Test function plays the same role.

Keep this brief — the point is that the KDEA table IS what a calculator's T-Test menu or a stats package computes under the hood, not a separate skill. Some students find seeing the "real world" version reassuring that the by-hand process isn't busywork.

4.5–4.6

Two-Sample Sampling Distributions

Same three questions, now for a difference: x̄₁ − x̄₂.

4.5 · The Difference in Means

The sampling distribution of x̄₁ − x̄₂

Shape
Approximately normal if both populations are normal, OR both sample sizes are large — CLT applies to each group separately.
Center
\( \mu_{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2} = \mu_1 - \mu_2 \) — the difference of the two population means, no bias.
Spread
\( \sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2} \) — variances add, not standard deviations.

"Variances add" is the one genuinely new mechanical rule this week — everything else is a doubled version of what you already know.

The variances-add rule surprises students who expect the standard deviations themselves to just add — it's worth deriving intuition rather than asserting it: independent sources of variability combine additively in variance (squared units), which is exactly why the spread formula has squares inside the square root instead of two SDs added directly.

4.6 · Conditions, Doubled

Every condition, checked for BOTH groups

Random
Both samples are random, or both groups came from random assignment in an experiment.
Independent
10% condition for EACH group separately, AND the two samples/groups must be independent of each other — no shared subjects.
Normal / Large Sample
EACH population is normal, or EACH n is large (n₁, n₂ ≥ 30), or each sample's shape is checked separately.

"Independent of each other" is the condition that will matter most next week — it's what separates this from a paired design.

Plant the seed for next week's paired-vs-two-sample distinction here, explicitly, without teaching it yet — "independent of each other" (no shared subjects, no matching) is precisely the condition that a paired design violates. Students who internalize this now will have an easier time with the Week 3 identification problems.

4.6 · Standard Error & df

\( SE = \sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2} \)

We estimate each population variance with its own sample variance, same substitution logic as the one-sample case. The degrees of freedom for two independent samples is uglier — and you will never compute it by hand.

In practice

A calculator's 2-SampTTest or 2-SampTInt, or software like scipy.stats.ttest_ind in Python or t.test(x, y) in R, computes the exact df automatically (the Welch approximation). Trust the technology output — don't try to reproduce the formula from memory.

Full two-sample confidence intervals and tests start next week — today is the sampling distribution underneath them.

Naming that the Welch df formula exists but is never hand-computed removes a common source of exam-day anxiety — students sometimes think they're missing a memorization step that was never actually expected of them. The technology-computes-df norm is standard AP practice, not a shortcut.

Quick Check · Turn & Talk · 5 minutes

Independent samples, or not?

With your table

For each scenario, decide: are these two independent samples, or is something else going on?

  1. Test scores from 30 randomly chosen students in Period 2 vs. 28 randomly chosen students in Period 5.
  2. Resting heart rate for 20 people, measured before and after a caffeine dose.
Answer for guiding circulation: (1) independent — different students, no shared subjects, this is the two-sample case we're building today. (2) NOT independent — same 20 people measured twice, which is the paired case Week 3 will formally introduce. Don't over-explain (2) yet; the goal here is just to notice the difference exists, not to teach the fix.

Common Traps

Two errors to watch for this week

z vs. t, again

Still about whether σ is known, not the size of n. Every problem this unit uses t.

Hand-computing df

For two independent samples, don't try to derive the Welch df by hand — report what the calculator or software gives you.

Keep reinforcing z vs t weekly — repetition is the fix for this specific confusion. The df trap is new this week specifically because the two-sample formula is genuinely more complex than df = n−1; naming "don't hand-compute it" as the correct move (not a workaround) prevents wasted study time.
