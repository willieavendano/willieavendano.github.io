Where We Are

You can summarize one group. Now compare groups.

Week 3 gave you the five-number summary for a single distribution. This week turns that into a graph (the boxplot), then uses it to compare two or more groups — which is where comparative investigative questions from Week 1 actually get answered.

Comparing groups is the single most common FRQ setup on the AP Statistics exam. Get this week right.

The claim in the chip is not an exaggeration for effect — comparative distribution questions (two boxplots, two histograms, side-by-side summary stats) appear on nearly every released AP free-response set. This week's skills are worth extra time investment relative to the calendar.

Topic 1.8

Boxplots

The five-number summary, drawn as a picture.

1.8 · 1 of 3

Building a boxplot

Five numbers from last week become five landmarks on a number line:

Worked example · Min=62, Q1=71, Med=76.5, Q3=82, Max=97
  1. Draw a box from Q1 to Q3 (the middle 50% of the data)
  2. Draw a line inside the box at the median
  3. Draw whiskers from the box out to Min and Max

Box width = IQR. A wider box means a more spread-out middle 50%.

This is the exact dataset from last week's KDEA worked example — say that connection out loud. Students should recognize the numbers before they see the picture; that recognition is the proof that boxplots aren't a new topic, just a new way to draw an old summary.

1.8 · 2 of 3

Modified boxplots: showing outliers

A modified boxplot stops the whiskers at the 1.5×IQR fences and plots any formal outliers as separate dots beyond them.

Why it matters

A plain boxplot hides outliers inside a stretched-out whisker. A modified boxplot makes them visible — which is the version you should default to drawing on the exam unless told otherwise.

Reinforce that "modified" is the default expectation on the AP exam, not an optional variant — say this explicitly since some textbooks present the plain boxplot first and modified second, which can leave the impression that plain is the standard.

1.8 · 3 of 3

Reading a boxplot for shape

↔
Symmetric
median centered in the box, whiskers roughly equal length
→
Skewed right
median pushed toward Q1; long whisker/outliers on the high side
←
Skewed left
median pushed toward Q3; long whisker/outliers on the low side

A boxplot can suggest shape, but it cannot show modality (unimodal vs. bimodal) — that information is lost. Pair a boxplot with a histogram when modality matters.

The modality limitation is a favorite AP multiple-choice trap: "which graph would you use to show a bimodal distribution?" A boxplot is the wrong answer every time — it can look identical for a unimodal and a bimodal dataset that share the same five-number summary.

Quick Check · 5 minutes

Sketch it

On your whiteboard

Given Min=10, Q1=20, Med=35, Q3=38, Max=42: sketch the modified boxplot. What does the shape suggest?

Answer: the box is narrow (Q1-Q3 = 18) with the median (35) sitting close to Q3 (38), and a long lower whisker from 10 to 20 — this suggests left skew. Check the 1.5×IQR fences too: IQR=18, lower fence = 20-27=-7, upper fence = 38+27=65 — nothing here is a formal outlier.

Topic 1.9

Comparing Distributions

Side by side — same axis, same scale, one honest comparison.

1.9 · 1 of 3

Putting two groups on one axis

Comparing distributions means the same graph types from Weeks 2–3, drawn on the same scale so the comparison is fair:

▤
Side-by-side boxplots
the workhorse comparison graph — fast to read, built from familiar five-number summaries
⋮
Parallel dotplots
stacked dotplots, same scale — good for smaller datasets
⇄
Back-to-back stemplots
shared stems, leaves grow in opposite directions

Different axis scales between two graphs = an unfair, invalid comparison.

The "same scale" requirement is the categorical-vs-quantitative-graph mistake's cousin from Week 2 — another easy-to-miss construction rule that costs credit on an otherwise correct graph. Flag it with the same seriousness.

1.9 · 2 of 3

Comparative language — this is the AP-reader trap

Describing two groups separately is not the same as comparing them. A comparison needs explicit comparative words.

Weak — loses credit

“Group A has a median of 75. Group B has a median of 68.”

Strong — full credit

“Group A's median (75) is higher than Group B's median (68), suggesting Group A typically scored better.”

This is one of THE most-cited AP Statistics reader complaints: students report two numbers side by side and never actually compare them with words like "greater than," "less than," "more variable than." The rubric explicitly looks for a comparative word, not just two correct numbers. Drill this relentlessly — it costs points every single year nationally.

1.9 · 3 of 3 · Worked Example

Full comparison, KDEA style

StepWhat you write
Ktwo boxplots: Section A (Med=82, IQR=6) and Section B (Med=74, IQR=15)
Da full SOCV comparison of the two sections' quiz scores
ESOCV, applied comparatively (shape, outliers, center, variability — each with a comparison word)
A“Section A's scores are higher on average (median 82 vs. 74) and less variable (IQR 6 vs. 15) than Section B's. Section B shows a wider spread of performance, while Section A's scores cluster more tightly near the top.”
Point out every comparative word in the A row is doing work: "higher," "less variable," "wider," "more tightly." A version of this answer with the same two numbers but none of those words would score lower on the actual AP rubric — the comparison has to be stated, not implied.

Turn and Talk · 10 minutes

Compare two boxplots

With your partner — boxplots on the handout

Write a full comparison: shape, outliers, center, and variability, each with an explicit comparative word. No bare numbers without a sentence.

Circulate specifically listening for comparative language, not just correct numbers — this is the skill under the microscope this week. If a pair reports two medians with no comparison word, stop them and ask "so what? which is bigger, and what does that mean?"

Topic 1.10

The Investigative Question, Revisited

Back to Week 1: did the data actually answer it?

1.10 · 1 of 3

Does the data actually answer the question?

Week 1's investigative questions were the starting point. Now, with real graphs and statistics in hand, we check whether the evidence actually supports a conclusion — or whether the study's design limits what we can claim.

Ask
  • Does the sample represent the population the question is really about?
  • Is the comparison fair (same scale, similar conditions)?
  • Are we describing a difference, or claiming one variable causes another?
The causation question is a deliberate plant for Week 6's experimental design (1.13) and for inference later in the year — don't resolve it fully today, just flag that "the data shows a difference" and "X causes Y" are not the same claim. That distinction gets its full treatment when random assignment enters the picture.

1.10 · 2 of 3

Full circle: start with the question

Investigative question (Week 1 style)

“Do students who take the bus have longer commutes than students who drive?” — comparative.

Every unit starts here. Everything from Weeks 2–4 exists to answer a question like this one.

This is the deliberate "aha" setup of the unit — the exact same kind of question from Week 1's classification activity, now about to get a real answer. Pause here before advancing; the payoff is the next slide.

1.10 · 3 of 3

Full circle: the chain to an answer

The chain

Variable (commute time, quantitative) → graph (side-by-side boxplots) → SOCV comparison (bus commutes higher median, more variable) → answer, in context: “Yes — in this sample, bus riders had a higher median commute time than drivers.”

Every arrow in that chain is a skill from Weeks 1–4. This is the whole unit, end to end.

Walk this slowly. Every single piece has already been taught; the only new idea is seeing the full pipeline in one place. This is also good rehearsal for Week 6's concept-map review slide.

Practice · Mini Research Design · 10 minutes

Design your own comparison

With your table
  1. Write a comparative investigative question about two groups in this school (e.g., grade levels, class sections, walkers vs. drivers).
  2. Name the variable you'd measure and its type.
  3. Name the graph you'd use to compare the groups.
  4. Predict, before collecting any data, what a “strong” comparative conclusion sentence would sound like.
This activity is a rehearsal for the capstone data project later in the year — the habit of planning the analysis pipeline (question → variable → graph → comparison) before touching any data is exactly the workflow the spring capstone project will require independently.
