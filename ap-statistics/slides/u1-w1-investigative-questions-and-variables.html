Welcome

Statistics is investigation, not just numbers

Every unit this year starts the same way: a real question, a real dataset, and a decision that actually matters. The math is the tool — the question is the point.

  • Does a new drug actually work?
  • Is a coin fair?
  • Which studying method helps more?
  • Is a survey result believable?

This year, you learn to answer questions like these — and defend your answer.

Set the tone for the whole course here: this is not "math with different symbols," it's a methodology for making defensible claims from data. Students who arrive expecting a calculation-only class need this reframe on day one. Keep it conversational — this is a hook, not a lecture.

KDEA · The Statistics Mapping

Same four letters. New rows.

StepIn AP Statistics, this means…
Kthe data, design, and conditions you're given
Dthe parameter, probability, or claim you're after
Ethe procedure — formula, interval, or test
Acompute AND conclude — always in context

Every KDEA table this year ends the same way: a number, tied back to the real-world question.

The D row will feel abstract in Unit 1 — we don't have formal parameters or claims yet, just summary statistics and descriptions. That's fine; D is still "what am I actually trying to report," even when it's just a mean or a shape description. The mapping gets sharper once Unit 3 introduces formal claims and hypotheses.

The Core Dogma

Context travels with the conclusion.

An answer without context is like an answer without units — it does not count. This is statistics, not just math.

This is the single most enforced rule in this classroom for the rest of the year — say it exactly like this every time it comes up. A correct number with no context sentence gets zero credit on any KDEA problem, full stop, starting today. This is the AP-reader-style failure mode we will fight all year: students who compute perfectly and then write nothing about what the number means for the actual question.

Topic 1.1

Investigative Questions

Not every question can be answered with data. The good ones can.

1.1 · 1 of 3

What makes a question “investigative”?

A statistical, or investigative, question expects variability in the answer — it can't be settled by looking up one fact.

Not investigative

“How many students are in this class?” — one fixed number, no data collection needed.

Investigative

“How much sleep do students at this school get on a school night?” — answers vary person to person; you need data.

The test students will keep failing at first: they write a question that has a single correct lookup answer and call it "investigative." Push them to ask "would two different people, or two different samples, give me two different numbers?" If yes, it's investigative.

1.1 · 2 of 3

Three flavors of investigative question

1
Descriptive
What does this one group look like? — “What's the typical commute time for students here?”
2
Comparative
How do two or more groups differ? — “Do 9th and 12th graders sleep different amounts?”
3
Relationship
Are two variables connected? — “Is study time related to test score?”

Every question you write this year should name which flavor it is — it drives everything downstream.

This classification isn't trivia — it previews the whole course roadmap. Descriptive → Unit 1 (today). Comparative → Unit 1 comparing distributions (Week 4) and Unit 3/4 two-sample inference. Relationship → Unit 5 regression. Naming the type now means students recognize the thread when it resurfaces months later.

1.1 · 3 of 3 · Turn and Talk · 4 minutes

Classify the question

With your table

For each question: is it investigative or not? If it is, is it descriptive, comparative, or relationship?

  1. “What's the average shoe size in this class?”
  2. “What year is it?”
  3. “Do students who eat breakfast score higher on morning quizzes than students who don't?”
  4. “Is there a relationship between screen time and hours of sleep?”
Answers: (1) investigative, descriptive. (2) not investigative — one fixed fact. (3) investigative, comparative. (4) investigative, relationship. Cold-call one table per question rather than going around in order — keeps everyone tracking all four.

Topic 1.2

Variables

Every investigative question is really a question about a variable.

1.2 · 1 of 3

Categorical vs. quantitative

Categorical — puts an individual into a group or category:

  • Eye color, favorite subject, pass/fail
  • Grade level, blood type, yes/no survey response

Quantitative — a number you can do math on:

  • Height, test score, number of siblings
  • Commute time, temperature

Test: can you sensibly average it? Averaging eye colors is nonsense — averaging test scores isn't.

The "can you average it" test is the fastest tiebreaker for edge cases. Zip code and phone number look like numbers but are actually categorical — you'd never average two zip codes and get a meaningful answer. Flag this explicitly; it's a classic AP multiple-choice trap.

1.2 · 2 of 3

Quantitative splits again: discrete vs. continuous

#
Discrete
Countable, usually whole numbers — number of siblings, number of pets, number of AP classes
≈
Continuous
Measured, can take any value in a range — height, weight, time, temperature

This distinction matters later: it decides which graphs make sense (Week 2) and, much later, which probability distribution applies (Unit 2).

Don't over-invest here — the discrete/continuous split matters more for its downstream consequences than as a definition to memorize. Flag the forward link to Unit 2 (discrete random variables vs. continuous, e.g. the normal distribution) so it isn't a surprise later.

1.2 · 3 of 3

Quick check

On your whiteboard
  1. Number of pets owned — categorical or quantitative? If quantitative, discrete or continuous?
  2. Favorite lunch period — categorical or quantitative?
  3. Time to run a mile, in seconds — categorical or quantitative? If quantitative, discrete or continuous?
Answers: (1) quantitative, discrete. (2) categorical. (3) quantitative, continuous. Watch for students who call "time in seconds" discrete because it's usually reported as a whole number — the underlying quantity is continuous even when the recorded value gets rounded.

Topic 1.3

One Categorical Variable

Tables and summary statistics — the first real data today.

1.3 · 1 of 3

Worked example: frequency table

A survey asked 40 students their favorite way to get to school: Car, Bus, Walk, or Bike.

StepWhat you write
K40 students, 4 categories: Car (18), Bus (12), Walk (7), Bike (3)
Da table that shows how the 40 responses are distributed across categories
Efrequency table — one row per category, one column for the count
ACar most common (18 of 40); Bike least common (3 of 40) — most students in this sample drive or ride the bus to school
The A row is the whole point of this slide: a table of counts alone isn't an answer, the sentence that follows it is. Model writing that sentence out loud every single time — this is the "context travels with the conclusion" rule from the KDEA divider, in its first real use.

1.3 · 2 of 3

Relative frequency: counts become proportions

A raw count doesn't compare across surveys of different sizes. A relative frequency (proportion or percentage) does.

CategoryCountRelative frequency
Car1818/40 = 0.45 → 45%
Bus1212/40 = 0.30 → 30%
Walk77/40 = 0.175 → 17.5%
Bike33/40 = 0.075 → 7.5%

Relative frequencies always sum to 1 (or 100%) — a fast self-check on your own work.

The "sums to 1" check catches arithmetic errors before a student ever turns in a table. Make them add the column out loud once. This habit pays off directly in Unit 2 when relative frequency becomes an estimate of probability.

1.3 · 3 of 3

Summarizing one categorical variable

Categorical data has no mean or median — you can't average “Bus.” Instead:

  • Mode — the most frequent category (here: Car)
  • Proportion in a category — e.g., “45% of this sample drives to school”

Every summary claim still needs context: which category, what proportion, of what group.

Students coming from a "everything gets a mean" mindset will try to average category codes (Car=1, Bus=2, ...) — shut this down explicitly. It's meaningless because the category order is arbitrary. Mode and proportion are the only legitimate "center" for categorical data.

Practice · 6 minutes

Build your own table

On your whiteboard

Poll your table group: “What's your favorite season?” (Summer, Fall, Winter, Spring)

  1. Build a frequency table from your group's responses.
  2. Convert to relative frequencies. Do they sum to 1?
  3. Write one sentence naming the mode, in context.
Small group sizes (4-6 students) will produce lumpy, unrepresentative distributions — that's a feature, not a bug, for a first activity: it sets up next week's conversation about sample size and variability naturally, without forcing the topic today.
