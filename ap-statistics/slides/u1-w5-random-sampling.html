Where We Are

Good analysis can't fix bad data

Every SOCV description and every comparison from the last four weeks assumed the data was collected well. This week asks: what does “collected well” actually require?

The best statistical analysis in the world can't rescue data from a badly chosen sample.

This reframe matters — students sometimes think "more sophisticated math" fixes weak data. It doesn't. A perfectly computed mean from a biased sample is still wrong. Say this plainly; it's the entire thesis of the week.

Topic 1.11

Why Random Sampling

If you can't ask everyone, you have to ask the right slice of them.

1.11 · 1 of 4

Population, sample, and the words that separate them

P
Population
the entire group you actually want to know about
S
Sample
the subset you actually collect data from
F
Sampling frame
the actual list you sample from — ideally, this equals the population
θ / x̄
Parameter vs. statistic
a parameter describes the population (usually unknown); a statistic describes the sample (what you actually calculate)
Parameter vs. statistic is a distinction that gets tested for the rest of the year — every confidence interval and hypothesis test from Unit 3 onward hinges on "we don't know the parameter, so we estimate it with a statistic." Plant the vocabulary now even though the formal notation (p, p̂, μ, x̄) doesn't show up until Unit 3.

1.11 · 2 of 4

Simple random sample (SRS)

Every possible group of a given size has an equal chance of being selected. This is the gold standard — every other method is judged against it.

Worked example

To sample 25 students from a school of 500: number every student 001–500, then use a random number generator (or R's sample()) to pick 25 unique numbers. Whoever holds those numbers is in the sample — no substitutions, no “close enough.”

Emphasize "no substitutions" — a classic error is picking a random number, finding that student is absent or hard to reach, and grabbing a convenient replacement instead. That single swap can reintroduce bias and breaks the "equal chance" guarantee the whole method depends on.

1.11 · 3 of 4

Beyond SRS: other sampling designs

St
Stratified
split the population into groups (strata) first, then SRS within each — guarantees representation of every group
Cl
Cluster
split into naturally occurring clusters, randomly pick whole clusters — cheaper, but less precise
Sy
Systematic
pick a random start, then take every k-th individual from a list

Stratified samples every group. Cluster samples whole groups. Don't mix these up on the exam.

Stratified vs. cluster is the most confused pair on this slide — the fastest tiebreaker: in stratified sampling you sample WITHIN every group; in cluster sampling you sample entire groups and skip the rest. Draw both as diagrams (grid of dots, some circled within every row vs. whole rows circled) if a whiteboard is handy.

1.11 · 4 of 4 · Quick Check

Name the sampling method

On your whiteboard
  1. A principal randomly picks 3 whole homerooms and surveys every student in each.
  2. A researcher assigns every voter a number and uses a computer to randomly select 200.
  3. A quality inspector checks every 50th item off the factory line.
  4. A pollster ensures the sample includes proportional numbers of every age group, sampled randomly within each.
Answers: (1) cluster. (2) simple random sample. (3) systematic. (4) stratified. If time is short, cold-call one per row rather than reviewing all four — the goal is quick recognition, not deep discussion here (that comes with the bias examples next).

1.11 · Why It Matters

Randomization is what makes inference possible later

Every confidence interval and hypothesis test in Units 3 and 4 starts with the same assumption: the data came from a properly randomized sample. Skip that step, and none of the later math is valid — no matter how sophisticated the formula.

Random sampling isn't a formality. It's the foundation everything else in this course stands on.

This is a genuine foreshadowing slide, not hype — every AP Statistics inference procedure literally has "random" as one of its named conditions to check before proceeding. Students will see this exact idea again by name in Unit 3's confidence interval conditions.

Topic 1.12

When Sampling Goes Wrong

Bias — a systematic tilt away from the truth, no sample size can fix.

The "no sample size can fix it" point deserves emphasis here — a common misconception is that a bigger biased sample is better than a smaller unbiased one. It's the opposite: a huge biased sample just gives you a very precise wrong answer.

1.12 · 1 of 4

Four ways bias sneaks in

Se
Selection bias
the method used systematically favors certain individuals over others
Un
Undercoverage
some part of the population has little or no chance of being included at all
Nr
Nonresponse bias
people selected don't respond, and non-responders differ systematically from responders
Re
Response bias
people respond, but the wording of a question or presence of an interviewer skews the answer
These four are the recurring cast of the AP exam's "identify the bias" free-response items. The distinction between undercoverage (never had a chance to be picked) and nonresponse (was picked but didn't answer) is the one students blur most — hammer that timing difference.

1.12 · 2 of 4

When the frame doesn't match the population

Undercoverage often comes down to a bad sampling frame — the list you sample from silently leaves people out.

Real-world example

A survey conducted only by landline phone call systematically undercovers younger adults, who are far more likely to be cell-only — the sampling frame (the landline directory) never matched the population (all adults) in the first place.

Ask students to generate their own modern examples — social-media-only surveys undercover people without accounts or without that platform; school-email surveys undercover families without reliable internet access. Concrete, current examples land harder than the textbook landline case alone.

1.12 · 3 of 4 · Worked Example

Diagnose the bias

Scenario

A local news station asks viewers to text in “YES” or “NO” to answer: “Should the city raise taxes to fund new parks?” 2,000 people respond.

StepWhat you write
Kvoluntary text-in poll, 2,000 self-selected respondents
Dis this sample representative of the city's actual opinion?
Echeck for selection/response/nonresponse/undercoverage bias
Athis is voluntary response bias — people with strong opinions (especially opposed) are far more likely to text in than people who don't care much. 2,000 responses is a large number, but it does not make the sample representative.
The "2,000 is a large number, but..." line is worth saying slowly — this is the same "sample size doesn't fix bias" idea from the divider slide, now demonstrated concretely. Voluntary response is arguably the single most exam-tested bias type; make sure this scenario sticks.

1.12 · 4 of 4

Designing around bias

  • Use a sampling frame that actually matches the population you care about
  • Use randomization (SRS, stratified, systematic) instead of convenience or volunteers
  • Word questions neutrally — avoid leading language (“Don't you agree that…?”)
  • Follow up on non-responders when possible, to check whether they differ from responders

None of these guarantee a perfect sample — they just reduce the systematic tilt.

Note the honest hedge in the chip: even a well-designed SRS still has sampling variability (covered formally in Unit 3's sampling distributions) — the goal here is eliminating bias (systematic tilt), not eliminating all error, which is impossible.

Worked Example · KDEA

Design a sampling plan

StepWhat you write
Kinvestigative question: “What proportion of students at this school walk to school?” School has 900 students across 6 grade levels, roughly equal size
Da sampling plan that gives every student a fair, known chance of selection
Estratified sample by grade level (guarantees every grade is represented), SRS within each stratum
Anumber students within each grade, randomly select a proportional number from each using a random number generator — e.g., 25 students per grade for a sample of 150. No volunteers, no convenience picks.
This example deliberately reuses the stratified-sampling definition from earlier so students see the vocabulary applied to their own plan, not just a textbook scenario. The "no volunteers, no convenience picks" line in A is the explicit contrast with the voluntary-response scenario two slides ago.

Turn and Talk · 8 minutes

Critique the study

With your partner

A college wants to know how satisfied students are with the cafeteria. They post a QR code survey on tables in the cafeteria during lunch.

  1. What's the population? The sampling frame?
  2. Name the bias (or biases) at work.
  3. Propose one concrete fix.
Answers to guide discussion: population = all students; sampling frame = students who eat in the cafeteria during that lunch period and notice/scan the QR code — undercovers students who skip the cafeteria or eat elsewhere, plus voluntary response bias among who bothers to scan. A fix: randomly select students in advance from the full enrollment list and follow up directly.

Tools of the Trade

Let the computer randomize

True randomness is hard to fake by hand — humans are bad at picking "random" numbers. Use software instead.

R
sample()
draws a true SRS from a numbered list in one line
SK
StatKey randomization tools
visualizes what a random sample looks like, run after run
Worth a quick demo if time allows — running sample() a few times and watching the selected numbers change reinforces "equal chance" in a way a static definition doesn't. This also previews the resampling/randomization tools that Unit 2 leans on for simulation.
