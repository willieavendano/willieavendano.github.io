Where We Are

Last week: tables. This week: pictures.

You can already classify a variable and build a frequency table. Today we turn that same data into a graph — and the variable type decides which graph is even valid.

  • Categorical → bar charts, pie charts
  • Quantitative → dotplots, histograms, stemplots

Mixing these up — a histogram of eye colors, say — is a hard error on the exam.

The "mixing these up is a hard error" line is worth dwelling on for a beat. A surprising number of students will try to histogram categorical data because it's the graph they remember best. Flag it now so it isn't a mystery deduction later.

Topic 1.4

Graphs for One Categorical Variable

Bar charts and pie charts — same data, different stories.

1.4 · 1 of 3

Bar charts

One bar per category. Bar height (or length) shows the count or relative frequency — and the bars don't touch, because the categories aren't a continuous scale.

Worked example

Favorite way to school (Week 1's data): Car 18, Bus 12, Walk 7, Bike 3. A bar chart shows four separated bars, tallest for Car, shortest for Bike — the shape of the data is visible at a glance, without reading a single number.

The "bars don't touch" detail is the single most-forgotten rule when students later meet histograms (where bars DO touch, because the x-axis is continuous). Plant the contrast now so next section's histogram slide feels like a deliberate exception, not an arbitrary new rule.

1.4 · 2 of 3

Pie charts — and the y-axis trap

Pie charts show the whole (100%) sliced into categories — useful when the “share of the whole” framing matters more than raw counts.

Best with a small number of categories; a pie with 12 slices is unreadable.

Common trap

A bar chart whose y-axis doesn't start at 0 exaggerates differences between bars — a small real difference can be made to look huge. Always check the axis before trusting a bar chart you didn't make.

This is a real AP-reader failure mode in reverse: students who correctly compute everything but never learn to critique a graph's honesty. This trap also has genuine media-literacy value — point out that this exact trick shows up in advertising and news graphics constantly.

1.4 · 3 of 3 · Turn and Talk · 4 minutes

Bar or pie?

With your table

For each dataset, would you reach for a bar chart, a pie chart, both, or neither? Defend your choice in one sentence.

  1. Market share of 4 phone brands (must sum to 100%)
  2. Number of students per grade level, 6th–12th (7 categories)
  3. Average test scores of 5 different classes
Answers: (1) pie chart works well — natural "share of 100%" framing, few categories. (2) bar chart — 7 categories starts to strain a pie, and bars make comparing exact counts easier. (3) neither — these are quantitative summary values (means), not one categorical variable's distribution; a bar chart of averages is a common but different chart type, not this topic yet.

Topic 1.5

Graphs for One Quantitative Variable

Now every individual data point has a real numeric value.

1.5 · 1 of 4

Dotplots

One dot per data point, stacked above its value on a number line. Best for small datasets — every individual value stays visible.

Worked example

Quiz scores out of 10 for 12 students: 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 8, 9, 9, 9, 9, 10, 10. A dotplot stacks two dots above 6, one above 7… you can literally count the stack.

Dotplots are the most "honest" graph in the unit — nothing is grouped or hidden, which makes them a good first stop before histograms introduce binning choices. Once class sizes exceed ~30-40 points, dotplots get cluttered; that's the natural bridge into histograms.

1.5 · 2 of 4

Histograms

Data gets grouped into equal-width bins; bar height shows how many values fall in each bin. Bars touch — the x-axis is a continuous scale, not separate categories.

Worked example

40 commute times (minutes), binned into 0–10, 10–20, 20–30, 30–40: counts 6, 19, 11, 4. The tallest bar (10–20 min) shows where most students' commutes fall.

Bin width is a choice — too wide hides structure, too narrow looks like noise.

The bin-width tradeoff is worth demonstrating live if there's time: the same dataset can look unimodal with wide bins and lumpy/multimodal with narrow bins. This is exactly the kind of "same data, different story" idea that recurs with the y-axis trap from the bar chart slide.

1.5 · a note on scale

Relative frequency histograms

Swap raw counts for proportions on the y-axis, and the shape stays identical — but now two datasets of different sizes can be compared fairly.

Why it matters

A histogram of 40 commute times and a histogram of 400 commute times have very different bar heights in counts — but nearly identical bar heights in relative frequency, if the two groups behave similarly. This is the version we'll reach for when comparing groups of different sizes in Week 4.

Keep this brief — it's a rescaling, not a new graph type. The forward link to Week 4 (comparing distributions) is the reason this slide exists at all; don't let it turn into a full re-teach of relative frequency from Week 1.

1.5 · 3 of 4

Stem-and-leaf plots

A histogram's shape, built from the actual digits — so no information is lost the way it is when data gets grouped into bins.

Worked example · test scores: 72, 75, 81, 83, 84, 88, 91, 95 
7 | 2 5
8 | 1 3 4 8
9 | 1 5

Stem = tens digit, leaf = ones digit. Turned sideways, it's already a histogram shape.

Stemplots are most useful for small-to-medium datasets where you want histogram-style shape AND the ability to read back exact values (e.g., "was there really an 81 and an 84, or just a bar labeled '80s'?"). Back-to-back stemplots for comparing two groups get their real workout in Week 4.

Practice · 10 minutes

Build a histogram from real data

On graph paper, individually

Using the 25 heights (in inches) handed out on your worksheet:

  1. Choose a bin width and build a histogram.
  2. Compare with a neighbor who chose a different bin width.
  3. Does the overall shape survive the different choice, or change?
The neighbor-comparison step is the actual point of this activity — it's a concrete, hands-on demonstration that bin width is a real analytical choice, not a mechanical step with one right answer. Circulate and ask pairs whether their two histograms "tell the same story."

Turn and Talk · 5 minutes

Sketch the shape from context

With your partner — sketch a rough dotplot or histogram shape (don't overthink the numbers)
  1. Ages of people running a marathon
  2. Household incomes in a large city
  3. Scores on an easy quiz most students aced
This is a preview of Week 3's shape vocabulary (symmetric, skewed) without naming it yet — students are building intuition before the formal vocabulary lands. Don't correct shape names today; just ask "why did you draw it that way?" and bank the reasoning for next week.

Watch For This

Four mistakes that break a graph

×
Touching bars, categorical data
bar charts get gaps between bars — touching bars imply a continuous scale that isn't there
×
Unequal bin widths
a histogram with mismatched bin widths makes bar heights impossible to compare fairly
×
Too many pie slices
past 5–6 categories, a pie chart stops being readable — switch to a bar chart
×
Y-axis that skips 0
exaggerates differences between bars — always check the axis first
This is the "gotcha" slide before the recap — worth a quick verbal walkthrough rather than silent reading. These four are the most common ways a technically-correct-looking graph earns no credit on an AP free-response graph-construction item.
