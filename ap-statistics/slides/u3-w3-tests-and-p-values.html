Why This Matters

“Is this just random chance?”

A coach claims a new training program improves free-throw percentage. A factory claims its defect rate is 2%. A significance test is the formal version of asking: is the difference we see in our sample real, or is it just the sampling variability we'd expect anyway?

Today's job: set up the question correctly. Next week: answer it and act on the answer.

Setting Up a Test

H₀ and Hₕ

Every test starts with two competing statements about the population.

This divider marks the start of new vocabulary — spend real time on hypothesis-writing before touching any arithmetic. A test set up on the wrong hypotheses can't be salvaged by good computation.

Hypothesis Tests · 1 of 5

The null and the alternative

Null hypothesis, H₀

The claimed or status-quo value. Always written as an equality: H₀: p = p₀. We assume this is true unless the data gives strong evidence otherwise.

Alternative hypothesis, Hₕ

What we suspect might be true instead: Hₕ: p ≠ p₀, p > p₀, or p < p₀.

p₀ (“p-naught”) is the specific number being tested — always given in the problem.

Hypothesis Tests · 2 of 5

One-sided or two-sided?

>
One-sided, greater
Hₕ: p > p₀ — “increased,” “improved,” “more than”
<
One-sided, less
Hₕ: p < p₀ — “decreased,” “fewer than”
≠
Two-sided
Hₕ: p ≠ p₀ — “different from,” “changed” (direction not specified)

The wording of the research question decides the direction — read it carefully before writing Hₕ.

Students often default to two-sided out of caution. Push them to read the claim's exact wording: "improves" or "increases" is one-sided (>); "changes" or "is different" is two-sided (≠). This choice directly affects how the p-value gets computed later this week, so it's worth the emphasis now.

Hypothesis Tests · 3 of 5

The conditions, one more variation

R
Random
Random sample or randomized experiment, stated explicitly.
10%
Independence
Sample size no more than 10% of the population.
LC
Large Counts
np₀ ≥ 10 and n(1−p₀) ≥ 10 — using p₀, not p̂, because we're assuming H₀ is true.

The only change from a confidence interval: Large Counts checks p₀ here, not p̂.

This is a genuinely subtle and commonly missed distinction: confidence intervals use p̂ for Large Counts (because we have no p to plug in), while tests use p₀ (because the entire test is conducted "assuming H₀ is true," and H₀ hands us an exact value to use). Say this contrast out loud explicitly.

Hypothesis Tests · 4 of 5

The test statistic: how far, in standard deviations?

A test statistic measures how far the observed p̂ is from the hypothesized p₀, measured in standard deviations — not in raw units.

Why standardize?

“0.05 away from p₀” means something very different with n = 30 versus n = 3000. Standardizing puts every test on the same z-scale, so we can compare it to the normal curve.

Hypothesis Tests · 5 of 5

The formula

One-sample z-test statistic for a proportion

\( z = \dfrac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}} \)

The numerator is how far off the data is from the claim. The denominator is the standard deviation we'd expect from sampling variability alone, if H₀ were true.

A large |z| means the data is far from what H₀ predicts — suspicious for the null.

P-Values

How surprising is this z, really?

The p-value turns a z-score into a probability.

This divider is the second half of the week's content — students often treat the test statistic (a number on a z-scale) and the p-value (a probability) as the same thing. They're related but distinct: z measures distance, the p-value measures how unusual that distance is.

P-Values · 1 of 3

The definition, word for word

P-value

The probability of getting a sample statistic as extreme or more extreme than the one observed, assuming H₀ is true.

A small p-value says: “if H₀ really were true, a result this extreme would be unusual.” That's evidence against H₀ — not proof it's false.

Have students say this definition aloud, slowly, more than once. Every word is load-bearing: "as extreme or more extreme" (not just this exact value), and "assuming H₀ is true" (the whole calculation lives inside a hypothetical world where the null is correct).

P-Values · 2 of 3

What a p-value is NOT

Wrong

“The p-value is the probability that H₀ is true.” A p-value says nothing about the probability of any hypothesis — H₀ is either true or false, full stop; it has no probability.

Right

The p-value is the probability of the data (or more extreme data), computed assuming H₀ is true. It describes the evidence, not the hypothesis.

This single confusion costs more AP points than almost any other misconception in the course.

Worth a direct callback here to the confidence-interval misconception from last week — both errors share the same root cause: treating a probability about a procedure/data as if it were a probability about a fixed parameter or a fixed hypothesis. Naming that pattern explicitly can help it stick.

P-Values · 3 of 3

Worked example · testing a factory's claim

A factory claims its defect rate is p₀ = 0.04. In a random sample of n = 300 parts, 18 are defective. Is there evidence the true defect rate is higher?

StepWhat you write
Kp̂ = 18/300 = 0.06, n = 300, p₀ = 0.04. Random ✓ (stated); 10%: 300 ≤ 10% of all parts ✓; Large Counts: np₀ = 12 ≥ 10, n(1−p₀) = 288 ≥ 10 ✓
DH₀: p = 0.04   Hₕ: p > 0.04, where p = the true defect rate for all parts
Eone-sample z-test: \( z = (\hat{p} - p_0)/\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n} \)
Az = (0.06 − 0.04)/√(0.04 × 0.96/300) ≈ 0.02/0.01131 ≈ 1.77. P-value = P(Z > 1.77) ≈ 0.038.

A p-value of ≈ 0.038 means: if the true defect rate really were 4%, a sample this far above it would happen only about 3.8% of the time.

Walk the standard error arithmetic: 0.04×0.96 = 0.0384, /300 ≈ 0.000128, √ ≈ 0.01131. z ≈ 1.77. For the p-value, use the standard normal table or calculator normalcdf: P(Z > 1.77) ≈ 0.0384, rounds to ≈ 0.038. We deliberately stop before a decision here — comparing this p-value to α and writing the conclusion sentence is next week's "carrying out the test."

Visualizing It

One-sided vs two-sided p-values

One-sided (> or <)

The p-value is the area in one tail beyond your z — the tail that matches the direction of Hₕ.

Two-sided (≠)

The p-value is the area in both tails — find one tail, then double it.

Forgetting to double for a two-sided test is one of the most common arithmetic misses on this topic.

A calculator's normalcdf or R's pnorm() finds either tail directly — worth showing both the table-lookup method (needed for a paper test) and the technology shortcut (used in real analysis) side by side if time allows.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 12 minutes

Set up the test, find the p-value

With your partner

A cereal company claims exactly p₀ = 0.20 of boxes contain a prize. A random sample of n = 150 boxes finds 21 with a prize. Is there evidence the true proportion differs from 20%?

  1. Write H₀ and Hₕ.
  2. Check all three conditions.
  3. Compute the test statistic z.
  4. Find the p-value (remember: two-sided).
p̂ = 21/150 = 0.14. H₀: p = 0.20, Hₐ: p ≠ 0.20. Large Counts: np₀ = 30 ≥ 10, n(1−p₀) = 120 ≥ 10 ✓. z = (0.14 − 0.20)/√(0.20×0.80/150) = −0.06/0.0327 ≈ −1.84. One-tail area ≈ 0.033, doubled for two-sided ≈ 0.066. Watch for groups that forget to double — flag it as the most common miss on this problem type.
