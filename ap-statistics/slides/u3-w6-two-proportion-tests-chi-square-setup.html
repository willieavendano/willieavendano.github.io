Testing Two Proportions

Is p₁ really equal to p₂?

Same test logic as every test this unit — one new wrinkle in the standard error.

Reassure students this is not a new protocol — hypotheses, conditions, test statistic, p-value, decision, conclusion, all identical in structure to week 3–4's single-proportion test. The only new idea this week is the pooled proportion, which gets its own dedicated explanation next.

Two-Proportion Test · 1 of 5

Hypotheses for comparing two groups

Two equivalent ways to write it

H₀: p₁ = p₂   or equivalently   H₀: p₁ − p₂ = 0

Hₕ: p₁ ≠ p₂,  p₁ > p₂, or  p₁ < p₂ — direction depends on the research question, same rule as before.

H₀ always claims “no difference” — that's what makes pooling possible next.

Two-Proportion Test · 2 of 5

Why pool the two samples?

Under H₀, we're assuming p₁ and p₂ are the same unknown number — call it p. If that's true, our best estimate of that shared p uses all the data from both samples combined, not either sample alone.

Pooling only happens for the test — the interval last week used separate p̂₁ and p̂₂ because it never assumed they were equal.

This is worth contrasting explicitly with last week's interval, which never assumes p₁ = p₂ — an interval is agnostic about whether the groups differ, that's the whole point of building it. A test of H₀: p₁ = p₂, by contrast, starts by assuming equality, which licenses combining the data into one pooled estimate. Students who don't see this contrast will pool for intervals too, which is wrong.

Two-Proportion Test · 3 of 5

The pooled proportion

p̂c — the combined estimate

p̂c = (x₁ + x₂) / (n₁ + n₂)

x₁ and x₂ are the raw counts of “successes” in each sample — add all the successes, divide by all the observations. It's just one combined sample proportion.

Two-Proportion Test · 4 of 5

The test statistic

Two-sample z-test statistic

\( z = \dfrac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}_c(1-\hat{p}_c)(1/n_1 + 1/n_2)}} \)

Same numerator as the interval (the observed difference), but the standard error now uses the pooled p̂c in place of the two separate p̂'s — because the whole test is conducted assuming p₁ = p₂.

Large Counts, for this test, also uses p̂c: n₁p̂c ≥ 10, n₁(1−p̂c) ≥ 10, and the same for n₂.

Two-Proportion Test · 5 of 5

Worked example · two study methods

Independent random samples: 42 of n₁ = 60 students using Method A pass a quiz; 33 of n₂ = 60 students using Method B pass. Is there evidence Method A has a higher pass rate? Test at α = 0.05.

StepWhat you write
Kp̂₁ = 0.70, p̂₂ = 0.55, p̂c = (42+33)/120 = 0.625. Random ✓, independent groups ✓, 10% ✓; Large Counts using p̂c: 60 × 0.625 = 37.5 ≥ 10, 60 × 0.375 = 22.5 ≥ 10 (both groups) ✓
DH₀: p₁ = p₂, Hₕ: p₁ > p₂, where p₁, p₂ are the true pass rates for each method
Etwo-sample pooled z-test
Az = (0.70−0.55)/√(0.625×0.375×(1/60+1/60)) ≈ 0.15/0.0884 ≈ 1.70. p-value ≈ 0.045. Since 0.045 < 0.05, reject H₀. We have convincing statistical evidence that Method A has a higher true pass rate than Method B.
Check: 0.625×0.375 = 0.2344, ×(1/60+1/60)=0.03333 gives 0.00781, √≈0.0884. z=0.15/0.0884≈1.70. One-tail normalcdf(1.70) ≈ 0.0446. This is a close call near α — good moment to discuss how a slightly different sample would flip the decision, reinforcing that "reject" doesn't mean "certain."

Practice · Turn & Talk · 12 minutes

Pool it, test it, conclude it

With your partner

A randomized experiment: 28 of n₁ = 50 plants given Fertilizer X flower; 19 of n₂ = 50 plants given Fertilizer Y flower. Test whether the true flowering rates differ, at α = 0.05.

  1. Find the pooled proportion.
  2. Check the conditions.
  3. Compute z and the (two-sided) p-value.
  4. State the decision and the conclusion, in context.

R's prop.test() and Python's statsmodels.proportions_ztest() run this exact procedure on real data in one line — useful to know for the capstone project later this year.

p̂c = 47/100 = 0.47. z = (0.56−0.38)/√(0.47×0.53×(1/50+1/50)) = 0.18/0.0999 ≈ 1.80. Two-sided p-value ≈ 2×0.036 ≈ 0.072. Since 0.072 ≥ 0.05, fail to reject H₀ — not convincing evidence the true flowering rates differ. Worth noting this is close to significant; a good discussion prompt for why "close" still means "fail to reject," no partial credit for almost.

A New Tool

What about more than two groups?

z-tests compare exactly two proportions. Chi-square compares many at once.

This divider marks the pivot to the second half of the week. Motivate it honestly: everything so far has been limited to two groups or a yes/no outcome. Real questions are often bigger — comparing three grade levels, or checking whether two categorical variables (not just two groups) are related. Chi-square is the tool built for exactly that.

Chi-Square · 1 of 5

Two questions, one statistic

H
Homogeneity
One categorical variable, compared across several separate groups or populations. “Do 9th, 10th, 11th, and 12th graders have the same distribution of favorite lunch option?”
I
Independence
Two categorical variables, measured on one single sample. “Are grade level and favorite lunch option associated, within one sample of students?”

Different question, different data-collection design — same arithmetic, coming next week.

The homogeneity-vs-independence distinction is graded heavily on the AP exam and is almost entirely about the STUDY DESIGN, not the numbers in the table. Homogeneity: separate samples were drawn from each group in advance. Independence: one sample was drawn, then classified two ways. Full worked examples of each arrive next week — today is just naming the difference.

Row and column totals are the raw material for the next step: what would we expect to see if there were no association at all?

Chi-Square · 3 of 5

Expected counts: the “no association” baseline

Formula

expected count = (row total × column total) / grand total

This is the count you'd expect in each cell if the row and column variables had no relationship — if the proportions were identical across every row.

Example — the Pizza / 9th-10th cell

(150 × 110) / 300 = 55. We observed 62 — a bit above what “no association” would predict.

Have students compute one or two more expected counts by hand for the table on the previous slide, just for the arithmetic pattern — full expected-count tables and the complete chi-square statistic arrive next week. Today's goal is fluency with the formula itself, not a finished test.

Chi-Square · 4 of 5

The statistic: how far is observed from expected?

χ2 statistic

χ2 = Σ [ (observed − expected)2 / expected ]

Sum this quantity over every cell in the table. A large χ2 means the observed counts are far from what “no association” would predict — evidence against H₀.

Squaring means every cell contributes positively — over and under both count as distance.

Chi-Square · 5 of 5

Degrees of freedom: (rows − 1)(columns − 1)

For the lunch-preference table (2 rows × 3 columns): df = (2−1)(3−1) = 2.

Why it matters

Just like the normal curve needed a z* table, the chi-square distribution needs its own critical values — and which chi-square curve applies depends on df. Next week: computing the full statistic, finding the p-value, and drawing the conclusion.

Keep this brief — df is a mechanical formula, not a conceptual struggle, so don't over-explain it today. The real work (full chi-square test with a p-value and AP-scorable conclusion, for both homogeneity and independence designs) is next week's content, explicitly signposted here.

Common Traps This Week

Three habits to watch

p̂c
Forgetting to pool
Two-proportion tests pool. Two-proportion intervals (last week) never do.
H/I
Design confusion
Homogeneity = separate samples from each group. Independence = one sample, two variables. Read the data collection, not just the table shape.
Σ
Partial sums
χ2 adds a term from every cell in the table — skipping cells understates the statistic.
