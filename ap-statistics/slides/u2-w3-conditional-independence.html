Act I · Conditional Probability

New information changes the sample space.

Conditional probability asks: among the cases where B happened, how often did A happen?

Use the phrase “among the cases where B happened” repeatedly. Students often read the vertical bar as division without translating the condition into a restricted group. The denominator is not a technicality; it is the meaning of the conditional probability.

Conditional Probability · 1 of 4

The bar means “given that.”

P(A | B)
Read it
The probability of A, given that B has occurred or is known.
P(B | A)
Different question
The probability of B among the A cases. Reversing the events usually changes the answer.
B
New reference group
Once B is given, count only outcomes inside B.
A ∩ B
Successful cases
The cases that satisfy both the condition and the event of interest.
Ask students to say both expressions aloud before calculating: “A given B” and “B given A.” A common failure is treating the bar as symmetric. It is not. Point to the restricted reference group B and then to the overlap A intersection B.

Worked Example · Setup

Which probability does the question actually ask for?

In a class of 100 students, 60 passed a quiz and 36 of those students used StatKey. What is the probability that a student used StatKey given that the student passed?

KDEA prompt
  1. K — identify the relevant counts and the condition.
  2. D — name the probability and its reference group.
  3. E — write the conditional probability rule.
  4. A — compute and conclude in context.

The condition chooses the denominator.

Worked Example · KDEA

Used StatKey given passed: a complete response

StepWhat you write
K36 passed students used StatKey; 60 students passed.
DP(uses StatKey | passed), the probability among students who passed.
EP(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)
AP(uses | passed) = 36 / 60 = 0.60
There is a 0.60 probability that a randomly selected student used StatKey, given that the student passed the quiz.

An answer without context is like an answer without units.

This is the stats translation of K-D-E-A: K is data, design, and conditions given; D is the parameter, probability, or claim sought; E is the procedure or formula; A is computation plus a contextual conclusion. The last sentence is not optional prose. It identifies the random student and the condition that defines the reference group. Here 0.60 is exact from the counts, so equals is appropriate.

Act II · Independence

Independent means the condition does not change the chance.

Independence is a claim about probabilities, not a synonym for “different.”

This divider is the pivot. Students frequently use “independent” to mean physically separate or personally unrelated. In AP Statistics, independence is checked by an equality involving a conditional probability, a product, or a joint probability.

Independence · 1 of 3

Three equivalent ways to recognize independence.

P(A | B) = P(A)
Condition test
Knowing B happened does not change the probability of A.
P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)
Multiplication test
The joint probability is the product of the separate probabilities.
P(B | A) = P(B)
Reverse test
Knowing A happened does not change the probability of B either.

One equality is enough to establish independence; use the form that matches the information.

Treat these as equivalent definitions, not three unrelated formulas. If the problem gives a conditional probability, use the condition test. If it gives a joint probability, use the multiplication test. Remind students that a relationship can be independent without the events being mutually exclusive.

Independence · 2 of 3

Mutually exclusive and independent are not the same.

Mutually exclusive
  • Cannot happen together.
  • P(A ∩ B) = 0
  • Knowing A happened makes B impossible.
Independent
  • One event does not change the chance of the other.
  • P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)
  • Both can happen together when both probabilities are positive.

Key consequence: two events with positive probability cannot be both mutually exclusive and independent.

Use a die example if students need one: A = roll an even number and B = roll a 4 are not mutually exclusive; they overlap. For a contradiction, if events are mutually exclusive then the intersection is 0, but if they are independent the product is positive. This is the common conceptual trap, especially when students hear “exclusive” and “independent” as both meaning “separate.”

Worked Example · KDEA

Compare, compute, conclude.

StepWhat you write
KP(uses) = 48 / 100 = 0.48; P(uses | passed) = 36 / 60 = 0.60.
DWhether using StatKey and passing the quiz are independent.
EIndependence requires P(uses | passed) = P(uses).
A0.60 ≠ 0.48. Using StatKey and passing the quiz are not independent in this class because the conditional probability differs from the overall probability.

A statistical conclusion says what the comparison means in context.

Emphasize that independence is a model claim supported or contradicted by the observed table. We are not claiming a causal explanation for why the probabilities differ. Also model the notation carefully: use an equality test, and use not-equal here because the two observed proportions differ. The conclusion must name both events and the class context.

The first pair is mutually exclusive and not independent for a standard die. The second pair overlaps at the king of hearts, so it is not mutually exclusive; whether it is independent depends on the model, and the natural one-card draw is not independent. The third pair is a context question: do not force a numerical answer without a model. Use it to distinguish a claim that needs evidence from a definition that can be checked mechanically.

Act III · Unions & Trees

Combine events without counting the overlap twice.

“Or” uses addition. “And” uses multiplication when the path is conditional.

Students often memorize “or means add” and “and means multiply” without checking overlap or order. The general addition rule subtracts the intersection; the general multiplication rule carries a conditional probability along a path. Tree diagrams make that path structure visible.

Unions & Trees · 1 of 3

Two general rules, two different jobs.

A ∪ B
Union: A or B
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
Subtract the overlap counted twice.
A ∩ B
Intersection: A and B
P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B | A)
Multiply along a conditional path.
A ∩ B
Independent shortcut
If A and B are independent, P(B | A) = P(B), so multiply P(A)P(B).
A ∪ B
Mutually exclusive shortcut
If A and B cannot happen together, P(A ∩ B) = 0, so just add.
Anchor every rule to the event words. Union is “at least one”; intersection is “both.” The shortcuts are special cases of the general rules, not competing formulas. Ask which assumption makes each simplification legal before students use it.

Multiply along a path. Add disjoint paths for an “or” event.

Draw the branches physically if possible: first the quiz result, then the StatKey split conditional on that result. The path product gives a joint probability. If students multiply the second-stage number by nothing, ask whether they are describing the branch or the whole path. We omit the fourth path from the table only to keep the slide readable; students can infer it as the remaining path.

Worked Example · KDEA

What is the probability a student passed or used StatKey?

StepWhat you write
KP(passed) = 0.60, P(uses) = 0.48, P(passed ∩ uses) = 0.36.
DP(passed ∪ uses), the probability of at least one event.
EGeneral addition rule: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).
AP(passed ∪ uses) = 0.60 + 0.48 − 0.36 = 0.72.
There is a 0.72 probability that a randomly selected student passed the quiz or used StatKey, or both.

“Or” includes “both” unless the problem says otherwise.

This is the place to surface the double-counting error. Students who add 0.60 and 0.48 are counting the 36 overlap students twice. The conclusion must say “or both,” because the union includes the intersection. This is exact for the displayed counts, so equals is appropriate; use approximately when a probability is estimated from simulation or rounded from a model.

  2. Using the same information, find the probability the student passed or used StatKey.
  3. Write one sentence explaining whether the two events are independent.

No answer key on this slide. Your conclusion needs context.

Keep this as an assessment-style practice item: do not reveal the numerical answers on the slide or in the live debrief. Ask students to identify “and” versus “or,” then inspect whether their A line includes a contextual conclusion. If time permits, have students compare the multiplication rule with the addition rule and explain why the overlap is needed in only one of them.

Recap

Screenshot this: the Unit 2 Week 3 moves.

Four sentences
  • Conditional probability — restrict the reference group to the condition; P(A | B) is not P(B | A).
  • Independence — the condition does not change the chance: P(A | B) = P(A).
  • Unions — add the event probabilities and subtract the overlap counted twice.
  • Trees — multiply along a path; add disjoint paths for an “or” event.
Have students point to the exact line that catches each common error: denominator for conditional probability, equality for independence, subtraction of the intersection for a union, and multiplication of conditional branches for a tree. The recap is intentionally compact so students can use it as a study image.
