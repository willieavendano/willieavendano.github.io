Where We Are

From a question to a full investigation

In Week 1, you asked a question worth investigating. Now you are ready to show the whole statistical story:

1
Question
A focused question about a population, relationship, or difference.
2
Design + data
A plan that makes the data relevant, ethical, and honest about scope.
3
Evidence + conclusion
A visual, numerical summary, and conclusion that returns to the question.

The capstone is not just a result. It is the chain of reasoning.

Students often want to lead with the most interesting graph. Re-center them on the chain: the question determines the design; the design determines what the data can support; the analysis and conclusion must stay inside that scope. This is the same reasoning used across the five-unit CED, now assembled in one investigation.

Act I · Presentations

Make the evidence easy to follow.

Your audience should know the question, trust the design, and understand the conclusion.

Use this divider to establish the standard for the room. Presentations are not a race to display every calculation. They are an act of statistical communication: make the logic visible and make the audience able to evaluate the claim.

Presentation Arc · 1 of 3

A short presentation still needs a complete arc

  • Open — question, context, and why it matters
  • Explain — variables, population, sample, and design
  • Show — one or two visuals that carry the evidence
  • Interpret — what the statistic or interval says in context
  • Limit — what the design cannot establish
  • Close — answer the original question, then invite one question

Question → design → evidence → conclusion → limits

Presentation Expectations · 2 of 3

What strong statistical communication sounds like

Specific
Names the population, variables, sample, and statistic instead of saying “the data” or “the results.”
Visible
Uses a readable display and points to the feature that supports the claim.
In context
Interprets numbers in the setting of the question, not as floating calculations.
Honest
Separates association from causation and reports limitations without apology.
Treat this as the rubric-style expectations slide. The word “honest” is important: a limitation is not a failure of the project. Naming a limitation demonstrates statistical judgment and protects the audience from overclaiming.

Presentation Expectations · 3 of 3

Rubric-style check before you present

Look-forAsk yourself
QuestionCan the audience repeat the investigative question after the opening?
DesignHave I explained who or what was measured, how, and why the design fits?
AnalysisDoes each graph or statistic answer a part of the question?
ConclusionDoes the final claim match the evidence and the scope of the design?

A polished slide cannot rescue an unclear claim.

Audience Roles

Listeners are part of the statistical work

During the presentation
  • Track the question, design, evidence, and conclusion.
  • Write one sentence that the evidence supports.
  • Flag one point that needs clarification.
During the question
  • Ask about a design choice or interpretation.
  • Use “What would change if…?” to probe scope.
  • Respond as a collaborator, not a prosecutor.
Give the audience a concrete listening protocol. Without a job, students drift into passive viewing and presenters receive only “good job.” The clarification prompt is especially useful because it makes feedback about statistical reasoning rather than personality or performance style.

KDEA for Statistics

Run the investigation through the class protocol

StepStatistics mappingPresenter prompt
KData, design, conditionsWhat was measured, on whom, and under what conditions?
DParameter, probability, or claim soughtWhat exactly are we trying to learn or decide?
EProcedure or formulaWhy does this graph, statistic, interval, or test fit the question?
ACompute and conclude in contextWhat does the evidence say here, and what does it not say?

KDEA keeps the conclusion attached to the design.

This is the capstone bridge back to the house protocol. The statistics version is K = data/design/conditions; D = the parameter, probability, or claim sought; E = the procedure or formula; A = compute and conclude in context. Remind students that “in context” is the statistics version of carrying units through the math.

KDEA in Action · Worked Prompt

Explain the evidence, not just the output

StepWhat the presenter prepares
KSample description, variables, display, and relevant conditions.
DThe question translated into a target: a proportion, mean, difference, relationship, or claim.
EThe chosen summary or inference procedure, plus the condition that makes it reasonable.
AThe numerical result, its meaning in context, and a conclusion with the correct scope.

If the audience asks “So what?”, return to D. If they ask “Can we trust that?”, return to K and E.

This slide is a worked communication scaffold, not an answer key. Model the two audience questions aloud: “So what?” asks students to return to the target; “Can we trust that?” asks them to revisit design and conditions. This gives students a recovery move when a question catches them off guard.

Act II · Year in Review

Look how far the question traveled.

From describing one variable to defending a statistical conclusion.

Use this divider after the presentation act, not before. The class has just heard several examples of statistical investigations; now make the year visible as a progression of increasingly consequential questions and increasingly careful evidence.

Year in Review · 1 of 3

First: learn to see data

U1
One-variable data + collecting
Describe distributions, compare groups, and ask whether the data collection can support the story.
U2
Probability + random variables
Build a language for uncertainty, expected behavior, and the variation we should anticipate.

Before inference, statistics teaches us to notice structure.

Keep this recap conceptual rather than turning it into a vocabulary list. Unit 1 established the visual grammar of distributions and data collection. Unit 2 gave students a way to reason about chance and the behavior of random variables before seeing a result.

Year in Review · 2 of 3

Then: learn to generalize carefully

U3
Inference for categorical data
Use proportions and chi-square methods for homogeneity or independence to make evidence-based comparisons.
U4
Inference for quantitative data
Use means and intervals or tests to reason about numerical measurements with uncertainty attached.

A sample can speak for a population only when the design earns that reach.

Be precise about the CED scope: Unit 3 includes inference for categorical data, including chi-square homogeneity or independence. Unit 4 is inference for quantitative data through means. Avoid introducing removed topics or any additional secure-test framing here; this is a celebration of ideas already taught.

Year in Review · 3 of 3

And: learn to model relationships

Unit 5 · Regression analysis

We moved from “What does one variable look like?” to “How do two quantitative variables move together, and how well can a model describe that relationship?”

  • Read the direction, form, and strength of an association.
  • Use a model to make a prediction while watching residuals and context.
  • Keep association, prediction, and causation separate.

A model is useful because it is interpretable, not because it is automatic.

This is the synthesis beat. The letters are intentionally not a replacement for KDEA; they name the arc of an investigation. KDEA is the protocol used inside that arc to keep data, target, procedure, and conclusion connected.

Closing Reflection

One sentence to carry forward

Write, then share
  1. A statistical habit I will keep is…
  2. A question I now know how to investigate is…
  3. One way my thinking changed this year is…

Leave with a method, not just a memory.

Give students quiet writing time before inviting a few voices. The reflection should not become a forced round-robin that crowds out the final celebration. Listen for habits that show statistical maturity: checking design, naming uncertainty, interpreting in context, and resisting causal language when it is not earned.

Recap

What you can do now

  • Describe data and recognize patterns without hiding variation.
  • Design a collection plan and explain what it can support.
  • Use probability to reason about uncertainty before a result arrives.
  • Choose an appropriate procedure, check conditions, and interpret in context.
  • Communicate a conclusion that is proportional to the evidence.

You started with investigative questions. You finish as statistical investigators.

Read this as a celebration of capability, not a final checklist for grading. The key phrase is “proportional to the evidence.” Students do not need certainty to do statistics well; they need disciplined claims that match the design, data, and uncertainty they actually have.
