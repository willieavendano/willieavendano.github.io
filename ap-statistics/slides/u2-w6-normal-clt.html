Act I · The Normal Model

Every area is a probability.

Read the model before you calculate.

This act is about translation. Students should be able to move among a verbal event, a shaded area, a cutoff value, and calculator-ready notation. The most common failure is calculating before naming whether the event is below, above, or between two values.

Normal Distribution · 1 of 4

A normal model has two numbers

μ
Mean · center
The balance point of the distribution. For a normal model, the mean is also the median.
σ
Standard deviation · spread
The typical distance from the mean, measured in the same units as the variable.

A model statement makes the scale explicit: X ∼ Normal(μ, σ).

Shape alone is not enough: always name center and spread.

Normal Distribution · 2 of 4

Standardize: turn a value into a location

z = (x − μ) / σ

  • z = 0 — at the mean
  • z > 0 — above the mean
  • z < 0 — below the mean
  • |z| — distance in SD units

A z-score has no measurement units; it says how unusual the location is.

Normal Distribution · 3 of 4

Translate the shaded region

<
Below a value
P(X < a) is the area to the left of a.
>
Above a value
P(X > a) is the area to the right of a.
↔
Between values
P(a < X < b) is the area between the two cutoffs.

For a continuous model, whether an endpoint is included does not change the area.

Insist on the picture or a verbal translation before calculator syntax. Students often reverse the direction for “at least” and “at most,” or subtract the smaller area from the larger without naming the two cutoffs. Ask for a quick hand gesture: left, right, or between.

Worked Example · Setup

Commute time: an area below a cutoff

Suppose commute time for a group of students is modeled by X ∼ Normal(μ = 32, σ = 6) minutes.

Question

What is the probability that a randomly selected commute takes less than 41 minutes?

First name the event: left of 41 minutes.

Worked Example · KDEA

P(X < 41), in context

StepWhat you write
KX ∼ Normal(μ = 32, σ = 6) minutes; cutoff x = 41 minutes
DP(X < 41), the probability a commute is shorter than 41 minutes
EStandardize with z = (x − μ) / σ; use the normal CDF for the area to the left
Az = (41 − 32) / 6 = 1.50; P(X < 41) ≈ 0.933. Approximately 93.3% of modeled commutes take less than 41 minutes.

KDEA in stats: compute and conclude in context.

This is the first full stats mapping of KDEA: K is data, design, and conditions; D is the parameter, probability, or claim sought; E is the procedure or formula; A is the computation plus a contextual conclusion. The common failure is stopping at 0.933. Say aloud that an answer without context is like an answer without units. Depending on the calculator, students may see 0.9332; accept a reasonable rounding but require the comparison to commute time.

Normal Distribution · 4 of 4

Percentiles work backwards

Area question

Given a cutoff x, find the area.

x → z → area

Percentile question

Given a left-tail area, find the cutoff.

area → z → x

For a percentile, the last step returns to the original units: x = μ + zσ.

Students who can find an area often freeze when the unknown is the x-value. Keep the direction visible: percentile means area to the left, inverse normal finds the z first, then transform back to minutes, points, grams, or whatever the context uses. A percentile is not the same thing as a percent correct.

Worked Example · KDEA

The 90th percentile of commute time

StepWhat you write
KX ∼ Normal(μ = 32, σ = 6) minutes; left-tail area 0.90
Dthe commute time at the 90th percentile, in minutes
EUse inverse normal to find z; then x = μ + zσ
Az0.90 ≈ 1.282; x = 32 + (1.282)(6) ≈ 39.7 minutes. Approximately 90% of modeled commutes are 39.7 minutes or less.

The answer is a cutoff in minutes, not “90%.”

Practice · Pair Work · 8 minutes

Normal model translation lab

With a partner · no answer key on this slide

Battery life is modeled by X ∼ Normal(μ = 18, σ = 2.5) hours.

  1. Write a KDEA setup for P(X > 20).
  2. Find the 25th percentile as a cutoff in hours.
  3. Write one sentence explaining what your percentile cutoff means.

Underline the event; circle the units; name the tail.

Do not reveal answers here. Listen for the right-tail reversal in part 1 and for students returning the percentile as a z-score instead of hours in part 2. Require the sentence in part 3 to identify battery life and the “25% at or below” meaning. If pairs finish early, have them sketch both areas.

Act II · Sampling Distributions

Now the random variable is a statistic.

One sample gives one value. Repeated samples make a distribution.

This divider marks the conceptual turn. A population distribution describes individual observations; a sampling distribution describes a statistic across repeated samples. Students commonly confuse the two standard deviations or speak as though one sample contains the entire sampling distribution.

Sampling Distributions · 1 of 4

Three distributions, three questions

X
Population
What do individual observations look like? Center & spread describe the variable.
x̄
One sample
What value did this sample produce? One sample statistic is one draw from a process.
x̄
Sampling distribution
How does the statistic vary over many random samples of the same size?

The sampling distribution is about the statistic, not the individual data values.

Sampling Distributions · 2 of 4

For sample means: center stays, spread shrinks

\( \mu_{\bar{x}} = \mu \)
Center
The mean of the sampling distribution is the population mean.
\( \sigma_{\bar{x}} = \sigma/\sqrt{n} \)
Standard error
Larger samples make sample means less variable.

The standard error measures typical sample-to-sample variation in x̄, in the original units.

Separate standard deviation from standard error explicitly. The population spread describes an individual observation; the standard error describes a sample mean. Students often divide by n instead of √n, or think the population itself becomes less variable when n increases. Only the statistic’s sampling distribution gets narrower.

Sampling Distributions · 3 of 4

The Central Limit Theorem is a shape claim

When samples are random and sufficiently large

The sampling distribution of a sample mean is approximately normal, even when the population distribution is not normal.

  • Random sample or random assignment
  • Observations are independent or approximately so
  • Sample size is large enough for the population shape
  • Center and standard error still matter

CLT is why normal models power Units 3–4 inference.

Avoid saying “n ≥ 30 always works.” The needed sample size depends on the population shape: a strongly skewed or heavy-tailed population needs more data than an already symmetric one. Plant the forward reference exactly: Unit 3 will use sampling distributions for proportions, and Unit 4 will use sampling distributions for means to build intervals and tests. The CLT is the bridge from Unit 2 probability to those inference procedures.

Worked Example · Setup

Average delivery time: a sample mean

Individual delivery times have mean μ = 42 minutes and standard deviation σ = 12 minutes. A random sample of n = 36 deliveries is selected.

Question

What is the probability that the sample mean delivery time is less than 45 minutes?

The random variable is x̄, not one delivery time.

Worked Example · KDEA

P(x̄ < 45), in context

StepWhat you write
Kμ = 42 min; σ = 12 min; random n = 36 deliveries
DP(x̄ < 45), the probability of a sample mean below 45 minutes
ECLT: x̄ is approximately normal with μx̄ = 42 and σx̄ = 12 / √36 = 2 minutes
Az = (45 − 42) / 2 = 1.50; P(x̄ < 45) ≈ 0.933. Approximately 93.3% of random samples of 36 deliveries have a mean below 45 minutes.

The unit is still minutes; only the spread changed.

This is the key worked example for the CLT. Have students say why the standard error is 2 minutes, not 12 minutes: we are modeling an average of 36 deliveries. The contextual sentence must mention repeated samples of 36, not individual deliveries. This is the exact reasoning students will reuse when Unit 4 asks whether a sample mean is unusual under a claimed population mean.

Sampling Distributions · 4 of 4

See the CLT before trusting the formula

StatKey

Resample from a skewed population. Watch the distribution of sample means become smoother and narrower as n grows.

R & Python

Repeat: sample, calculate x̄, store it. Plot the stored means and compare their center and spread.

Simulation makes “repeated samples” visible.

Use StatKey for a fast visual, then connect the same loop to R or Python if students are ready. The teaching move is to change one input at a time: first sample size, then number of repetitions. Watch for the misconception that more repetitions change the standard error; repetitions improve the picture, while n changes the sampling distribution itself. Do not turn this into a coding assessment today.

Practice · KDEA Sprint · 10 minutes

Which spread belongs to the statistic?

Individual work, then compare

A population of waiting times has μ = 8.0 minutes and σ = 3.6 minutes. A random sample of n = 81 waits is selected.

  1. Describe the center and standard error of the sampling distribution of x̄.
  2. State why a normal model for x̄ is reasonable.
  3. Set up, but do not finish, a KDEA solution for P(x̄ > 8.8).

No answer key: show the model choice and the reasoning.

Do not reveal numerical answers on this assessment-style prompt. Check that students use 3.6 / 9 for the standard error, not 3.6 / 81. For the normal-model justification, accept either a roughly normal population or the sufficiently large random sample argument, but require the independence condition to be considered. The prompt stops before the final calculation so you can inspect setup.
