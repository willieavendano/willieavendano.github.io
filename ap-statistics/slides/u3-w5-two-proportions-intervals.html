Why This Matters

“Is Group A different from Group B?”

Do 10th graders and 12th graders support a schedule change at different rates? Does a new fertilizer outperform the old one? These are two-sample questions — and they show up constantly, in every field that runs experiments or compares groups.

Random assignment (an experiment) lets us use causal language later. Random sampling (an observational study) doesn't — keep that distinction in mind all week.

Worth a brief callback to Unit 1's experimental design unit here — the statistical machinery for comparing two proportions is identical whether the data came from an experiment or an observational study, but the conclusions we're allowed to draw are not. Random assignment supports "caused a difference"; random sampling without assignment only supports "is associated with a difference."

Design Matters

Two ways to get two groups

RA
Random assignment
An experiment: individuals are randomly placed into groups (e.g., treatment vs. control). Balances lurking variables — supports causal language if a difference shows up.
RS
Random sampling
An observational study: two groups already exist (e.g., 10th graders vs. 12th graders), and we sample from each. Supports association language only — never “caused.”

The formulas this week don't care which one you have — but the words you're allowed to use in your conclusion absolutely do.

This is a direct callback to Unit 1's experimental design content — worth explicitly naming that connection. Students consistently want to say "caused" whenever a confidence interval excludes zero, regardless of design. Hold the line here: the interval only ever tells you whether a difference is real; the study design is what tells you whether you're allowed to call it causal.

A New Statistic

p̂₁ − p̂₂

One number, built from two independent samples.

Frame this as directly parallel to Week 1's introduction of p̂ — same move, new statistic. A difference of two random quantities is itself a random quantity, with its own sampling distribution, its own center, and its own spread. Nothing conceptually new, just one more layer.

Two Proportions · 1 of 5

Two independent samples, one difference

Take a random sample from Population 1 (proportion p₁) and an independent random sample from Population 2 (proportion p₂). Compute:

The statistic

p̂₁ − p̂₂

This estimates the true difference p₁ − p₂ — positive means Group 1's proportion is higher, negative means Group 2's is.

Two Proportions · 2 of 5

Center and spread of p̂₁ − p̂₂

Mean

\( \text{mean of } (\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) = p_1 - p_2 \)

Standard deviation

\( SD = \sqrt{\dfrac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1} + \dfrac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}} \)

The variances add — because the two samples are independent, their sampling variabilities combine under the square root.

Students sometimes expect the SDs to subtract, mirroring the mean subtracting. Emphasize explicitly: variances always add for independent quantities, regardless of whether the statistic itself is a sum or a difference. This mirrors variance-addition rules from Unit 2's random variables work if students remember that — a useful callback, but don't over-derive it, just state and use it.

Two Proportions · 3 of 5

Conditions: the same three, doubled

R
Random
Both samples are random (or the data comes from a randomized experiment) — and the two samples are independent of each other.
10%
Independence within groups
Each sample size is no more than 10% of its own population.
LC₁
Large Counts, Group 1
n₁p̂₁ ≥ 10, n₁(1−p̂₁) ≥ 10
LC₂
Large Counts, Group 2
n₂p̂₂ ≥ 10, n₂(1−p̂₂) ≥ 10

Four checks now, not three — Large Counts has to hold for BOTH groups separately.

This is the most common condition-checking miss this week: students check Large Counts for one group and stop. All four boxes need a check mark before the interval is valid. "Independent of each other" is also worth flagging — this is different from the within-group 10% independence and gets conflated easily.

The Confidence Interval

(p̂₁ − p̂₂) ± margin of error

Same shape as every interval this unit.

Two Proportions · 4 of 5

The formula

Two-sample z-interval for a difference of proportions

\( (\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) \pm z^* \sqrt{\dfrac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \dfrac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}} \)

Same z* table as one-sample intervals. The standard error is the only new piece — and it's exactly the SD formula from two slides ago, with sample p̂'s standing in for the unknown p's.

Two Proportions · 5 of 5

Worked example · two grade levels, one question

Independent random samples ask whether students support a later start time: 84 of n₁ = 120 tenth-graders say yes; 65 of n₂ = 110 twelfth-graders say yes. Build a 95% confidence interval for the true difference in support.

StepWhat you write
Kp̂₁ = 84/120 = 0.70, p̂₂ = 65/110 ≈ 0.591. Random ✓ (both, independent of each other); 10% ✓ (both); Large Counts: n₁p̂₁=84, n₁(1−p̂₁)=36 ✓; n₂p̂₂=65, n₂(1−p̂₂)=45 ✓
Dp₁ − p₂ = the true difference in the proportion supporting a later start time, 10th-graders minus 12th-graders
Etwo-sample z-interval, z* = 1.96
A(0.70−0.591) ± 1.96√(0.70×0.30/120 + 0.591×0.409/110) ≈ 0.109 ± 0.121 → (−0.012, 0.230). We are 95% confident the interval from −0.012 to 0.230 captures the true difference in support (10th minus 12th grade).
SE arithmetic: 0.70×0.30/120 = 0.00175; 0.591×0.409/110 ≈ 0.002197; sum ≈ 0.003947; √ ≈ 0.0628; ×1.96 ≈ 0.123 (rounds close to the 0.121 shown after intermediate rounding — either is acceptable, note the rounding sensitivity for students). Since the interval contains 0, this is a natural bridge to the next slide's "does 0 tell us something" discussion.

Margin of Error

What widens or narrows this interval?

n₁, n₂ ↑
Bigger samples
Both standard-error terms shrink — margin of error gets smaller. The smaller sample usually dominates the total SE, so growing the weaker sample helps most.
C% ↑
Higher confidence
Same trade-off as always: z* grows, margin of error grows.

A lopsided design (n₁ = 500, n₂ = 20) is only as precise as its smaller sample — balance matters.

Worth a quick arithmetic demonstration if time allows: plug n₂ = 20 into the worked example's SE formula and show how dramatically the margin of error grows compared to n₂ = 110. Students planning their own capstone data project later this year will need this intuition for sample-size decisions.

Reading the Interval

Does zero fall inside the interval?

The key check
  • 0 is inside the interval → plausible that p₁ = p₂ — not convincing evidence of a real difference between the groups.
  • 0 is outside the interval → convincing evidence of a real difference, and the sign of the interval tells you which group is higher.

In the worked example, −0.012 to 0.230 contains 0 — we do not have convincing evidence of a true difference between 10th and 12th graders.

This mirrors exactly the "inside/outside the interval" logic from Week 2's justifying-claims lesson — same skill, applied to zero instead of a claimed value, because zero is the value that means "no difference." Worth saying that parallel out loud; it collapses two ideas into one for students.

Randomization & bootstrap intervals

The two methods rarely agree to the decimal, but they should land in the same neighborhood — a good way to sanity-check a formula-based answer.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 15 minutes

Build the interval, read the zero

With your partner

An agricultural researcher runs a randomized experiment: n₁ = 90 plots get the new fertilizer, 68 show healthy growth; n₂ = 90 plots get the old fertilizer, 52 show healthy growth.

  1. Check all four conditions.
  2. Build a 90% confidence interval for the true difference in the proportion showing healthy growth (new minus old).
  3. Write the full interpretation sentence.
  4. Does the interval suggest a real difference between the fertilizers? Is causal language justified here?
p̂₁ = 68/90 ≈ 0.756, p̂₂ = 52/90 ≈ 0.578. SE ≈ √(0.756×0.244/90 + 0.578×0.422/90) ≈ √(0.00205+0.00271) ≈ 0.0690. ME (z*=1.645) ≈ 0.1135. Interval ≈ (0.064, 0.312) — does not contain 0, so convincing evidence the new fertilizer has a higher healthy-growth rate. Because this was a randomized experiment (plots randomly assigned to fertilizer), causal language IS justified: the new fertilizer caused higher healthy growth rates in this study.

Common Traps

Three ways two-sample problems go wrong

+
Wrong SE formula
Variances add under the square root — don't subtract them, and don't forget one group's term entirely.
×2
Half the conditions
Large Counts must be checked for both groups separately — four checks, not two.
→
Causal overreach
Only a randomized experiment supports “caused a difference.” An observational study supports “is associated with” only.
