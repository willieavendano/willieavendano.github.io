Topic 1.13

Experimental Design

The difference between “these are related” and “this causes that.”

1.13 · 1 of 4

Observational study vs. experiment

Observational study

Researchers watch and record — no treatment is imposed. Can show association, never causation.

Experiment

Researchers impose a treatment and use random assignment to groups. This is the only design that can support a cause-and-effect claim.

“Correlation isn't causation” — this is the design reason why.

This is arguably the single most-tested distinction in Unit 1. Every year, students confuse "random sampling" (Week 5, who's in the study) with "random assignment" (this week, which group a subject lands in) — say the words side by side and make the difference explicit before moving on. Random sampling supports generalizing to a population; random assignment supports causal claims. They answer completely different questions.

1.13 · 2 of 4

What makes an experiment trustworthy

RA
Random assignment
subjects are randomly placed into treatment groups — balances out unknown differences between groups
C
Control group
a comparison group that doesn't get the treatment — the baseline for judging an effect
Pl
Placebo
a fake treatment for the control group, so subjects don't know which group they're in
Bl
Blinding
subjects (single-blind) or subjects AND researchers (double-blind) don't know who got the real treatment

Double-blind removes bias from both the subject's expectations AND the researcher's judgment.

Blinding is worth a concrete medical example: in a drug trial, if the doctor knows who got the real pill, that knowledge can unconsciously bias how they interpret and report symptoms — that's exactly what double-blind design eliminates on both sides at once.

1.13 · 3 of 4

Confounding variables

A variable that's tangled up with the treatment, so you can't tell which one actually caused the result.

Worked example

An observational study finds people who drink more coffee have more heart disease. But coffee drinkers in the study also tend to smoke more — smoking is confounded with coffee use. Without random assignment, there's no way to isolate coffee's effect from smoking's.

Random assignment is the tool that spreads confounding variables evenly across groups — by chance.

Tie this directly back to slide 4: this is exactly WHY random assignment matters, made concrete. With enough subjects randomly assigned, smokers end up roughly evenly split between the coffee and no-coffee groups purely by chance — which is what lets a real experiment isolate the treatment's effect.

1.13 · 4 of 4 · Worked Example

Critique the design

Scenario

A researcher wants to know if a new study app improves test scores. She lets students choose whether to use the app, then compares the test scores of app users vs. non-users.

StepWhat you write
Kstudents self-selected into “app” and “no app” groups
Dcan this design support a causal claim about the app?
Echeck for random assignment
Ano — this is observational, not an experiment. Students who choose to use a study app may already be more motivated, which is a plausible confounding variable. The design can show association, not causation.
"Motivation" as the confounder here is a great discussion prompt — ask students to name other plausible confounders (prior GPA, time available to study, access to a device). The point is recognizing self-selection as the design flaw, not the specific confounder named.

Turn and Talk · 10 minutes

Design an experiment

With your partner

Design a real experiment (not an observational study) to test: “Does listening to music while studying improve quiz scores?”

  1. Name the treatment and control groups.
  2. Describe how you'd randomly assign subjects.
  3. Name one confounding variable random assignment protects you against.
Watch for designs that accidentally become observational again — e.g., "let students pick whether to listen to music" reintroduces self-selection. The randomization step has to be an explicit, described mechanism (coin flip, random number, drawing names), not a vague "randomly."

Common Test Traps · 1 of 4

Trap #1: An answer without context

A correct number with no sentence tying it back to the real-world question earns little or no credit on the AP rubric — this is the single most common way students lose points on an otherwise-correct calculation.

The fix

Every final answer needs: the number, its unit or label, AND a sentence connecting it back to the original question. “The median is 76.5” is not a complete answer. “The median commute time is 76.5 minutes, meaning half of students commute longer than that” is.

This is the same rule from Week 1's KDEA divider ("context travels with the conclusion"), restated as the #1 test trap because it's genuinely the #1 point-loser nationally on AP Statistics free responses. Say it one more time before the test: no bare numbers, ever.

Common Test Traps · 2 of 4

Trap #2: Describing instead of comparing

When a question asks to compare two groups, reporting each group's statistics separately — without an explicit comparative word — loses credit, even if both numbers are correct.

Weak

“Group A: median 82. Group B: median 74.”

Strong

“Group A's median (82) is higher than Group B's (74).”

Reused directly from Week 4 — worth repeating verbatim this close to the test, since it's the second-most common point-loser after bare-number answers. Ask the room to supply the missing comparative word before you show it.

Common Test Traps · 3 of 4

Trap #3: Mean and SD on skewed data

Reporting the mean and standard deviation for a distribution that's skewed or has outliers is a common, costly mistake — those statistics are exactly the ones outliers distort the most.

The fix

Check shape first. Skewed or outlier-heavy → report median and IQR. Roughly symmetric, no outliers → mean and SD are fine. Justify the choice by naming the shape.

This connects Week 3's resistant/non-resistant vocabulary directly to test performance. The justification sentence ("because the distribution is skewed...") is graded, not optional — a correct choice of median with no shape justification can still lose a point.

Common Test Traps · 4 of 4

Trap #4: Random sampling ≠ random assignment

These two phrases get swapped constantly — and they answer completely different questions.

TermAnswersSupports
Random samplingwho is in the studygeneralizing results to a population
Random assignmentwhich treatment group a subject lands ina cause-and-effect claim

A study can have one, both, or neither — check for each separately.

A study with random assignment but no random sampling (e.g., volunteers randomly assigned to treatment/control) can support causation for those volunteers, but not generalize to a wider population — that's a genuinely subtle, testable distinction worth stating explicitly if time allows.

Confidence Check · Turn and Talk · 10 minutes

One last problem before Thursday

With your partner

A school wants to know if a new lunch menu increases the proportion of students eating a vegetable at lunch. They randomly assign each lunch period to either the new menu or the old menu for one month, then compare.

  1. Is this observational or experimental? How do you know?
  2. Name the explanatory and response variables, and their types.
  3. What would a strong, in-context conclusion sentence look like?
This problem deliberately touches every major thread from the unit at once — variable classification, experimental design, and the context-sentence requirement — as a genuine rehearsal for the test's likely format. Answers: (1) experimental — random assignment of lunch periods to treatments. (2) explanatory = menu type (categorical), response = ate a vegetable (categorical). (3) must name both groups, name the direction of any difference, and state it in terms of the actual proportions, not just "yes it worked."
