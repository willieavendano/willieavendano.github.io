Confidence Intervals for a Proportion

Week 2

3.3–3.4 · building an interval · justifying claims from it

Full three-meeting week: Mon 1/11 (44m), Tue 1/12 (80m), Thu 1/14 (80m). Last week described the theoretical sampling distribution of p̂ assuming p was known. This week flips it: p is unknown, p̂ is all we have, and we build an interval of plausible values for p around it. This is the first real inference tool of the course — treat it with the weight that deserves.

Where We Are

Last week: theory. This week: a tool.

You already know the sampling distribution of p̂ is approximately normal, centered at p, with \( SD = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n} \) — when the conditions hold. The catch: that formula needs p, and in real problems p is exactly what we don't know.

  • Mon 1/11 — the confidence interval formula, conditions, worked example
  • Tue 1/12 — interpreting intervals, margin of error
  • Thu 1/14 — justifying claims from an interval, practice

Solution: estimate the SD using p̂ instead of p. That's the whole trick.

Why This Matters

A single number is honest about being wrong

Reporting just p̂ = 0.85 implies false precision — as if the true p is exactly 0.85. It almost never is. A confidence interval reports a range of plausible values instead, and is upfront about how confident we are in that range.

Point estimate ± margin of error. Every confidence interval in this course has that shape.

The Confidence Interval

p̂ ± margin of error

A range of plausible values for the unknown p, built from one sample.

This divider is the pivot from "describing" (last week) to "estimating" (this week). Keep the framing simple and repeat it: every interval this unit — one proportion, two proportions, later means — has the same skeleton, point estimate ± margin of error. Students who internalize this shape early recognize new formulas as variations on one idea, not new things to memorize.

Confidence Intervals · 1 of 5

The formula, piece by piece

One-sample z-interval for a proportion

\( \hat{p} \pm z^* \sqrt{\dfrac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}} \)

  • p̂ — the point estimate, from your sample
  • z* — the critical value, set by your confidence level
  • \( \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n} \) — the standard error — the same SD formula from last week, with p̂ standing in for the unknown p

Standard error, not standard deviation — we're estimating the spread now, not stating it exactly.

Vocabulary precision matters for the exam: "standard deviation" of p̂ uses the true p (theoretical, from last week); "standard error" uses p̂ in its place (what we actually compute, since p is unknown). AP readers do look for this distinction in FRQ language.

Sample size is no more than 10% of the population — keeps observations close to independent.

Large Counts

All three go in the K row of every KDEA table this unit — skipping one is an incomplete answer, not a shortcut.

Point out precisely what changed from last week: Large Counts now uses p̂ instead of p, because p is the very thing we're trying to estimate — we have no other number to plug in. This is a subtle but AP-graded distinction: using p instead of p̂ here is a condition-checking error readers do catch.

Confidence Intervals · 4 of 5

Worked example · a school lunch survey

A random sample of n = 240 students finds that 156 would support a later lunch period. Build a 95% confidence interval for the true proportion of all students who would support it.

StepWhat you write
Kp̂ = 156/240 = 0.65, n = 240, random sample ✓; 10%: 240 ≤ 10% of the student body ✓; Large Counts: np̂ = 156 ≥ 10, n(1−p̂) = 84 ≥ 10 ✓
Dp = the true proportion of all students who would support a later lunch period
Eone-sample z-interval: \( \hat{p} \pm z^* \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n} \), \( z^* = 1.96 \) for 95%
A0.65 ± 1.96√(0.65 × 0.35 / 240) ≈ 0.65 ± 0.0603 → (0.590, 0.710). We are 95% confident the interval from 0.590 to 0.710 captures the true proportion of all students who would support a later lunch period.
Walk the margin-of-error arithmetic slowly: 0.65×0.35 = 0.2275, /240 = 0.000948, √ ≈ 0.0308, ×1.96 ≈ 0.0603. Round the endpoints to three decimals, not more — false precision on a bounded proportion is its own small error AP readers notice. The interpretation sentence in the A row is not optional flourish — it is graded content on the actual exam.

Confidence Intervals · 5 of 5

The interpretation sentence, exactly

Template — memorize the shape, not the words

“We are C% confident that the interval from ___ to ___ captures the true [parameter, in context].”

Every blank must be filled with the actual numbers and the actual context — “the true proportion” with no context is not a complete sentence on the AP exam.

This template gets reused, with small variation, for every interval type all year — one proportion now, two proportions in week 5, means in Unit 4. Worth having students write it from memory a few times today so it's automatic under test pressure.

A Common Misconception

“95% confident” is not a probability about this interval

Wrong

“There is a 95% probability that the true p is between 0.590 and 0.710.” The interval is already built — p is a fixed number, either in it or not. There's no probability left to talk about.

Right

“If we repeated this sampling process many times, about 95% of the resulting intervals would capture the true p.” The confidence is in the method, not any one interval.

This exact distinction shows up on FRQs almost every year.

This is one of the highest-yield misconceptions to correct on the AP exam — "95% chance the parameter is in the interval" is a near-automatic point loss on interpretation questions. Have students say the "right" version out loud a few times; the long-run-repeated-sampling framing feels unnatural at first and needs repetition to stick.

Margin of Error

What makes the interval wider or narrower?

n ↑
Bigger sample
Standard error shrinks (√n in the denominator) — margin of error gets smaller. More data, more precision.
C% ↑
Higher confidence
z* gets bigger — margin of error gets larger. More confidence costs precision.

You cannot get more confidence AND more precision for free — one always trades against the other.

Justifying Claims · 3.4

Use the interval to settle an argument

Does a claimed value fall inside your interval, or outside it?

This is the payoff of building an interval at all — 3.4 is about using it as evidence, which is exactly the skill that shows up as an FRQ part (b) or (c) after building the interval in part (a). Students who only practice "build the interval" and never "use it to argue" get caught off guard.

Justifying Claims · 1 of 2

Inside the interval, or outside it?

Claimed value is OUTSIDE the interval

The interval provides convincing evidence that the true parameter is different from the claimed value.

Claimed value is INSIDE the interval

The interval does not provide convincing evidence against the claim — the claimed value is still plausible.

“Plausible” is not the same as “proven.” We never prove a claim true.

Justifying Claims · 2 of 2

Worked example · does the interval support the claim?

District leadership claims exactly 60% of students support the later lunch period. Our 95% interval from the lunch survey was (0.590, 0.710).

Answer, in context

Since 0.60 falls inside the interval (0.590, 0.710), our data do not provide convincing evidence that the true proportion differs from the district's claimed 60%. The claim is plausible given our sample.

Contrast this quickly with a second claim that would fall outside — e.g. "district claims only 50% support it." 0.50 is below 0.590, outside the interval, so that claim WOULD be contradicted by the data. Doing both in class back-to-back cements the inside/outside logic better than one example alone.

Practice · Turn & Talk · 12 minutes

Build it, interpret it, use it

With your partner

A random sample of n = 180 Cushman families finds 117 support moving the fall fundraiser to the spring.

  1. Check all three conditions.
  2. Build a 90% confidence interval for the true proportion of families in favor.
  3. Write the full interpretation sentence.
  4. A board member claims “only 55% of families actually support this.” Does your interval provide convincing evidence against that claim?
p̂ = 117/180 ≈ 0.65. SE = √(0.65×0.35/180) ≈ 0.0356. ME = 1.645×0.0356 ≈ 0.0585. Interval ≈ (0.591, 0.709). 0.55 is below 0.591, outside the interval — yes, convincing evidence the true proportion is higher than 55%. Watch for groups that use z* = 1.96 out of habit instead of the 90% value 1.645 — a very common slip once 95% becomes muscle memory.

Common Traps

Four ways to lose easy points

√
Skipped conditions
No credit for the interval itself if Random / 10% / Large Counts aren't checked, even if the arithmetic is perfect.
%
“95% chance”
Confidence describes the method over many samples, never the probability for this one interval.
z
Wrong z*
Using 1.96 out of habit when the problem asks for a different confidence level.
∅
No context
“The true proportion” with no subject — always name of what population, doing what.
