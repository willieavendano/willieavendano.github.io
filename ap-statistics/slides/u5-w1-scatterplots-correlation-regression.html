Act I · The Picture

Read the cloud.

A scatterplot is a sentence about two quantitative variables.

Pause here and ask students what a cloud can communicate before introducing vocabulary. The common failure mode is narrating individual points instead of describing the overall pattern. We will build one complete sentence from four parts: direction, form, strength, and unusual features.

Scatterplots · 1 of 3

Start with the four-part description

1
Direction
Positive, negative, or neither: what happens to y as x increases?
2
Form
Roughly linear, curved, or another visible shape?
3
Strength
How tightly does the cloud follow its form: weak, moderate, or strong?
4
Unusual features
Outlier, cluster, gap, or influential-looking point?

Do not say “strong” until you name the form it is strong around.

Model the sentence stem: “There is a [direction], [form], [strength] association between x and y, with [unusual feature if present].” Students frequently use “strong” as a synonym for “positive”; keep redirecting them to strength as closeness to a form. Use “association” until a design supports causal language.

Scatterplots · 2 of 3

Every point still has a story

Axes

Name the variables and units. The explanatory variable belongs on the horizontal axis; the response variable belongs on the vertical axis.

Context

Describe the association in the situation: “As study time increases, predicted score tends to increase.”

+
Direction is not causation
A positive association is a pattern, not proof that x causes y.
!
Unusual means investigate
Ask whether the point is a recording error, a different group, or a real observation.
This slide prevents two common errors: treating the axes as decoration and turning association into causation. Require students to say what an increase means in context. For an unusual point, they do not need to delete it; they need to notice it and ask what role it may play.

Activity · Turn & Talk · 8 minutes

Describe before you calculate

A school surveys 18 students about weekly study time and weekly sleep. A scatterplot is projected without a trendline.

With your partner
  1. Write one complete four-part description.
  2. Choose one point you would investigate and explain why.
  3. State which variable is explanatory and which is response.

No calculator yet. Your first sentence should come from the cloud.

Do not provide a verbal “correct” description before pairs commit. Listen for “study time causes sleep” and for students who call a cloud “strong” without saying linear. Cold-call for the sentence stem, then ask the class what additional context would be needed before making a causal claim.

Act II · The Number

Correlation r

One number summarizes direction and strength for a linear association.

Correlation is a summary, not a replacement for the graph. Students need to see why the plot remains essential: r cannot name an outlier, identify a cluster, or supply context. Keep the graph visible whenever technology reports r.

Correlation · 1 of 3

What r can — and cannot — say

−1
Range
−1 ≤ r ≤ 1. The sign gives direction.
0
Near zero
Little linear association. It does not mean no relationship of any kind.
+1
Strength
The closer |r| is to 1, the stronger the linear association.
U
Units
Correlation has no units; changing units does not change r.
<>
Symmetric
Correlation of x with y equals correlation of y with x.
*
Sensitive
An outlier can change r substantially, so inspect the plot.
The “near zero” tile is the key misconception check: r near zero means little linear association, not no association. Emphasize that r has no units and is symmetric, while regression will depend on which variable is explanatory. The range inequality is exact; do not use an approximation sign there.

Correlation · 2 of 3

Say the number in context

Sentence frame

“There is a strong/moderate/weak positive/negative linear association between x and y; r = ... summarizes its direction and strength.”

  • Do connect sign to direction.
  • Do use the variables' names.
  • Do not call r a slope.
  • Do not infer causation from r.

A statistic is not a conclusion until the variables come back into the sentence.

Students often write “r = 0.82, so it is strong” and stop. Require direction, strength, and context. Also separate r from slope: r is unitless and symmetric; the slope has units and changes when x and y are swapped. This language will transfer directly into FRQ responses in the new 10-point format.

Worked Example · KDEA

Study time and quiz score

For 18 students, let x be weekly study hours and y be the quiz score out of 20. Technology reports the correlation.

StepWhat you write
KPaired quantitative data for 18 students; x = study hours; y = quiz score. Calculator output: r ≈ 0.84.
DDescribe the direction and strength of the linear association in context.
Er = [Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ)] / [(n − 1)sxsy]; use technology for the arithmetic.
Ar ≈ 0.84, so there is a strong positive linear association: students who study more hours per week tend to have higher quiz scores.

The conclusion names the people, the variables, the direction, and the strength.

This is a model of the AP Statistics mapping: K is data/design/conditions, D is the claim sought, E is the statistic or formula, and A is both computation and a contextual conclusion. Point out that the formula is not the point of hand arithmetic here; StatKey, R, and Python can calculate r, but students still own the interpretation. The rounded output uses ≈.

Activity · Gallery Walk · 10 minutes

Compare three projected scatterplots with the same reported r. The axes and contexts differ.

A correlation is evidence about a pattern, not the whole investigation.

Use this as a quick visual check, not a worksheet with a hidden answer key. The goal is to make students uncomfortable with treating equal r values as equal stories. If one plot contains a visible unusual feature, ask what would happen to r if that point were removed, without requiring a recalculation.

Act III · The Line

From pattern to prediction

A model compresses a cloud into a rule we can interpret and use carefully.

Transition from “what is happening?” to “what value would the model predict?” Make clear that a line is a useful summary of a linear pattern, not a claim that every point lies on it. Residuals and least squares are next week, so this deck stops after model interpretation, prediction, and extrapolation.

Linear Models · 1 of 4

The linear regression model

Notation

ŷ = a + bx

ŷ is the predicted response; a is the intercept; b is the slope.

  • Observed response: y
  • Predicted response: ŷ
  • Input: explanatory variable x
  • Output: model's estimate on the response scale

The hat matters: prediction is not observation.

Insist that students read y-hat aloud as “predicted y,” not just “y.” The distinction matters for later residual work: an observed value and a model prediction are different quantities. Keep the equation exact; coefficients reported by technology will be rounded.

Linear Models · 2 of 4

Translate coefficients into the situation

CoefficientInterpretation templateUnit check
Slope bFor each increase of 1 [x unit], predicted y changes by b [y units], on average.y units / x units
Intercept aWhen x = 0, the model predicts a [y units], if x = 0 is meaningful.y units
Language guardrail

“Predicted” and “on average” belong in the interpretation. A regression line does not make every individual prediction exact.

The most common slope error is dropping “predicted” and “on average.” The most common intercept error is interpreting x = 0 when zero is outside the data's meaningful context. Ask students to include units in both interpretations; this is the statistics version of units traveling with the math.

Worked Example · KDEA

Predicting quiz score from study time

A regression output for the study-time data gives ŷ = 11.2 + 1.35x, where x is weekly study hours.

StepWhat you write
Kŷ = 11.2 + 1.35x; x = weekly study hours; y = quiz score out of 20.
DInterpret slope and intercept; predict the score for x = 4 hours.
Eŷ = a + bx; slope and intercept templates; substitute the given x.
ASlope: each additional hour is associated with 1.35 predicted points. Intercept: at 0 hours, predicted score is 11.2 points, if meaningful. For 4 hours, ŷ = 11.2 + 1.35(4) = 16.6 points.

Compute, then say what the prediction means in the data's context.

This is the core modeling example. The intercept is mathematically available but may be less useful if zero study hours is not represented or meaningful; students should qualify it. The prediction arithmetic is exact for the displayed model and x-value, while the coefficients themselves are presented as an output model. Ask: “Is 4 hours within the observed study-time range?” before endorsing the prediction.

Linear Models · 3 of 4

Prediction is conditional on the data

OK
Interpolation
Predicting for an x-value inside the observed range. The model is speaking where it has data.
!
Extrapolation
Predicting beyond the observed range. The line may not remain reasonable there.
ŷ
Predicted is not observed
A prediction is an estimate on the response scale, not a guaranteed individual outcome.
<>
Direction matters
Switching explanatory and response variables changes the regression problem.
Students tend to treat extrapolation as a numerical issue instead of a meaning issue. The arithmetic still works; the concern is whether the pattern is justified beyond the data. Contrast this with correlation's symmetry: regression is directional because the model predicts a response from an explanatory variable.

Activity · Table Talk · 12 minutes

Model or warning?

For each proposed prediction, decide whether you would report it, qualify it, or refuse to use it without more information.

A
Inside the range
Predict for an observed type of case, using the model's units and context.
B
Outside the range
Predict far beyond the observed x-values, with no evidence the pattern continues.
C
Causal wording
Write “an extra hour causes 1.35 points” from an observational study.

Your justification is the answer: range, context, and design all matter.

This is deliberately a judgment task, not an answer-key slide. Have each table defend one choice using the words interpolation, extrapolation, association, or prediction. Listen for students who think a high r automatically licenses causal wording; return to how the data were collected.

Synthesis

Picture → number → model

Keep the context attached at every step.

This divider is the bridge to the recap. Ask students to name what gets added at each stage: the graph reveals structure, r summarizes linear direction and strength, and the model supplies interpretable predictions. The context never disappears when the technology output appears.
