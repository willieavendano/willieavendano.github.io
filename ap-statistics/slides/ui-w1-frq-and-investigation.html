Where We Are

Units 1–2 are done. Now we use them.

The December Interlude has two jobs: practice communicating statistical reasoning and begin a small investigation that makes design choices visible.

  • FRQs — write the reasoning, not just the number
  • Design — ask a question that data can answer
  • Collection — sample or assign treatments deliberately
  • Reflection — name bias, limits, and what the data can support

A small investigation now becomes a rehearsal for the capstone later.

Frame the week as transfer, not extra content. Students already know one-variable summaries, collecting data, probability language, and distributions. The work now is choosing an appropriate design and communicating a defensible claim. Keep the phrase "what the data can support" in circulation; overclaiming is the most common failure mode in both FRQs and projects.

Act I · FRQ Practice

Make the reasoning visible.

The new 10-point format rewards a complete statistical argument.

Use this divider to reset the room. Students often think an FRQ is a calculation with a few words around it. The emphasis this week is the reverse: the calculation is evidence inside an argument.

FRQ Format · 1 of 3

What earns credit in the new 10-point format

1
Reason in context
Name the population, variable, units, or real-world situation. A correct number without its meaning is incomplete.
2
Show the statistical move
State the method, formula, condition, or design choice that connects the evidence to the claim.
3
Conclude completely
Answer the question asked, in context, and include the direction or comparison when the prompt requires it.
4
Respect the evidence
Use cautious language when the design is observational, and do not generalize beyond the population or treatment structure.
Do not present this as a secret rubric or a point-by-point prediction. It is a writing discipline. Ask students to underline the sentence that answers the question and circle the words that identify context. A frequent error is a conclusion that merely repeats a statistic: "the mean is 14.2" is not a conclusion about the situation.

FRQ Format · 2 of 3

KDEA for statistics: the answer needs a home

StepStatistics mappingWhat belongs on the page
KData, design, conditionsWhat was measured? Who or what was studied? What conditions or assumptions matter?
DParameter, probability, or claim soughtState the target with its context: a proportion, a mean, a probability, or a comparison.
EProcedure or formulaName the appropriate summary, model, simulation, or design principle and why it fits.
ACompute and conclude in contextShow the result, then answer the original question with a complete, defensible sentence.

If the reader cannot tell what your number means, the A step is not finished.

This is the statistics version of the house KDEA protocol. Keep the letter order unchanged. For students who jump straight to a calculator, ask: "Where is your D? What exactly are you trying to learn?" For students who write a method name with no reason, ask: "Why does that method fit this design and variable?"

FRQ Format · 3 of 3

A complete conclusion has three parts

What
Answer the question
State the statistic, probability, comparison, or decision the prompt asked for.
Who
Name the context
Identify the population, sample, group, or setting. Keep the noun from the prompt alive.
So what
Interpret the evidence
Explain what the result suggests, including direction and limits. Use association or causation carefully.
Sentence frame

"In this study, [what] for [who], so [so what]."

Have students read the sentence frame aloud once, then remove it mentally. The frame is not a substitute for thinking; it is a check that the final sentence contains all three jobs. Watch for the common mistake of using "causes" after a random sample with no treatment assignment.

Practice · Read First

One prompt, before any arithmetic

FRQ practice

A school surveys a random sample of 120 students about the number of hours they sleep on a typical school night. In the sample, 78 students report at least 8 hours.

  1. Identify the population and the variable of interest.
  2. Calculate the sample proportion of students reporting at least 8 hours.
  3. Write one complete conclusion in context.

Do K and D before touching the calculator.

Give students 90 seconds to annotate only. The point is to separate reading from computing. Ask them to mark the population, the measured variable, the sample size, and the target. Do not solicit a final answer yet; the next slide models the map, not a hidden answer key.

Practice · KDEA Map

Map the response before you write it

StepPrompt details to captureWriter's checkpoint
KRandom sample of 120 students; 78 report at least 8 hours; categorical responseWhat was measured, and how were students selected?
DSample proportion p̂ of students reporting at least 8 hoursCan I name the target without saying only "find p"?
Ep̂ = x/n for the observed sampleDoes this formula match a count divided by a sample size?
ACompute, then state what that proportion says about the sampled students and the population the random sample representsDid I answer in a complete sentence, not just report a decimal?

The last sentence is part of the answer, not decoration after it.

Keep the decimal out of the spotlight for now. The transferable move is identifying what the proportion refers to. If you want students to calculate, let them do so on paper and compare methods, but do not turn this into a displayed answer key. Ask pairs to critique whether a conclusion names both the sample and the population.

FRQ Practice · Turn & Talk · 8 minutes

Audit the conclusion, not the handwriting

Partner A reads
  • Underline the answer to the question.
  • Circle the context: who, what, and setting.
  • Put a question mark beside any overclaim.
Partner B asks
  • What does the number mean here?
  • What population can this design represent?
  • What would make the sentence complete?

Revision target: one sentence that a non-statistician could understand.

This is a fast formative check. The teacher should listen for students treating "random sample" as a magic word that permits every claim. It supports generalization to the population sampled, but it does not establish causation. Ask pairs to revise one conclusion aloud rather than correcting every line.

Act II · Investigation Launch

Ask a question worth collecting data for.

Design first. Data second. Claims last.

The investigation is a two-week mini-project, not a full capstone. Today students pose an investigative question and design collection. Next week is analysis and presentations. Keep the scope narrow enough that the design, not the logistics, is the intellectual center.

The Mini-Project

Two weeks, one defensible question

W1
Design + collection
Pose a question, define the target group and variables, choose a design, and collect data ethically.
W2
Analysis + presentation
Summarize the data, explain what the design permits you to say, and present evidence with limits.
U1
Design principles
Sampling, bias, experiments versus observational studies, and the role of random assignment.
Capstone
Preview at small scale
This is the same question-design-collection-analysis-presentation arc used again at year end.
State the deadlines verbally: design and collection this week; analysis and presentations next week. Students do not need a polished research paper. They need a clear question, a transparent collection plan, usable data, and a claim matched to the design.

Question Design · 1 of 2

A good investigative question names the comparison

Too broad

"Are students healthy?"

Undefined population, variable, and standard.

Ready to design

"Among students in our class, is average sleep different on nights before school than on weekend nights?"

Population, quantitative variable, and comparison are visible.

Question test: who, what variable, and what comparison or relationship?

Invite students to use familiar school-life variables only if they can measure them respectfully and consistently. A question can be descriptive, comparative, or relational. Reject questions that require private health, identifying, or unsafe data. The second example is a paired comparison structure; students may choose a simpler design if the measurement burden is too high.

Question Design · 2 of 2

Define variables before you collect them

Who
Target group
Which students, days, objects, or trials does the question concern?
What
Operational definition
Exactly how will the variable be measured, recorded, and coded?
Care
Ethical boundary
Voluntary participation, no names, no sensitive information, and the right to skip.
Before collection

Write the question and the measurement rule so another group could repeat the plan without asking you what you meant.

This is where vague questions become measurable. Ask, "If two people collect this tomorrow, would they code the same response the same way?" Require an anonymous identifier or no identifier at all. Do not allow students to collect names, grades, medical information, or other sensitive responses for this classroom project.

Design Principles · 1 of 3

Sampling answers: who can your data speak for?

ChoiceWhat it doesRisk to name
Convenience sampleUse the people easiest to reachUndercoverage and voluntary-response bias
Random sampleGive each member of the target population a known chance to be selectedNonresponse can still distort the results
Stratified sampleSeparate the population into meaningful groups, then sample within eachStrata must be defined before selection
CensusAttempt to measure every member of the target populationNonresponse and measurement problems do not disappear

A sample can be large and still be biased.

Keep the emphasis on the target population. Students often say "random" when they mean "we asked whoever was nearby." Ask each group to write one sentence describing who was excluded by its plan. That sentence often reveals the actual bias more clearly than a list of vocabulary words.

Design Principles · 2 of 3

Experiment or observational study?

Observe
Observational study
Measure variables as they occur. You can describe association, but confounding may explain a relationship.
Assign
Experiment
Impose treatments and use random assignment when possible. This creates the strongest path to a causal claim.
Decision question

Are you merely recording a condition, or are you deliberately assigning a treatment?

"Students who choose music while studying" is observational. "Randomly assign a playlist, then compare quiz scores" is an experiment.

Association is not causation unless the design earns the claim.

This distinction should determine the language of every final presentation. Students may not randomly assign anything that could harm or disadvantage a participant. For a classroom mini-project, observational questions are often the safest choice; the limitation is not a flaw if the group states it honestly.

Design Principles · 3 of 3

Predict the bias before it happens

Who
Undercoverage
Some members of the target group have no realistic chance to appear in the sample.
How
Response bias
The wording, setting, or interviewer changes what participants are willing to report.
Missing
Nonresponse
Selected participants do not provide data, and those who are missing differ from those who respond.
Hidden
Confounding
A third variable is mixed with the explanatory variable, making a relationship hard to interpret.

A strong design does not pretend bias is impossible; it makes the risk visible.

Ask groups to predict one plausible source of bias before collecting. This is more useful than asking them to label bias after the fact. A group that cannot name a risk probably has not specified its population or collection procedure precisely enough.

Design Workshop · 35 minutes

Build the collection plan

Group protocol
  1. 5 min: Draft two questions; select the one with a measurable variable and a clear target group.
  2. 8 min: Write the operational definitions and the exact sampling or assignment procedure.
  3. 8 min: Identify one likely bias and one ethical safeguard.
  4. 10 min: Pilot the procedure with one or two cases; revise confusing wording or coding.
  5. 4 min: Submit the design card: question, variables, design, sample plan, bias risk, and next step.

A plan is ready when another group could run it without improvising.

Protect the pilot time. It is the fastest way to expose ambiguous wording, categories that do not cover all responses, and procedures that take too long. Circulate with three questions: "Who is your target population?" "What exactly will you record?" and "What claim would your design allow next week?" Collect design cards before students leave so the teacher can catch unsafe or unworkable plans overnight.

Collection Checklist

Before the first response

  • Question is specific and answerable
  • Target population is named
  • Variables have operational definitions
  • Sampling or assignment steps are written in order
  • Participation is voluntary
  • No names or sensitive data are collected
  • Likely bias is predicted
  • Each response can be coded consistently

If the plan changes during collection, record the change.

This slide is a handoff from design to collection. Stress that an unplanned change is not automatically a disaster; hiding it is. Students should record the change and explain how it might affect the data. That habit becomes essential in the year-end capstone.

Recap

Two habits to carry forward

FRQ habit
  • KDEA: data/design/conditions → target → method → compute and conclude in context.
  • A complete conclusion answers what, who, and so what.
Investigation habit
  • Design determines what your data can support.
  • Sampling, bias, and assignment belong in the plan before collection begins.
Keep recap and next steps separate in your delivery. The recap is the durable content students should be able to screenshot. Do not let logistics replace the two central ideas: context completes an FRQ, and design limits a claim.
