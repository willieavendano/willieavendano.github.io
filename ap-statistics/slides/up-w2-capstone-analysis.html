Analysis & Write-Up

Week 2

Choose the method · check conditions · conclude in context

This is the second and final working week of the capstone. Last week groups created a question, designed a study, and collected data. This week is not a tour of every AP procedure; it is a disciplined decision-and-communication cycle. The Contract · Calendar Wk 36 · May 24

Turn collected data into a defensible claim.

Each group will choose and execute the analysis that matches its question, then write a report another reader could audit without asking the group to explain every missing step.

  • Describe the data that were actually collected
  • Analyze with descriptive statistics, inference, or regression as appropriate
  • Check conditions before trusting the output
  • Conclude in the study's real context

Today: method choice. This week: report draft.

Make the deliverable concrete: a finished analysis is not a screenshot from StatKey, R, or Python. It is a chain from question to variables to method to evidence to conclusion. Groups can use StatKey, R, or Python, but the report must make the statistical reasoning visible in plain language.

Act I

Choose the analysis.

The question decides the method — not the software menu.

Use this divider to interrupt the common workflow of opening software first and choosing the most familiar button. The response variable, explanatory variable, design, and claim determine the method. Software performs arithmetic; it does not choose the scientific question for students.

Question → Method

Start with the shape of the question.

1
One variable
What does this sample look like? Use graphs, center, spread, and unusual features to describe the distribution.
2
Compare groups
Do two groups differ? Describe side-by-side distributions, then consider an interval or test for a difference.
3
Association
How are two quantitative variables related? Use a scatterplot, correlation, and a fitted regression model when appropriate.
4
Proportion or claim
Is a categorical outcome common, or does a sample support a claim about a population? Match the parameter to the procedure.
The tiles are a first-pass map, not a substitute for reading the design. A group comparing two variables may still need only description if it did not plan an inferential claim. A regression line can summarize association without proving causation. Ask groups to point to the exact sentence in their question that selects a tile.

Method Check · 5 minutes

Before you calculate, name four things.

Write this at the top of the group's draft
  1. Response: What outcome is measured, and is it quantitative or categorical?
  2. Explanatory variable: Is there a comparison group, a predictor, or neither?
  3. Design: How were observations selected or assigned?
  4. Claim: Is the goal description, estimation, a test of evidence, or prediction?

If the claim is vague, the method cannot be precise.

Circulate and listen for labels without definitions: "our response is study habits" is not enough. Students should identify the variable and its measurement. Also force the distinction between a population parameter and a sample statistic. This short check prevents several pages of analysis that answers a different question than the one the group actually asked.

Worked Mapping · Descriptive Statistics

Question: what does our sample look like?

StepWhat the group writes
KVariable type, sample size n, units, and the observed distribution: shape, center, spread, unusual features.
DA description of the sample, not a population claim; identify which statistics best represent the shape.
EGraph plus numerical summary: dotplot or histogram; median and IQR for skew/outliers, or mean and standard deviation for a roughly symmetric distribution.
AReport the statistic with units, explain the pattern, and connect it to the group's setting. Do not write a method name as a conclusion.

A graph is evidence; the sentence beside it explains what the evidence says.

Worked Mapping · Interval

Question: what values are plausible for the population?

StepWhat the group writes
KSample statistic, target parameter, design, sample size, and conditions for the chosen interval.
DThe parameter in context: for example, the population proportion p or population mean μ.
EThe matching confidence interval procedure and confidence level; state why its conditions are reasonable.
ACompute the interval, then interpret it as a range of plausible parameter values in the population context.

A 95% interval is about the method's long-run capture rate — not a 95% probability about this fixed parameter.

Students often state the confidence level as if it modifies the parameter: "there is a 95% chance the mean is in the interval." Redirect them to the repeated-sampling interpretation and then require the second half of the sentence: plausible values for which population, measured in what units?

Worked Mapping · Test

Question: is the observed pattern stronger than chance?

StepWhat the group writes
KObserved statistic, null value, design, and the conditions that support the null model.
DThe claim sought: a population proportion, mean, difference, or association, stated in the group's context.
ENull and alternative hypotheses, test statistic or simulation model, and significance level α if used.
AUse the p-value as evidence against the null model; make a decision in context without saying the null is proven true.

A small p-value measures incompatibility with the null model, not the size or importance of an effect.

Keep the language precise and humane. "Reject" is a decision about the null model, not a verdict on students, people, or a group. If a group's design is observational, the test can speak to evidence of association under its assumptions, but it cannot turn the study into an experiment.

Worked Mapping · Regression

Question: how do two quantitative variables move together?

StepWhat the group writes
KExplanatory variable x, response variable y, scatterplot, units, and the observed direction, form, and strength.
DAssociation or prediction for a specified x; distinguish a typical predicted response from a causal effect.
ECorrelation r, least-squares line ŷ = a + bx, residual plot, and a check for influential points.
AInterpret slope and predictions with units and context; report residual evidence and avoid extrapolation beyond the data.

Regression explains a pattern in the observed data; it does not manufacture an experiment.

Conditions · Non-negotiable

Conditions are part of the analysis, not a footnote.

S
Sampling / assignment
Explain how observations entered the study. Random sampling supports generalization; random assignment supports causal language.
I
Independence
Check whether observations can reasonably be treated as independent; account for paired or clustered data.
N
Normal / large counts
Use the shape or success-failure counts required by the procedure. Do not call a small sample normal by habit.
L
Linear / residuals
For regression, inspect the scatterplot and residual plot: linear pattern, roughly constant spread, and no influential point driving the story.
Have students annotate the evidence for each condition directly on their report outline. "The condition is met" is not a check; "the residual plot shows no curved pattern and the spread stays roughly constant" is. If a condition fails, students should change the claim, change the method, or clearly label the limitation rather than quietly continuing.

Workshop Flow · 20 minutes

Run the analysis in a visible order.

Pass 1 · Understand
  1. Clean labels, units, missing values, and coding.
  2. Graph before calculating.
  3. Record the sample size and unusual observations.
Pass 2 · Defend
  1. Run the matching procedure in StatKey, R, or Python.
  2. Check conditions and sensitivity to unusual points.
  3. Draft the conclusion before formatting the report.

Save the graph, output, and decision trail. A reader should be able to reproduce the story.

The Report · 1 of 3

What a complete write-up contains

Q
Question & design
Research question, population or setting, variables, operational definitions, and how data were collected.
D
Data description
Sample size, graph or table, center and spread, units, missingness, and notable features.
M
Method
Parameter or claim, procedure, hypotheses or confidence level, conditions, and software evidence.
C
Conclusion
Statistical evidence translated into a sentence about the original question and its context.
L
Limitations
Sampling, response, measurement, confounding, scope, unusual observations, and what the study cannot establish.
R
Reproducibility
Data source, code or calculator settings, labeled output, and enough detail for another reader to follow.
This is the required checklist slide. Tell students that a report can be visually beautiful and still incomplete if it omits the design or limitations. Conversely, a modest report with labeled evidence, accurate conditions, and a contextual conclusion is statistically strong. Ask groups to mark each tile as drafted, missing, or needs revision.

The Report · 2 of 3

Write in the order a reader needs.

SectionReader's question
IntroductionWhat question is this study answering, and why does it matter?
Data & designWho or what was observed, how, and with which variables?
AnalysisWhat pattern or inferential procedure was used, and why is it appropriate?
Conditions & limitationsWhat must be true for the method to be trusted, and what remains uncertain?
ConclusionWhat does the evidence say about the original question, in context?

Every figure gets a sentence. Every number gets a meaning.

The Report · 3 of 3

Make the last sentence do statistical work.

Weak

“The p-value is 0.03, so we reject the null.”

Procedure language only. No population, variables, direction, or evidence claim.

Stronger frame

“In this study of [population / setting], the data provide [strength of evidence] that [contextual claim].”

Add the estimate, interval, or observed pattern when it helps a reader judge size.

Evidence + direction or size + population/setting + limitation.

Do not give students a sentence to copy as a ritual. Use the frame to expose the ingredients they must supply. A conclusion should answer the original question, not merely repeat the output. If the study is observational, explicitly use association language and name the main limitation.

Common Analysis Pitfalls

The three ways a correct calculator output goes wrong.

!
Conditions unchecked
A p-value or interval is reported without evidence that the procedure's sampling, independence, counts, shape, or residual conditions are reasonable.
!
Causation overclaimed
An observational association is rewritten as “X causes Y.” Without random assignment, confounding remains a live alternative explanation.
!
Context disappears
The conclusion says “reject,” “significant,” or “the slope is 2.4” without naming the variables, population, units, or practical meaning.
Treat these as revision flags, not gotcha mistakes. Ask groups to highlight the exact sentence where each problem appears. Then have them repair the sentence or add the missing evidence. The goal is to make statistical writing precise without turning it into a vocabulary performance.

Peer Review · 12 minutes

Audit another group's conclusion.

Reader test
  1. Underline the parameter, statistic, or model the group used.
  2. Circle the condition evidence. If you cannot find it, write “missing.”
  3. Box the context: variables, units, population or setting.
  4. Draw a line through any causal verb the design cannot support.
  5. Write one question a skeptical reader would still ask.

Revise for a reader who was not in your group.

Pair groups with different methods when possible so the review is about communication rather than shared button-clicks. The reviewer is not grading the partner's arithmetic; the reviewer is testing whether the reasoning is visible and the claim stays within the design. Require one specific praise and one specific revision request before groups switch back.

Revision Decisions

If a condition fails, the analysis still has choices.

1
Change the method
Use a procedure or summary that fits the data structure and stated question better.
2
Narrow the claim
Describe only the observed sample or association; stop short of generalization or causation.
3
Name the limitation
Keep the analysis, but state exactly which condition is doubtful and how it affects interpretation.

A limitation named clearly is stronger than an assumption hidden.

Recap

Capstone analysis in five moves

Screenshot this
  1. Question — identify variables, design, and the claim.
  2. Describe — graph first; summarize shape, center, spread, and unusual features.
  3. Choose — match parameter and procedure to the question.
  4. Check — show the conditions and what the design permits you to say.
  5. Conclude — evidence, context, size or direction, and limits in one honest story.

No output is self-explanatory.

Before Presentations

Finish the report before you polish the slides.

  • One sentence that answers the original question
  • One labeled graph or table that supports the story
  • One visible condition check
  • One limitation the group can defend
  • One reproducible record of software or calculations
  • Every claim uses the right verbs: describe, estimate, associate, not overclaim

The presentation is a clear window into the report — not a replacement for it.

This is the production checkpoint for the rest of the week. Ask groups to submit or show the report draft before spending time on animation or design. The presentation should compress the reasoning, not hide it. If a group cannot explain its condition check aloud, the report is not ready.
