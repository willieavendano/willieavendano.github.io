AP STATISTICS · DATA INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Presentations

Week 2

Calendar week 15 · Dec 7 · Units 1–2 in action

This is the second and final week of the small data investigation. Students already designed a question and collected data last week. Today the emphasis is not on finding a flashy result; it is on making a defensible statement from the data they actually collected. Keep the scope narrow: Unit 1 descriptions, summaries, comparisons, and simple Unit 2 probability reasoning only.

Where We Are

The investigation has reached the evidence stage.

Last week you asked a question, chose a design, and collected data. This week you turn those observations into an answer that is:

  • descriptive — what the sample shows
  • comparative — how groups or conditions differ
  • probabilistic — what chance reasoning adds
  • contextual — what the result means for the original question

Question → data → evidence → conclusion

Students often jump straight from a calculator output to a claim. Slow down the chain and name the four checkpoints. A summary is not a conclusion until it is tied back to the investigative question and the design. If the sample cannot support a broad claim, the honest conclusion must say so.

Act I · From Rows to Evidence

Make the data speak clearly.

Good analysis is organized, visual, numerical, and honest about what the design can support.

Use this divider to establish the standard for the week. Students are not being graded on a surprising result. They are being graded on whether a reader can follow the path from question to evidence to claim.

Before You Calculate

Audit the data before trusting the output.

Record
  • What is one observational unit?
  • Which variable is categorical? Which is quantitative?
  • Are the units and labels consistent?
  • How many observations are usable?
Notice
  • missing or duplicated rows
  • values outside the planned scale
  • unusual observations worth checking
  • design choices that limit the conclusion

A spreadsheet can calculate a mistake very efficiently.

Make every group show this audit somewhere in its working notes. The most common failure is analyzing a column without being able to define what one row represents. A second failure is deleting an unusual value because it makes the graph look inconvenient. Ask what happened first; do not remove it silently.

Unit 1 Tools · 1 of 2

Choose summaries that match the variable.

1
One quantitative variable
Dotplot, histogram, or boxplot; describe shape, center, spread, and unusual features. Use mean and standard deviation when the shape supports them; use median and IQR when resistant summaries fit better.
2
One categorical variable
Frequency table or bar graph; report counts and proportions. Define the denominator before saying “percent.”
3
Two quantitative variables
Scatterplot; describe direction, form, strength, and unusual features. Correlation describes linear association, not causation.
4
Two groups or conditions
Use side-by-side graphs and compare the same features: center, spread, shape, and overlap.
This is a selection menu, not a command to use every tool. Students should choose the smallest set of displays and statistics that answers their question. Correctly matching the variable types matters more than producing a crowded report.

When two groups are part of the question, compare the same features in the same units:

“Higher” is incomplete. Higher by how much, with what spread, and in which context?

KDEA · Statistics Mapping

K · D · E · A

Know the design. Define the claim. Explain the tool. Analyze and conclude in context.

Statistics uses the same protocol students already know, but the last step is not just arithmetic. The conclusion must be written in context, and the design determines how cautious that conclusion must be.

KDEA · 1 of 2

Map the investigation before choosing a formula.

StepStatistics versionQuestion to write down
KData, design, and conditionsWhat was measured, on whom, and how?
DParameter, probability, or claim soughtWhat exact statement would answer the question?
EGraph, summary, comparison, or probability ruleWhich Unit 1 or simple Unit 2 tool matches the variables?
ACompute, interpret, and state limitsWhat does the result say in context, and what can it not say?

In statistics, context is part of the algebra.

Explicitly connect this table to the house KDEA language. “Parameter” here means the population number or feature the investigation is trying to learn about; if the project is only descriptive, the D step can be a sample summary or comparison instead. Do not force formal inference language onto this interlude.

KDEA · Worked Investigation

Worked example: compare two conditions.

Suppose a class investigates: Do students report different commute times on days with and without rain?

Set up before the calculator
  • K: commute time in minutes; rain status; number of observations; how days were selected
  • D: compare the typical commute time for rainy and non-rainy days
  • E: side-by-side displays plus a difference in centers; check spread and overlap
  • A: describe the observed difference, then connect it to the commute question and design

The procedure is chosen by the question and variables, not by the biggest button in the software.

KDEA · Worked Investigation

Use one table to keep the reasoning visible.

StepWhat belongs in the project notes
KQuantitative response: commute time (minutes). Categorical explanatory variable: rain / no rain. State the observational units and selection process.
DDifference in typical commute time: rainy days compared with non-rainy days.
ESide-by-side dotplots or boxplots; report the same center and spread summaries for both groups; calculate the center difference in minutes.
ADescribe the direction and size of the observed difference, discuss overlap or unusual values, answer the commute question, and state the design limitation.

A complete A step has both evidence and a sentence a reader can understand.

Keep this example worked at the level of reasoning, not arithmetic. There are deliberately no project data values to reveal and no answer key. Ask groups to replace each generic phrase with their own variables, comparison, and limitation. “We calculated a difference” is not a conclusion; the direction, units, context, and scope must all appear.

Unit 2 Tools

Probability can sharpen the story without replacing the data.

S
Sample space
Name the possible outcomes or observations before counting an event.
E
Event
Define exactly what counts: “at least 3 successes,” “a commute over 30 minutes,” or another stated condition.
P
Probability
Use a proportion, a complement, or a multiplication rule when the situation supports it.
C
Context
Translate the probability into a long-run or observed-frequency statement. Do not confuse probability with certainty.
This is a light Unit 2 bridge. Keep students inside familiar reasoning: define an event, identify the denominator, and interpret a probability as a proportion or long-run fraction. Do not introduce formal distributions or removed topics here; the investigation should remain the center of gravity.

Unit 2 Tools · Quick Check

Probability language must show its denominator.

Observed proportion

In the collected data, 18 of 60 observations meet the event. The observed proportion is 18/60 = 0.30.

Say: “In this sample, 30% met the event.”

Complement

If P(E) = 0.30, then P(not E) = 1 − 0.30 = 0.70.

Say: “The chance of not meeting the event is 70%,” only when the probability model is appropriate.

Count first. Divide by the correct total. Interpret the result in the setting.

Presentation Expectations

What a complete statistical conclusion sounds like

Sentence frame

“For [the observational units or setting], the data show [direction and size of the pattern] in [units or proportion]. This answers our question by [interpretation]. Because we used [design / sample], we should not claim [specific limit].”

Evidence
Name the display, statistic, or probability that supports the claim.
Context
Use the variables and setting from the investigative question.
Scope
Separate what the data show from what the design allows you to generalize.
Read the frame once, then ask students to improve it rather than copy it mechanically. The common failure is a conclusion that says “there is a correlation” or “Group A is better” with no variables, units, or population. The final sentence should make the design limitation concrete, not just say “more research is needed.”

Presentation Expectations · 1 of 2

A short presentation still needs a complete arc.

01
Question + design
State the question, observational units, variables, and how data were collected.
02
Data display
Use readable labels, units, and a display matched to the variable type.
03
Evidence
Give the key summary, comparison, or simple probability calculation. Explain what the audience should notice.
04
Conclusion + limit
Answer the original question in context, then name one honest limitation or next step.

If the audience cannot repeat your question, the presentation has not started yet.

Presentation Expectations · 2 of 2

Make the evidence easy to see and hard to misread.

  • One main graph or table per idea
  • Axes, units, categories, and denominators are visible
  • Numbers are rounded consistently with ≈ when needed
  • Say “association” unless the design supports a causal claim
  • Do not hide unusual observations without explanation
  • End with the answer, not with “any questions?”
Audience test

Can a classmate identify the question, the evidence, the conclusion, and the limitation in one minute?

Respect the clock: concise evidence makes more room for better questions.

Adjust the number of rounds to the class size, but preserve the three non-negotiables: every group presents, every audience member records evidence, and every student writes an individual conclusion. If technology fails, groups should be able to present from a printed graph or a shared image; the statistical reasoning is the assessment, not the slide software.

Rehearsal · 12 minutes

One rehearsal, three checks.

Partner feedback
  1. Underline the sentence that answers the investigative question.
  2. Circle the evidence: graph, statistic, comparison, or probability.
  3. Box the limitation: what the design does not allow you to claim.

Then ask the presenter: “What would change your conclusion?”

A strong presentation survives a careful question.

The rehearsal question is intentionally diagnostic. Students who cannot say what would change the conclusion usually have a claim that is too broad or evidence that is not connected to the question. Do not let groups use “more data” as the only response; push them toward a specific change in design, variable, or pattern.

Recap

Screenshot this: the investigation checklist.

Before you present
  • Question is visible and answerable from the collected data.
  • Variables, observational units, labels, and denominators are defined.
  • Displays and summaries match the variable types.
  • Comparisons use a common ruler: center, spread, shape, and unusual features.
  • Probability statements define the event and interpret the denominator.
  • Conclusion answers in context and states the design limit.
This is the last student-facing checklist before the closer. Ask groups to self-score it quickly, but do not turn the score into a second rubric. The point is to catch missing context before the audience has to ask for it.
