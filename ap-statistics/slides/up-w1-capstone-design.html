Capstone: Design the Investigation

Week 1

Question · design · planned analysis · data collection

This is the post-AP-exam launch, not an extension of test prep. Frame the capstone as the December investigation at full scale: students now have the whole year toolkit, including inference and regression. This week is about making a defensible plan before collecting a single observation.

The Capstone

The December investigation, now with the whole toolkit

In December, you designed a small investigation with the early-year tools. Now you will build a complete statistical investigation from a question you genuinely want to answer.

This week: plan first. Data collection begins only after the design is defensible.

Act I · The Question

Good data cannot rescue a vague question.

Start with the claim you want evidence to support.

Slow down here. Students often want to jump to a graph or a sample size because those feel like "doing statistics." The question is the anchor: it determines the variables, design, collection protocol, and eventual inference. Ask students to keep the question visible while they work.

Question Design

Make the question answerable

Strong questions name:

  • a population or setting
  • a measurable response
  • an explanatory variable or comparison
  • the kind of claim the evidence could support

Rewrite questions like:

  • “Does music help?”
  • “What do students think?”
  • “Is sleep important?”

into questions with variables, units, and a defined population.

The common failure mode is a question that is really a topic. Push for an operational definition: what exactly gets measured, in what units, and for whom? A question can be interesting and still be impossible to answer with the proposed data.

Design Workshop · 12 minutes

Question studio: from curiosity to a claim

Solo first, then a three-person critique
  1. Write three possible questions about a real decision, pattern, or comparison.
  2. Underline the response variable. Box the explanatory variable or treatment.
  3. Circle the population you want to describe or compare.
  4. Trade with a partner: identify one ambiguity and one promising feature.

Leave with one draft question and one sentence beginning: “I want evidence about…”

Give students quiet writing time before groups talk; otherwise the first idea in the room becomes the group's idea. During critique, listen for questions that contain an unmeasured word such as “better,” “healthy,” or “effective.” Require a measurable definition before approving the draft.

Question Design · Variables

Name what the data will actually contain

Y
Response variable
The outcome measured on each observational unit. State the scale, units, and how it will be recorded.
X
Explanatory variable
The variable used to explain, predict, compare, or assign. State whether it is categorical or quantitative.
U
Unit of observation
The person, object, day, class, or other case that contributes one row of data.
P
Population
The larger group your claim is about. It must match who or what your design can represent.

If a variable cannot be recorded consistently, it is not ready for collection.

Act II · The Design

Design decides what your data can say.

Sampling supports generalization. Random assignment supports cause-and-effect.

This is the central design distinction for the capstone. Students may use any tool from the year, but the strength of their eventual claim is bounded by the design. Repeat the language exactly: random sampling is about representing a population; random assignment is about creating comparable treatment groups for a causal claim.

Design Choice

First decision: sample or experiment?

S
Sample survey
Observe a sample to estimate a population proportion, mean, or relationship. Randomly select units when generalization matters.
E
Experiment
Impose treatments on units to compare responses. Random assignment makes treatment groups comparable and supports causal reasoning.
O
Observational study
Measure variables without assigning a treatment. Describe association carefully; lurking variables can explain a difference.
D
Design constraint
Ethics, access, time, and privacy are part of the design. A realistic protocol beats an impressive imaginary one.
Do not let students label every comparison an experiment. Ask: “Who assigned the explanatory variable?” If the answer is “nobody,” it is observational. Also check that a proposed experiment is ethical and feasible for high-school researchers; the design should not depend on coercion or sensitive data.

Design Audit

Predict the threats before they happen

ThreatQuestion to askDesign response
UndercoverageWho cannot be selected?Define the sampling frame; explain who is missing.
NonresponseWho might refuse or disappear?Plan follow-up; compare responders and nonresponders when possible.
Response biasCould wording or privacy change answers?Neutral wording, anonymous collection, consistent script.
ConfoundingWhat other variable could explain a difference?Random assignment, blocking, or a narrower claim.

A limitation is not an apology. It is part of the conclusion you will eventually write.

KDEA · Stats Mapping

Plan the investigation with the same protocol

StepWhat you write for a statistics investigation
KData, design, population, variables, units of observation, and conditions you can defend.
DThe parameter, probability, or claim sought: what will the evidence answer, in context?
EThe procedure or formula: sampling method, randomization, interval/test, regression model, or simulation.
ACompute and conclude in context. State what the evidence supports and what it cannot establish.

KDEA is not only for calculations. It is a design-and-reasoning checklist.

This table is the capstone's working protocol. Students should be able to point to each row in their proposal. The A row is intentionally broader than arithmetic: a statistical answer that never returns to the original context is incomplete, even when the calculation is correct.

Design Workshop · 15 minutes

KDEA your proposal before you collect

Partner audit
  1. K: Can your partner identify one row of data and the population?
  2. D: Is the target a parameter, probability, or claim—not just “find a graph”?
  3. E: Does the planned method match the variable types and design?
  4. A: What conclusion will be possible if the result is large, small, or inconclusive?

Partner response: “Your design can support ___, but not ___, because ___.”

The sentence stem keeps peer feedback evidence-based rather than aesthetic. If students say a design “looks good,” ask what claim it licenses. Collect one completed audit from each group as a formative checkpoint before approving data collection.

Planned Analysis

Choose the unit's tool that answers the question

Question shapeTool family to planWhat it can answer
One categorical proportionOne-proportion interval or testEstimate or evaluate a population proportion.
Two categorical groupsTwo-proportion interval or testCompare population proportions, with conditions checked.
One quantitative meanOne-sample t interval or testEstimate or evaluate a population mean.
Two quantitative groupsTwo-sample t interval or testCompare population means when the design supports it.
Two quantitative variablesRegression analysisDescribe association, prediction, and residual evidence; do not turn association into causation.
This is a planning menu, not a command to run every method. Students should name one primary tool and explain why it matches the response variable, design, and target. If the question does not fit a listed method, that is a signal to revise the question or use a simulation-based plan.

Planned Analysis · Conditions

Write the conditions now, not after the result

Before data collection

  • What makes observations independent enough?
  • What randomization or sampling condition matters?
  • What sample size or success-failure condition will you check?

Before the conclusion

  • Which graphical or numerical check will you use?
  • What would make the planned method inappropriate?
  • How will a violation change the claim?

A method is not a button. It is a method plus conditions plus context.

Data Collection

Build a protocol another student could follow

01
Recruit / select
Who is eligible? How is a sample or treatment group chosen? What is the stopping rule?
02
Measure
Use the same wording, instrument, timing, units, and coding for every observational unit.
03
Record
One row per unit. Use clear variable names, a missing-data code, and no names in the analysis file.

Pilot the protocol on two or three units before full collection.

Students often describe a topic and call it a protocol. Ask them to write exact instructions, including what happens when someone skips a question or a measurement fails. A short pilot catches ambiguous wording and reveals whether the planned variables can actually be recorded.

Data Collection · Ethics

Responsible data is part of good statistics

  • Ask only for data you need to answer the question.
  • Tell participants what will happen with their responses.
  • Make participation voluntary and allow a genuine opt-out.
  • Separate identifiers from the analysis data.
  • Do not publish a small group in a way that identifies individuals.
  • Report missingness and exclusions honestly.

If the collection plan would feel unsafe or embarrassing to a participant, redesign it.

Design Workshop · 25 minutes

Work session: leave with a launch-ready plan

The room should get quieter as the plan gets clearer
  1. 5 min: finalize the question and define variables.
  2. 8 min: write the sampling or experimental design and threat audit.
  3. 7 min: name the planned analysis and conditions.
  4. 5 min: write collection instructions and pilot with a partner.

Checkpoint before dismissal: question, design, method, and first collection step are visible.

Conference with groups in this order: question first, then design, then analysis. Students will try to ask whether a graph is “good” before they have decided what the graph is for. Require each group to show the four pieces on one page before they leave; this is the approval artifact for the next meeting.

Recap

Before you collect, you should be able to say:

Q
Question
“I want evidence about ___ in the population ___, measured by ___.”
D
Design
“We will select or assign ___ using ___, so our claim can / cannot ___.”
E
Evidence plan
“The unit's tool that answers this question is ___ because ___.”
A
Action
“Our first collection step is ___, and the pilot will test ___.”

A defensible investigation is a chain: question → design → data → analysis → claim.
